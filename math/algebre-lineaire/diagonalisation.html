Diagonalizability

"Diagonalizable = there is a basis in which the transformation acts separately on each axis."

Intuition

A diagonalizable map has independent privileged directions that stay on themselves. Along each of these directions, the map only stretches, contracts, reverses direction, or annihilates. In the right basis, there is no more mixing between coordinates; each coordinate evolves separately:

$$(x_1, \dots, x_n) \mapsto (\lambda_1 x_1, \dots, \lambda_n x_n).$$

That is why "diagonalizable" almost always means "understandable via a change of basis": in a bad basis the transformation looks complicated; in the right basis, it becomes "axis by axis".

Formal definition

A linear map $T \colon V \to V$ is diagonalizable if there exists a basis of $V$ in which the matrix of $T$ is diagonal. Equivalently, there exists a basis made entirely of eigenvectors of $T$. For matrices, this amounts to saying that there exists an invertible matrix $P$ such that

$$P^{-1} A P = D, \qquad D \text{ diagonal}.$$

Worked example — a real symmetric matrix

Real symmetric matrices are the best starting point: they are orthogonally diagonalizable — one can choose an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, so the change of basis adds no distortion coming from the frame. Take

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Its characteristic polynomial is

$$\chi_A(\lambda) = (2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda - 1),$$

giving two eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$, with eigenvectors $v_1 = (1, 1)$ and $v_2 = (1, -1)$ — which are orthogonal. In the basis $(v_1, v_2)$, the map simply becomes "multiply the first coordinate by 3 and keep the second":

$$P^{-1} A P = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Interactive visualization

Both views show the same operator. On the left, the standard basis: the grid deforms and the transformation seems to mix the coordinates. On the right, the eigenbasis: the action is "axis by axis". Also try the shear — it keeps one real eigendirection but is not diagonalizable.

The counterexample to know — the shear

The shear matrix

$$S = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

deforms the grid, keeps one real eigendirection (the axis $e_1$, with $\lambda = 1$), but still mixes the coordinates: its only eigenline has dimension 1, too small to span the plane. So there is no basis of eigenvectors — $S$ is not diagonalizable. It is the ideal contrast with a genuinely diagonal or symmetric matrix.

Classic pitfalls

Having real eigenvalues is not enough. The shear has a real eigenvalue (a double one!) and yet is not diagonalizable: the eigenspaces must be large enough to rebuild the whole space (the sum of their dimensions must equal $n$).

Diagonalizable does not mean invertible. $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ is diagonal (hence diagonalizable) but not invertible; conversely, the shear is invertible but not diagonalizable. The two notions are independent.

Mini-exercises

Diagonalize $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$.

It is already diagonal! The canonical basis is an eigenbasis: $e_1$ for $\lambda = 3$ (stretch) and $e_2$ for $\lambda = -1$ (reflection). Every diagonal matrix is diagonalizable, with $P = I$.

Why is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ not diagonalizable?

It is a nonzero nilpotent matrix: its only eigenvalue is $0$. If it were diagonalizable, it would be similar to the zero matrix, hence zero — contradiction. (This is the bridge to the nilpotency page.)

If an $n \times n$ matrix has $n$ distinct eigenvalues, why is it diagonalizable?

Eigenvectors associated with distinct eigenvalues are linearly independent. With $n$ distinct eigenvalues we get $n$ independent eigenvectors — that is an eigenbasis. Beware, the converse is false: the identity is diagonalizable with a single eigenvalue.
