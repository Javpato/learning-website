Written correction — Exercise 1

The full text, to read at your own pace. For the step-by-step version with figures, see the animated correction.

1. Linearity of φ

Transposition acts entry by entry: $(M^{T})_{ij} = M_{ji}$. For $M, N \in M_n(\mathbb{R})$ and $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, we therefore have, for all indices $i, j$:

$$\big( (aM + bN)^{T} \big)_{ij} = (aM + bN)_{ji} = a\,M_{ji} + b\,N_{ji} = a\,(M^{T})_{ij} + b\,(N^{T})_{ij},$$

that is,

$$\boxed{\varphi(aM + bN) = a\,\varphi(M) + b\,\varphi(N)}$$

$\varphi$ is indeed an endomorphism of $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ — a space of dimension $n^2$.

2. φ is an involution

Transposing twice gives back the original matrix: $(M^{T})^{T} = M$, so

$$\boxed{\varphi^2 = \mathrm{id}}$$

The polynomial $X^2 - 1 = (X-1)(X+1)$ therefore annihilates $\varphi$. The eigenvalues of $\varphi$ are roots of every annihilating polynomial:

$$\operatorname{Sp}(\varphi) \subseteq \{-1,\, 1\}.$$

Moreover, this annihilating polynomial splits with simple roots — that alone already guarantees that $\varphi$ is diagonalizable; the next questions make this diagonalization explicit.

3. Decomposition Mₙ(ℝ) = Sym(n) ⊕ Skew(n)

Every matrix $M$ decomposes as

$$M = \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}(M + M^{T})}_{=\,S} + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}(M - M^{T})}_{=\,K},$$

where $S$ is symmetric and $K$ skew-symmetric. Indeed:

$$S^{T} = \tfrac{1}{2}(M^{T} + M) = S, \qquad K^{T} = \tfrac{1}{2}(M^{T} - M) = -K.$$

So $M_n(\mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{Sym}(n) + \mathrm{Skew}(n)$. The sum is direct: if $M$ is both symmetric and skew-symmetric, then $M = M^{T} = -M$, hence $2M = 0$ and $M = 0$. Thus

$$\boxed{M_n(\mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{Sym}(n) \oplus \mathrm{Skew}(n)}$$

Dimensions. A symmetric matrix is determined by its entries on and above the diagonal: $n$ diagonal entries and $\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ entries strictly above. A skew-symmetric matrix has a zero diagonal and is determined by its entries strictly above the diagonal. Hence:

$$\dim \mathrm{Sym}(n) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}, \qquad \dim \mathrm{Skew}(n) = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}.$$

Along the way we check that $\frac{n(n+1)}{2} + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = n^2 = \dim M_n(\mathbb{R})$.

4. Eigenspaces and diagonalizability

On $\mathrm{Sym}(n)$, we have $\varphi(S) = S^{T} = S$: $\varphi$ acts as $+1$. On $\mathrm{Skew}(n)$, we have $\varphi(K) = K^{T} = -K$: $\varphi$ acts as $-1$. So

$$\mathrm{Sym}(n) \subseteq E_{1}(\varphi), \qquad \mathrm{Skew}(n) \subseteq E_{-1}(\varphi).$$

Since $\mathrm{Sym}(n) \oplus \mathrm{Skew}(n)$ already fills the whole space and distinct eigenspaces are in direct sum, these inclusions are equalities:

$$E_{1}(\varphi) = \mathrm{Sym}(n), \qquad E_{-1}(\varphi) = \mathrm{Skew}(n).$$

Concatenating a basis of $\mathrm{Sym}(n)$ and a basis of $\mathrm{Skew}(n)$ gives a basis of $M_n(\mathbb{R})$ made entirely of eigenvectors:

$$\boxed{\varphi \text{ is diagonalizable}}$$

5. Trace and determinant

In this eigenbasis, the matrix of $\varphi$ is diagonal with $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ entries $+1$ followed by $\frac{n(n-1)}{2}$ entries $-1$. The trace is the sum of the eigenvalues:

$$\operatorname{tr}(\varphi) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} - \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = \frac{n\big[(n+1) - (n-1)\big]}{2} = \frac{2n}{2},$$ $$\boxed{\operatorname{tr}(\varphi) = n}$$

The determinant is the product of the eigenvalues:

$$\boxed{\det(\varphi) = 1^{\,n(n+1)/2} \cdot (-1)^{\,n(n-1)/2} = (-1)^{\,n(n-1)/2}}$$

6. Check for n = 2

$M_2(\mathbb{R})$ has dimension $4$. The symmetric part has dimension $3$ — a basis:

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix},$$

and the skew-symmetric part has dimension $1$ — spanned by

$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

So $\varphi$ has three eigenvalues $+1$ and one eigenvalue $-1$, and we read off immediately:

$$\operatorname{tr}(\varphi) = 3 - 1 = 2 = n, \qquad \det(\varphi) = 1^3 \cdot (-1)^1 = -1 = (-1)^{2 \cdot 1 / 2}. \checkmark$$
