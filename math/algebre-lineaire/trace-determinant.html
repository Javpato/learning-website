Trace & determinant

"Trace = sum of the eigenvalues; determinant = their product — two numbers that do not depend on the basis."

Intuition

For a square matrix $A$, the trace $\operatorname{tr}(A)$ is simply the sum of its diagonal entries. From the point of view of a linear operator, it is also the sum of the eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity — very useful for connecting matrix computations with the structure of the map.

The determinant of a linear map $T \colon V \to V$ is the determinant of its matrix in any basis: this definition does not depend on the choice of basis. It can also be seen as the product of the eigenvalues, with multiplicity. Geometrically, $|\det T|$ measures the area or volume scaling factor, and the sign of the determinant encodes any change of orientation.

Formal definition

$$\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{ii} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i, \qquad \det(A) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i,$$

the eigenvalues being counted with multiplicity. Both quantities are invariant under a change of basis: $\operatorname{tr}(P^{-1}AP) = \operatorname{tr}(A)$ and $\det(P^{-1}AP) = \det(A)$.

Input matrix versus operator on a space of matrices. $\operatorname{tr}(A)$ is the trace of a matrix $A$, whereas $\det(\varphi)$ can be the determinant of a linear map $\varphi$ acting on a space of more complicated objects — for instance the space of matrices $M_n(\mathbb{R})$, which has dimension $n^2$. In the example below, do not confuse $\det(M)$ (a number attached to each matrix $M$) with $\det(\varphi)$ (a number attached to the transposition operator itself).

Worked example — transposition $\varphi(M) = M^{T}$

Consider

$$\varphi \colon M_n(\mathbb{R}) \to M_n(\mathbb{R}), \qquad \varphi(M) = M^{T}.$$

Transposition is indeed a linear map. The key is the canonical decomposition

$$M = \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}(M + M^{T})}_{S \text{ symmetric}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}(M - M^{T})}_{K \text{ skew-symmetric}},$$

which shows that $M_n(\mathbb{R}) = \mathrm{Sym}(n) \oplus \mathrm{Skew}(n)$, with

$$\dim \mathrm{Sym}(n) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}, \qquad \dim \mathrm{Skew}(n) = \frac{n(n-1)}{2}.$$

The concrete case $n = 2$, to see first

$M_2(\mathbb{R})$ has dimension $4$: the symmetric part has dimension $3$, the skew-symmetric part has dimension $1$. So $\varphi$ has three eigenvalues $+1$ and one eigenvalue $-1$, and we read off immediately

$$\operatorname{tr}(\varphi) = 3 - 1 = 2, \qquad \det(\varphi) = 1^3 \cdot (-1)^1 = -1.$$

The general case

On $\mathrm{Sym}(n)$, we have $\varphi(S) = S$: the operator acts as $+1$. On $\mathrm{Skew}(n)$, we have $\varphi(K) = -K$: it acts as $-1$. In other words, $\varphi$ is diagonalizable: its space splits into an eigenspace for the eigenvalue $1$ and another for the eigenvalue $-1$. Applying "trace = sum of the eigenvalues" and "determinant = product of the eigenvalues":

$$\operatorname{tr}(\varphi) = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} - \frac{n(n-1)}{2} = n,$$ $$\det(\varphi) = 1^{\,n(n+1)/2} \cdot (-1)^{\,n(n-1)/2} = (-1)^{\,n(n-1)/2}.$$

Classic pitfalls

The trace is not multiplicative and the determinant is not additive. In general $\operatorname{tr}(AB) \neq \operatorname{tr}(A)\operatorname{tr}(B)$ and $\det(A + B) \neq \det(A) + \det(B)$. The correct rules are $\operatorname{tr}(A+B) = \operatorname{tr}(A) + \operatorname{tr}(B)$ and $\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$.

$\operatorname{tr}(A) = 0$ does not imply that $A$ is nilpotent. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ has zero trace but eigenvalues $\pm 1$: it is even diagonalizable and invertible.

Mini-exercises

Compute $\operatorname{tr}(\varphi)$ and $\det(\varphi)$ for $n = 3$.

$\dim \mathrm{Sym}(3) = 6$ and $\dim \mathrm{Skew}(3) = 3$, so $\operatorname{tr}(\varphi) = 6 - 3 = 3$ and $\det(\varphi) = (-1)^3 = -1$.

What are the possible eigenvalues of an involution ($\varphi^2 = \mathrm{id}$)?

The polynomial $X^2 - 1 = (X-1)(X+1)$ annihilates $\varphi$, so $\operatorname{Sp}(\varphi) \subseteq \{-1, 1\}$. Since this annihilating polynomial splits with simple roots, every involution is diagonalizable — exactly the argument used for transposition.

What is $|\det A|$ geometrically for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$?

$\det A = 6$: the unit square is sent to a $2 \times 3$ rectangle of area $6$. Every figure has its area multiplied by 6, and orientation is preserved since the determinant is positive.
