Rank

"Same rank = same image dimension, not necessarily the same image."

Intuition

Rank measures how much space survives a linear map — the number of independent directions actually carried through to the image. Saying that two matrices have the same rank means they lose the same amount of information: the same "compression level".

Geometrically, in dimension 2: rank $2$ keeps a whole surface, rank $1$ flattens the surface onto a line, rank $0$ crushes everything to the point $0$. In dimension 3: rank $2$ flattens onto a plane, rank $1$ onto a line. This is not a visual gimmick: it is the direct translation of rank as the dimension of the image.

Formal definition

The rank of a matrix is the dimension of its row space or, equivalently, the dimension of its column space. These two dimensions always coincide, the rank can be read off as the number of pivots in an echelon form, and

$$\operatorname{rank}(A) = \operatorname{rank}(A^{T}).$$

For an $m \times n$ matrix, the rank–nullity theorem relates rank and kernel:

$$\operatorname{rank}(A) + \operatorname{nullity}(A) = n.$$

If two $m \times n$ matrices have the same rank $r$, they have the same number of pivots, and the same nullity.

Worked example — reduction and canonical form

Let us row-reduce

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{\;L_2 \leftarrow L_2 - 2L_1,\; L_3 \leftarrow L_3 - L_1\;} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \xrightarrow{\;L_3 \leftarrow L_3 - L_2\;} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Two pivots: $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 2$, nullity $= 1$.

The most important structural implication: two matrices of the same size have the same rank if and only if they are equivalent in the sense of row and column operations — in other words, if both can be brought, by left and right multiplication by invertible matrices, to the same block canonical form

$$\begin{pmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Classic pitfalls

Same rank does not mean same map. Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$: both have rank $1$, but the image of $A$ is the $x$-axis while the image of $B$ is the $y$-axis. Same rank implies neither same image, nor same kernel, nor same eigenvalues, nor same determinant.

The rank is not the number of nonzero rows of the raw matrix. $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ has two nonzero rows but rank $1$: the second row is twice the first. Nonzero rows are only counted after reduction to echelon form.

Mini-exercises

What is the rank of $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$?

Rank $1$: the determinant is $4 - 4 = 0$ but the matrix is not zero. The two columns are collinear — the image is the line spanned by $(1, 2)$.

Can a $2 \times 3$ matrix have rank $3$?

No: the rank is bounded by the number of rows and of columns, so $\operatorname{rank}(A) \le \min(2, 3) = 2$. The image lives in $\mathbb{R}^2$, which has no room for three independent directions.

Find $A$ and $B$ such that $\operatorname{rank}(AB) < \min(\operatorname{rank} A, \operatorname{rank} B)$.

With $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ (rank $1$ each), we have $AB = 0$, so $\operatorname{rank}(AB) = 0 < 1$: the image of $B$ falls exactly inside the kernel of $A$.
