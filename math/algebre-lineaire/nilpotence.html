Nilpotency on $\mathbb{R}_n[X]$

"Nilpotent = applying it again and again eventually sends everything to 0."

Intuition

A nilpotent map is not a transformation that "stretches" the space along independent eigendirections: it is a transformation that erases information in stages. With each application, something disappears for good, and after finitely many steps nothing is left.

Visually, everything is pushed step by step into smaller and smaller subspaces, until it lands on $\{0\}$:

$$P_n \supset P_{n-1} \supset \cdots \supset P_1 \supset P_0 \supset \{0\}.$$

Formal definition

A linear map $T \colon V \to V$ is nilpotent if there exists an integer $N \ge 1$ such that

$$T^N = 0,$$

that is, after applying $T$ finitely many times, we get the zero map. The smallest such $N$ is called the index of nilpotency (or the order). In a finite-dimensional space, being nilpotent is equivalent to all eigenvalues being zero.

Worked example — differentiation on $\mathbb{R}_n[X]$

The standard and most important example is the differentiation operator

$$D \colon \mathbb{R}_n[X] \to \mathbb{R}_n[X], \qquad D(p) = p',$$

which is indeed a linear map. The clearest intuition comes from working in the basis $(1, x, x^2, \dots, x^n)$. In this basis, $D$ moves each monomial down one step:

$$1 \mapsto 0, \quad x \mapsto 1, \quad x^2 \mapsto 2x, \quad x^3 \mapsto 3x^2, \quad \dots$$

On the coefficients, applying $D$ amounts to the shift rule

$$(a_0, a_1, a_2, \dots, a_n) \longmapsto (a_1,\, 2a_2,\, 3a_3,\, \dots,\, na_n,\, 0).$$

The degree drops by 1 with each application. The $(n+1)$-th derivative of any polynomial of degree $\le n$ is therefore zero:

$$D^{n+1} = 0,$$

and the order is exactly $n+1$, because $D^n(x^n) = n! \neq 0$. The differentiation operator on $\mathbb{R}_n[X]$ is nilpotent of order $n+1$.

Classic pitfalls

A nonzero nilpotent map is never diagonalizable. If all its eigenvalues are $0$ and it were diagonalizable, then in an eigenbasis its matrix would be diagonal with only $0$s on the diagonal — so it would be the zero map. This remark links nilpotency to diagonalizability.

The index of nilpotency never exceeds the dimension. To test whether $T$ is nilpotent in dimension $d$, it suffices to check $T^d = 0$ — no need to try larger powers.

Mini-exercises

Is the matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ nilpotent? Of what order?

Yes: its square is the zero matrix, so it is nilpotent of order $2$. It is the basic nilpotent "building block" — it sends $e_2$ to $e_1$ and then $e_1$ to $0$, exactly like $D$ on $\mathbb{R}_1[X]$.

What are the eigenvalues of a nilpotent endomorphism?

Only $0$. If $T(v) = \lambda v$ with $v \neq 0$, then $T^N(v) = \lambda^N v = 0$, so $\lambda^N = 0$, hence $\lambda = 0$.

Is the sum of two nilpotent matrices always nilpotent?

Not in general. Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$: each is nilpotent, but $A + B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\pm 1$. (The result becomes true if $A$ and $B$ commute.)
