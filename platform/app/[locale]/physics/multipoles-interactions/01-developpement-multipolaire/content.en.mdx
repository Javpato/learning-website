## Intuition — a photograph that loses its details

At large distance, the monopole answers "how much total charge?". If the answer is zero, the dipole measures the separation between positive and negative charges. If it also vanishes, the quadrupole describes a finer anisotropy. Each vanishing causes the potential to decrease by an additional power.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Moments: $Q=\int\rho\,dV$ and $\mathbf p=\int\mathbf r'\rho(\mathbf r')\,dV'$.
- Distant potential: $V\sim Q/r+(\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})/r^2+\text{quadrupôle}/r^3$.
- The first non-zero moment sets the anisotropy and the decay law.

</KeyResults>

<Collapsible title="The rigorous version — definitions and conditions">

<Rappel title="Limited development of 1/(1+u)">

For $|u|\ll1$, $(1+u)^{-1}=1-u+u^2+\cdots$. Here the small parameter is the size of the source divided by the distance, $a/r$; the expansion is therefore only valid for $r\gg a$.

</Rappel>

## Formal definition

For a localized source of size $a$ and a point such that $r\gg a$,

$$
\frac1{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}
=\frac1r+\frac{\mathbf r'\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}
+\frac{3(\mathbf r'\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})^2-r'^2}{2r^3}+\cdots.
$$

After integration on the charge,

$$
V(\mathbf r)=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}
\left[
\frac Qr+\frac{\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}
+\frac1{2r^3}\sum_{ij}Q_{ij}\hat r_i\hat r_j+\cdots
\right],
$$

with

$$
Q=\int\rho\,dV,\qquad
\mathbf p=\int\mathbf r'\rho(\mathbf r')\,dV',
$$

$$
Q_{ij}=\int\rho(\mathbf r')
(3x_i'x_j'-r'^2\delta_{ij})\,dV'.
$$

<KeyResults title="Laws of potential decay">

-  non-zero monopole: $V\sim r^{-1}$;
-  zero monopole, non-zero dipole: $V\sim r^{-2}$ ;
-  zero monopole and dipole, non-zero quadrupole: $V\sim r^{-3}$.

The field decreases each time with an additional power.

</KeyResults>

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — linear quadrupole

Let's place $+q$ in $z=\pm a$ and $-2q$ at the origin. The total charge and the dipole cancel out. On the axis, for $z>a$,

$$
V(z)=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}
\left(\frac1{z-a}+\frac1{z+a}-\frac2z\right).
$$

By expanding $1/(z\mp a)$ in powers of $a/z$, the constant and linear terms cancel, and

$$
V(z)\sim\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2qa^2}{z^3}.
$$

The quadrupole is therefore the first visible moment.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — translation of the origin and dipole moment">

For discrete charges, $\mathbf p=\sum_iq_i\mathbf r_i$. If the new origin is shifted by $\mathbf a$, then

$$
\mathbf r_i'=\mathbf r_i-\mathbf a,\qquad
\mathbf p'=\sum_iq_i\mathbf r_i'
=\mathbf p-\mathbf a\sum_iq_i
=\mathbf p-Q\mathbf a.
$$

Thus $Q$ is invariant. If $Q=0$, the dipole is also independent of the origin; for an ion, its value depends on the choice of origin.

</Collapsible>

## Symmetries and chemistry

-  An inversion symmetry cancels the dipole;
-  A spherical symmetry leaves only the external monopole;
-  A polar neutral molecule is dominated by its dipole;
- A neutral nonpolar molecule but anisotropic, like a symmetrical linear model, can be quadrupolar.

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Apply the development near the source.** This is an asymptotic expansion valid for $r\gg a$, not a global identity.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forget the quadrupole convention.** Several conventions exist; you must state which one is used before comparing components.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Declare the invariant dipole for a charged system.** The formula $\mathbf p'=\mathbf p-Q\mathbf a$ shows exactly the missing condition.

</Pitfall>

<MissionSolved>

The total charge is $q+q-2q=0$ and the inversion symmetry cancels the dipole. The first visible moment is therefore the quadrupole. On the axis, $V(z)\sim2qa^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0z^3)$: the potential decreases in $1/r^3$, a power faster with each canceled lower moment. Three charges are thus compressed into a single distant signature.

</MissionSolved>
