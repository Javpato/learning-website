# Induced dipoles and polarizability

<Accroche>

Even without a permanent dipole, a molecule can be polarized: the external field distorts its electron cloud and creates a dipole.

## Intuition

An electric field slightly displaces the centers of positive and negative charges. For a weak field, the response is proportional to the field. A molecule that is easy to deform has a large polarizability.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Response: $\mathbf p_{\mathrm{ind}}=\boldsymbol\alpha\mathbf E$.
-  Isotropic energy: $U_{\mathrm{ind}}=-\tfrac12\alpha E^2$.
- Ion–polarizable molecule: $U\propto-r^{-4}$ and $F\propto-r^{-5}$.

</KeyResults>

<Collapsible title="The rigorous version — definitions and conditions">

<Rappel title="Permanent or induced dipole">

A permanent dipole exists before the field and carries the energy $-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E$. An induced dipole gradually grows with the field; this construction work produces the factor $1/2$.

</Rappel>

## Linear response

In an isotropic medium,

$$
\mathbf p_{\mathrm{ind}}=\alpha\mathbf E.
$$

For an anisotropic molecule,

$$
\mathbf p_{\mathrm{ind}}=\boldsymbol\alpha\mathbf E,
$$

where $\boldsymbol\alpha$ is a symmetric tensor in the usual equilibrium situations.

Energy is not $-\mathbf p_{\mathrm{ind}}\cdot\mathbf E$, because the dipole did not exist before the application of the field. It is built up gradually:

$$
\boxed{\ U_{\mathrm{ind}}=-\frac12\alpha E^2\ }
$$

in the isotropic case.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — the one-half factor">

During the quasi-static increase of the field from $0$ to $E$, the dipole is $p(E')=\alpha E'$. The polarization work leads to

$$
U_{\mathrm{ind}}
=-\int_0^E p(E')\,dE'
=-\int_0^E\alpha E'\,dE'
=-\frac12\alpha E^2.
$$

Using $-pE$ directly would count as if the final dipole had existed throughout the entire construction.

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — polarizable ion and molecule

The field of an ion $q$ is

$$
E(r)=\frac{k_e|q|}{r^2}.
$$

Thus

$$
U(r)=-\frac12\alpha k_e^2\frac{q^2}{r^4},
\qquad
F_r=-\frac{dU}{dr}
=-2\alpha k_e^2\frac{q^2}{r^5}.
$$

The force is always attractive in this model: changing the sign of the ion reverses the induced dipole, but the energy depends on $q^2$.

## Anisotropy and orientation

For a linear molecule making an angle $\theta$ with a uniform field,

$$
U=-\frac12E^2
\left(\alpha_\perp\sin^2\theta+\alpha_\parallel\cos^2\theta\right).
$$

If $\alpha_\parallel>\alpha_\perp$, the most polarizable axis aligns with the field. The torque is

$$
\tau_\theta
=-\frac12E^2(\alpha_\parallel-\alpha_\perp)\sin2\theta.
$$

## Mental visualization

Gradually vary $E$: the length of the arrow $\mathbf p_{\mathrm{ind}}$ increases linearly, while the depth of the energy increases like $E^2$. Double the field: the dipole doubles, but the energy is multiplied by four.

## Induction and dispersion

The charge–induced dipole interaction has an energy in $r^{-4}$. An induced dipole–dipole interaction can produce a law in $r^{-6}$. London dispersion also follows a law $r^{-6}$, but its origin is quantum: correlated fluctuations, not a simple permanent classical dipole.

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Forget the factor $1/2$.** It distinguishes an induced dipole from a permanent dipole already present in the field.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Treat $\alpha$ as always scalar.** An anisotropic molecule can respond differently depending on its axes.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Identifier induction and dispersion.** Their distance law may coincide, but their physical mechanisms are not the same.

</Pitfall>

<MissionSolved>

When $r$ doubles, the ionic field is divided by $4$, so is the induced dipole. Like $U_{\mathrm{ind}}\propto-E^2$, the energy is divided by $16$ while remaining attractive; the force decreases even faster, in $r^{-5}$. A law in $r^{-6}$ can appear between induced dipoles, but the London dispersion that shares it is a distinct quantum mechanism.

</MissionSolved>
