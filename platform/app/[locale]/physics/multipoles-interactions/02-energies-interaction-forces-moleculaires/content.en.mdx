## Intuition

Two distributions do not attract or repel each other based only on their distance: their orientation matters. Two dipoles can be attractive head-to-tail and repulsive side-by-side, even when the same moments are used.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

-  Dipole–dipole: $U_{dd}=\dfrac{\mathbf p_1\cdot\mathbf p_2-3(\mathbf p_1\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})(\mathbf p_2\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}$.
-  Force and torque: $\mathbf F=-\nabla U$, $\boldsymbol\tau_2=\mathbf p_2\times\mathbf E_1$.
- Si $U=-C/r^n$, then $F_r=-nC/r^{n+1}$.

</KeyResults>

<Collapsible title="The rigorous version — definitions and conditions">

<Rappel title="Projections and radial derivative">

$\mathbf a\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}$ is the signed projection of $\mathbf a$ on the separation axis. With a fixed orientation, $F_r=-dU/dr$, with $\mathbf e_r$ oriented towards increasing distances.

</Rappel>

## The fundamental interactions

For a charge $q$ placed in the potential of a dipole $\mathbf p$,

$$
U_{q-p}=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}
\frac{q\,\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}.
$$

For two permanent dipoles,

$$
\boxed{
U_{dd}=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}
\left[
\mathbf p_1\cdot\mathbf p_2
-3(\mathbf p_1\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})
(\mathbf p_2\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})
\right].
}
$$

With fixed orientation,

$$
\mathbf F=-\nabla U,\qquad
\boldsymbol\tau_2=\mathbf p_2\times\mathbf E_1.
$$

<KeyResults title="Energy scales">

-  charge–charge: $r^{-1}$;
-  charge–dipole: $r^{-2}$ ;
-  dipole–dipole: $r^{-3}$.

A $U=-C/r^n$ energy produces an attractive radial force of magnitude $nC/r^{n+1}$.

</KeyResults>

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — four orientations

Two equal dipoles are separated along the $x$-axis. With $k_e=1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$:

-  parallel to $x$: $U=-2k_ep^2/r^3$;
-  antiparallel to $x$: $U=+2k_ep^2/r^3$;
- parallel along $y$: $U=+k_ep^2/r^3$;
-  antiparallel along $y$: $U=-k_ep^2/r^3$.

The sign therefore does not depend solely on the product $\mathbf p_1\cdot\mathbf p_2$: the projections on the separation axis are essential.

The widget shows a dipole in an imposed field. For the interaction of two dipoles, the imposed field is simply the one created locally by the first: the second tries to align with it.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — small orientation oscillations">

At a fixed position, the second dipole has $U(\theta)=-p_2E_1\cos\theta$. Near the stable alignment, $\cos\theta\approx1-\theta^2/2$, therefore

$$
U(\theta)\approx-p_2E_1+\frac12p_2E_1\theta^2.
$$

With a moment of inertia $I$, the equation $I\theta''=-dU/d\theta$ becomes $I\theta''+p_2E_1\theta=0$, and $\omega=\sqrt{p_2E_1/I}$.

</Collapsible>

## Connection to chemistry — and model frontier

Multipoles are effective descriptors of long-range interactions and molecular force fields. At short distance, electronic overlap, exchange, charge penetration, and quantum reorganization become essential. This classical electrostatics is therefore not an exact theory of chemical bonding.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Deduce the attraction of 'parallel dipoles'.** It is necessary to specify their orientation with respect to the separation axis.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Derive a magnitude without defining the radial sense.** First write $F_r=-dU/dr$ with $\mathbf e_r$ oriented towards increasing distances.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Extrapolate up to $r=0$.** The divergence indicates the breakdown of the point approximation, not an infinite physical molecular energy.

</Pitfall>

  </QItem>

<MissionSolved>

In a head-to-tail arrangement, the two moments are parallel to $\widehat{\mathbf r}$: the bracket equals $p^2-3p^2=-2p^2$. Thus $U=-2p^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3)$ is attractive and $F_r=-6p^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^4)$. Orientation counts as much as distance; at short range, electron exchange and reorganization necessarily complement this model of the hydrogen bond.

</MissionSolved>
