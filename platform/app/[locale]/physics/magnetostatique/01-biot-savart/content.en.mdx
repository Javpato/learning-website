# Current distributions and Biot–Savart law

<LessonMeta id="phys-em-c05" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

Each small piece of current produces a small piece of magnetic field — perpendicular to everything, given by a cross product — and the total field is the sum of these contributions.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="From an isolated charge to the hydrogen bond — currents, the other half of electromagnetism">

A circular loop of radius $R$ carries a steady current $I$. In which direction does the field point at its center, and how does its magnitude change if the radius doubles? **Try first:** apply the right hand to some elements $I\,d\boldsymbol\ell$ and predict which components cancel. This micro-loop is the classic model of magnetic moments that underlie NMR and molecular magnetism.

</Mission>

## Intuition

Cut a circuit into infinitesimal elements $I\,d\boldsymbol\ell'$ (a small vector that follows the direction of the current). Each element creates at an observation point a field $d\mathbf B$:

- **perpendicular** to both $d\boldsymbol\ell'$ and to the vector $\mathbf R$ which goes from the element to the observation point;
- with its direction given by the **right-hand rule** of the cross product $d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\mathbf R$ ;
-  with a magnitude that decreases as $1/R^2$, like Coulomb — but modulated by the angle between $d\boldsymbol\ell'$ and $\mathbf R$ (zero along the axis of the element, maximum perpendicular to it).

The total field is obtained by **superposition** : we integrate over the entire circuit. Magnetic field lines never diverge from a 'magnetic charge': they **coil** around the currents.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

-  Biot–Savart: $d\mathbf B=\dfrac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\dfrac{d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\widehat{\mathbf R}}{R^2}$.
- Infinite wire: $B=\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$.
- Center of a turn: $B=\mu_0I/(2R)$.

</KeyResults>

<Collapsible title="The rigorous version — definitions and conditions">

<Rappel title="Vector product and right hand">

$\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$ is perpendicular to the plane of the two vectors, with a magnitude of $ab\sin\theta$. Orient the index finger along $d\boldsymbol\ell'$, the middle finger along $\widehat{\mathbf R}$: the thumb gives $d\mathbf B$.

## The Biot–Savart law

$$
d\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,
\frac{I\,d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\widehat{\mathbf R}}{R^2}.
$$

Every symbol counts:

- $I$: current intensity in the circuit, in amperes (A);
- $d\boldsymbol\ell'$: element of length **oriented in the direction of the current**, at the source point $\mathbf r'$ (the prime always denotes the source);
- $\mathbf R = \mathbf r - \mathbf r'$: vector going from the source to the observation point; $R$ its magnitude, $\widehat{\mathbf R} = \mathbf R/R$ the associated unit vector;
- $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T·m·A}^{-1}$: vacuum permeability, the magnetic analogue of $\varepsilon_0$;
- $d\mathbf B$: contribution to the field, in teslas (T).

**Hypotheses**: steady current (time-independent), thin-wire circuit (negligible cross-section), empty medium. The total field is

$$
\mathbf B(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}
\oint\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\widehat{\mathbf R}}{R^2}.
$$

<KeyResults title="The three canonical results">

- **Infinite straight wire**, at a distance $r$ of the axis: $B(r)=\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$, circular field lines around the wire.
- **Center of a circular loop** of radius $R$: $B=\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2R}$, directed along the axis.
- **Axis of a coil**, on the coast $z$ : $B_z(z)=\dfrac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2\,(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}$.

</KeyResults>

## The symmetry of currents — a warning

Current is a quantity **oriented**. When you use a symmetry plane to predict the direction of $\mathbf B$, reflection transforms geometry **and** the direction of the current. Moreover, $\mathbf B$ is a vector *axial* (it comes from a cross product): in a plane of symmetry that contains the currents, $\mathbf B$ is **perpendicular** to this plane — the opposite of the electric field, which would be contained there. Always determine which components cancel out *before* to integrate: it's often half the work.

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — the infinite wire by direct integration

An infinite straight wire carries $I$ along the $z$ axis. Let us find $\mathbf B$ at a point $M$ at a distance $a$ from the wire.

**Step 1 — symmetry and direction.** By invariance under translation and rotation around the wire, $B$ depends only on $a$. Each product $d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\widehat{\mathbf R}$ points along the orthoradial vector $\mathbf e_\varphi$ (right-hand rule): the field **winds** around the wire.

**Step 2 — implementation.** Source element in $z$: $d\boldsymbol\ell' = dz\,\mathbf e_z$; observation point in $(a, 0, 0)$; therefore $\mathbf R = a\,\mathbf e_r - z\,\mathbf e_z$ and $R = \sqrt{a^2+z^2}$. The cross product gives

$$
d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\widehat{\mathbf R}
= dz\,\mathbf e_z\times\frac{a\,\mathbf e_r - z\,\mathbf e_z}{\sqrt{a^2+z^2}}
= \frac{a\,dz}{\sqrt{a^2+z^2}}\,\mathbf e_\varphi .
$$

**Step 3 — integral.**

$$
B=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{a\,dz}{(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}}
=\frac{\mu_0 I a}{4\pi}
\left[\frac{z}{a^2\sqrt{a^2+z^2}}\right]_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
=\frac{\mu_0 I a}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{2}{a^2}.
$$

**Step 4 — result.**

$$
\boxed{\ B(a)=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a}\ },
\qquad \mathbf B = B(a)\,\mathbf e_\varphi .
$$

**Step 5 — order of magnitude.** For $I = 1\ \text{A}$ and $a = 1\ \text{cm}$: $B = 2\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}$ — comparable to the Earth's magnetic field ($\sim 5\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}$). The fields of usual circuits are tiny.

## Interactive Visualization

Before handling: mentally place your right hand along the wire, thumb in the direction of the current — your fingers indicate the winding direction of $\mathbf B$. Then, on the coil: why do the *radial* $d\mathbf B$ components from two diametrically opposite elements cancel outside the axis of symmetry? Move the $d\boldsymbol\ell'$ element along the circuit and observe the $d\mathbf B$ arrow rotating with it; then activate the total field to see the sum.

<BiotSavartWidget />

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — field at the center of a circular loop">

Circular loop of radius $R$ in the $xy$ plane, current $I$, observation point at the center $O$. For each element, $d\boldsymbol\ell'$ is tangent to the circle and $\widehat{\mathbf R}$ points from the element toward $O$: the two are **perpendicular**, and the cross product
$d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\widehat{\mathbf R}$ points along $+\mathbf e_z$
(counterclockwise) with $R = \text{const}$:

$$
dB_z=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\frac{d\ell'}{R^2}.
$$

All contributions add up (same direction!) and $\oint d\ell' = 2\pi R$:

$$
B=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi R^2}\,2\pi R=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2R}. \qquad\blacksquare
$$

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — field on the axis of a coil">

Same loop, observation point in $(0,0,z)$. Here $R=\sqrt{R_{\rm spire}^2+z^2}$ is the same for all elements — note
$\rho = \sqrt{R^2+z^2}$ with $R$ the radius of the coil. Each $d\mathbf B$
is perpendicular to $\widehat{\mathbf R}$ and makes an angle with the axis: by symmetry of revolution, the transverse components **transverses** of two opposite elements cancel each other out, only the axial component survives

$$
dB_z = dB\,\cos\beta = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\frac{d\ell'}{\rho^2}\cdot\frac{R}{\rho},
$$

where $\cos\beta = R/\rho$ is the projection factor on the axis. By integrating ($\oint d\ell' = 2\pi R$):

$$
B_z(z)=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\frac{2\pi R\cdot R}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}
=\frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2\,(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}. \qquad\blacksquare
$$

In $z=0$ one does indeed find $\mu_0 I/2R$.

</Collapsible>

## Connection to chemistry

A spinning charge is a current loop: the 'orbiting' electron of the classical model is a tiny coil, whose far field is that of a **magnetic dipole** (lesson 3 of this theme). It is the classic gateway to orbital and spin magnetic moments — those probed by NMR and that distinguish diamagnetic and paramagnetic molecules (O$_2$ !). The model used in this UE remains classical; quantization will come with quantum chemistry.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Reverse the direction of $\mathbf R$.** The vector $\mathbf R$ goes **from the source to the observation point** ($\mathbf R = \mathbf r - \mathbf r'$), not the other way around. Confusing it reverses $d\mathbf B$ — the most common sign error in the chapter.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Sum the magnitudes $dB$ instead of the vectors $d\mathbf B$.** Outside the points of high symmetry, the $d\mathbf B$ have different directions: you need to project, identify the components that cancel out in pairs, and then integrate the surviving component. Integrate $dB$ directly overestimates the field.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Apply to $\mathbf B$ the symmetry reflexes of $\mathbf E$.** In a symmetry plane containing the sources, $\mathbf E$ is *in* the plane but $\mathbf B$ is *perpendicular* to it: the magnetic field is an axial vector. Using the electric rule gives a wrong direction to 90°.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forget that $d\boldsymbol\ell'$ is oriented.** Reversing the direction of the circuit path reverses $\mathbf B$ everywhere. Fix the direction of $I$ at the beginning of the calculation and keep it until the end.

</Pitfall>

## Checks

**Dimensional analysis.** $[\mu_0] = \text{T·m·A}^{-1}$, so $\mu_0 I/(2\pi r)$ has the dimension $\text{T·m·A}^{-1}\cdot\text{A}\cdot\text{m}^{-1} = \text{T}$. Same for $\mu_0 I R^2/(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}$: $\text{m}^2/\text{m}^3$ compensates the meter of $\mu_0$.

**Limiting cases.**

- Loop axis in $z=0$: $B_z(0) = \mu_0 I/2R$ — the field in the center. ✔
-  Loop axis for $z\gg R$: $B_z \approx \mu_0 I R^2/2z^3$ — decrease in $1/z^3$, signature of the magnetic dipole (lesson 3). ✔
- The wire **finite** half-length $L$ (exercise P4.1 of the TD 4) gives $B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a}\frac{L}{\sqrt{L^2+a^2}}$, which tends towards $\mu_0 I/2\pi a$ when $L\to\infty$. ✔

<MissionSolved>

All $d\mathbf B$ point along the normal oriented by the right hand; their transverse components cancel each other out. The integration gives $B(0)=\mu_0I/(2R)$. Doubling $R$ therefore divides the central field by two: moving the current elements apart weakens the micro-coil that serves as the model for the magnetic dipole.

</MissionSolved>

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

-  Biot–Savart: $d\mathbf B=\dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{I\,d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\widehat{\mathbf R}}{R^2}$, with $\mathbf R$ from the source to the observation.
- Total field by vector superposition: project, cancel by symmetry, integrate the surviving component.
-  Infinite filament: $B=\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$, circular field lines.
- Loop: $B=\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2R}$ in the center, $B_z=\dfrac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}$ on the axis, $\sim 1/z^3$ in the distance (dipole).
- The current is oriented: the reflections transform geometry **and** current; $\mathbf B$ is perpendicular to the symmetry planes containing the currents.
-  When the symmetry is very high, Ampère's theorem (next lesson) avoids the integral.

</KeyResults>
