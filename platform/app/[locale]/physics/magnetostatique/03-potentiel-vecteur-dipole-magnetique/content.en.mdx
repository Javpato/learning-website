# Potential vector and magnetic dipole

<LessonMeta id="phys-em-c07" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

Because there is no magnetic charge, the magnetic field derives from a potential — but a vector potential — and the smallest possible magnetic source is not a pole: it is a current loop, the magnetic dipole.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="From an isolated charge to the hydrogen bond — currents, the other half of electromagnetism">

A coil of radius $R=2{,}0\ \mathrm{cm}$ carries $I=0{,}50\ \mathrm A$ in a field $B=0{,}10\ \mathrm T$, at $60°$ from its normal. What is its magnetic moment, what torque does it experience, and in which direction does it rotate? **Try first:** treat the loop as a dipole $\mathbf m=IS\mathbf n$. This same classical object organizes orbital moments, spin, paramagnetism, and NMR.

</Mission>

## Intuition

Two experimental facts structure magnetostatics:

- **No magnetic charge** has ever been observed: the lines of $\mathbf B$ neither start nor end anywhere, they close on themselves. Mathematically: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$, zero flux through any closed surface.
- A divergence-free field is exactly the curl of another field: there exists $\mathbf A$ such that $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$.

Cut a magnet in two: you do not isolate a north pole, you get two magnets. The elementary brick is not the monopole (which does not exist) but the **dipole** — physically realized by a simple current loop.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf A=\dfrac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int d\boldsymbol\ell'/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|$.
- Moment of a loop: $\mathbf m=IS\mathbf n$.
-  External field: $\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf m\times\mathbf B$ and $U=-\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B$.

</KeyResults>

<Collapsible title="The rigorous version — definitions and conditions">

<Rappel title="Divergence, curl and potential">

The identity $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf A)=0$ explains why a field without a monopole can be written as a curl. Adding a gradient to $\mathbf A$ does not change $\mathbf B$, because $\nabla\times(\nabla\chi)=0$.

</Rappel>

## The potential vector

### Existence and expression

Since $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ everywhere,

$$
\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A .
$$

For a thin-wire circuit carrying a direct current $I$:

$$
\mathbf A(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}
\int\frac{d\boldsymbol\ell'}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}.
$$

- $\mathbf A$: potential vector, in $\text{T·m}$ (or $\text{Wb·m}^{-1}$);
- $d\boldsymbol\ell'$: circuit-oriented element at the source point $\mathbf r'$;
- $|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|$: source–observation distance.

Note the relationship with the scalar potential $V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{dq'}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}$: same kernel in $1/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|$, but the source $I\,d\boldsymbol\ell'$ is a **vector**, so the potential is too. $\mathbf A$ "follows" locally the direction of the currents that create it.

### Liberty of gauge

$\mathbf A$ is not unique: for any scalar function $\chi$,

$$
\mathbf A\;\to\;\mathbf A+\nabla\chi
$$

gives the same $\mathbf B$, because $\nabla\times(\nabla\chi)=\mathbf 0$. It is the analogue of the additive constant of $V$ — but much richer: a whole *function* of freedom. Only $\mathbf B$ is measurable in classical physics; we choose the gauge that simplifies the calculation (often $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 0$, called the Coulomb gauge).

## The magnetic dipole

### Magnetic moment of a planar loop

$$
\mathbf m=I\,S\,\mathbf n ,
$$

- $I$: current in the loop (A);
- $S$: area of the loop (m²);
- $\mathbf n$: unit normal oriented by the right-hand rule from the direction of the current;
- $[\mathbf m] = \text{A·m}^2$.

### Torque, energy, far field

In an external field $\mathbf B$:

$$
\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf m\times\mathbf B,
\qquad
U=-\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B .
$$

The torque tends to **align** $\mathbf m$ with $\mathbf B$ (minimum energy position $U = -mB$) ; anti-alignment is an unstable equilibrium ($U = +mB$). In a **uniform** field, the dipole experiences a torque but no net force — a field gradient is necessary to pull it (exercise P4.4 of the TD 4).

Far from the loop ($r$ large compared to its size), the created field is

$$
\mathbf B(\mathbf r)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}
\left[3(\mathbf m\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\,\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf m\right] :
$$

decay in $1/r^3$ and angular structure **identical** to those of the far field of the electric dipole.

## Electrical/magnetic analogy

| Magnitude | Electric dipole | Magnetic dipole |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moment | $\mathbf p = q\,\mathbf d$ (C·m) | $\mathbf m = IS\,\mathbf n$ (A·m²) |
| Torque | $\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf p\times\mathbf E$ | $\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf m\times\mathbf B$ |
| Energy | $U = -\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E$ | $U = -\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B$ |
| Distant field | $\mathbf E = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3}\left[3(\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf p\right]$ | $\mathbf B = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}\left[3(\mathbf m\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf m\right]$ |

**O where the analogy breaks.** It is perfect *from afar*, misleading *up close*:

- The electric dipole is made of **two separable charges**; the magnetic dipole is a **current loop** — there are no « magnetic charges » to separate.
- **Near field**: between the charges of an electric dipole, $\mathbf E$ points *opposite* to $\mathbf p$; at the center of a loop, $\mathbf B$ points *in the direction* of $\mathbf m$. Nearby fields differ where far fields coincide.
- Structurally: $\mathbf E$ derivative of a scalar potential ($\nabla\times\mathbf E=\mathbf 0$ in static) $\mathbf B$ of a potential vector ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$).

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — magnetic moment and torque of a coil

A circular coil of radius $R = 2{,}0\ \text{cm}$ carries
$I = 0{,}50\ \text{A}$, in a uniform field $B = 0{,}10\ \text{T}$
forming an angle of $60°$ with its normal.

**Step 1 — moment.**

$$
m = I\pi R^2 = 0{,}50\times\pi\times(2{,}0\times10^{-2})^2
\approx 6{,}3\times10^{-4}\ \text{A·m}^2 .
$$

**Step 2 — torque.**

$$
\tau = mB\sin 60°
\approx 6{,}3\times10^{-4}\times0{,}10\times0{,}866
\approx 5{,}4\times10^{-5}\ \text{N·m} .
$$

**Step 3 — orientation energy.**

$$
U = -mB\cos 60° \approx -3{,}1\times10^{-5}\ \text{J} .
$$

**Step 4 — assessment.** The torque brings the normal toward $\mathbf B$; the recoverable work when the loop aligns is $U(60°)-U(0°) = mB(1-\cos60°) \approx 3{,}1\times10^{-5}\ \text{J}$.

**Step 5 — distant field.** On the axis, at $z = 1\ \text{m}$: $B \approx \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{z^3} = 10^{-7}\times2\times6{,}3\times10^{-4} \approx 1{,}3\times10^{-10}\ \text{T}$ — the $1/r^3$ makes the dipole field quickly tiny.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — the distant field of the loop from Biot–Savart (on the axis)">

On the axis of a coil (lesson 1):

$$
B_z(z)=\frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2\,(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}} .
$$

For $z\gg R$, $(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}\approx z^3$:

$$
B_z\approx\frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2z^3}
=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{2\,(I\pi R^2)}{z^3}
=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{2m}{z^3}.
$$

This is exactly the dipolar formula evaluated on the axis ($\widehat{\mathbf r}=\mathbf n$, therefore $3(\mathbf m\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf m = 2\mathbf m$). The fixed identification $m = I\pi R^2 = IS$: the definition $\mathbf m = IS\,\mathbf n$ is not an arbitrary convention, it is what the far field *measures*. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — torque and energy of a rectangular loop in a uniform field">

Rectangular loop with sides $a$ (horizontal) and $b$, current $I$, uniform horizontal field $\mathbf B$, normal $\mathbf n$ making an angle $\theta$ with $\mathbf B$. The Laplace force on a straight segment is $\mathbf F = I\,\mathbf L\times\mathbf B$.

Both sides $b$ are subjected to opposing forces carried by the same average line of action: they cancel each other out without torque. Both sides $a$ are subjected to $F = IaB$, opposing, separated by a lever arm $b\sin\theta$:

$$
\tau = IaB\cdot b\sin\theta = I(ab)B\sin\theta = mB\sin\theta ,
$$

either $\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf m\times\mathbf B$ (the direction brings $\mathbf n$ back to $\mathbf B$). The resultant of the forces is zero: uniform field = torque without translation.

Energy is obtained by integrating the work of the torque:

$$
U(\theta)=-\int \tau\,d\theta' = -mB\cos\theta + C
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
U=-\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B
$$

with the $C=0$ agreement (from zero to $\theta = 90°$). $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Connection to chemistry

The current loop is the **classical model of the molecular magnetic moment**. An electron of mass $m_e$ and of charge $-e$ "in orbit" constitutes a micro-loop whose moment is proportional to its angular momentum: this is the origin of **orbital** moments. The **spin** adds to this — without a satisfactory classical picture, but with the same algebra ($\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf m\times\mathbf B$, $U=-\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B$). It is this orientation energy, applied to nuclear spins in the field of a superconducting solenoid, that makes the **MRI** ; and it is the existence of permanent unpaired moments that makes O$_2$ paramagnetic. Boundary to remember: this UE stops at the **classical** model — the quantization of moments falls under quantum chemistry.

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Seek a scalar potential for $\mathbf B$.** Writing $\mathbf B = -\nabla V_m$ is generally impossible in the presence of currents: the circulation of $\mathbf B$ around a loop encircling a current equals $\mu_0 I \neq 0$, which a gradient cannot do. The magnetic potential is a **vector**.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believe $\mathbf A$ unique or measurable.** Two potentials differing by one
$\nabla\chi$ describe the same physics. Never compare two $\mathbf A$
without specifying the gauge; only the gauge-invariant quantities ($\mathbf B$, the flux of $\mathbf B$) are observable here.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Waiting for a force from a uniform field.** A uniform field exerts on a dipole a **torque** but a net **zero** force. The $F_z = m\,\partial B_z/\partial z$ force (aligned dipole) requires a gradient — it is this that makes the iron filings 'stick' to the poles of a magnet.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Push the electric/magnetic analogy up to the near field.** Between the charges, $\mathbf E$ is opposite to $\mathbf p$; at the center of the loop, $\mathbf B$ is parallel to $\mathbf m$. The analogy is only valid for $r$ much larger than the size of the source.

</Pitfall>

## Checks

**Dimensional analysis.** $[\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B] = \text{A·m}^2\cdot\text{T} = \text{A·m}^2\cdot\text{kg·s}^{-2}\text{A}^{-1} = \text{J}$ ✔. Far field: $[\mu_0 m/r^3] = \text{T·m·A}^{-1}\cdot\text{A·m}^2\cdot\text{m}^{-3} = \text{T}$ ✔.

**Limiting cases.**

- Distant field on the axis: $\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{z^3}$ exactly matches the limit $z\gg R$ of the exact formula of the loop. ✔
- $\theta = 0$: zero torque, minimal energy — stable equilibrium; $\theta = 180°$: zero torque, maximal energy — unstable equilibrium. ✔
- $\mathbf B$ uniform: $\nabla U = \mathbf 0$, zero force, consistent with the direct calculation of Laplace forces. ✔

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ (no monopole) $\Rightarrow$ $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$, with
  $\mathbf A=\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\displaystyle\int\dfrac{d\boldsymbol\ell'}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}$
  for a filiform circuit.
- Gauge freedom: $\mathbf A\to\mathbf A+\nabla\chi$ leave $\mathbf B$ unchanged — alone $\mathbf B$ is observable.
-  Magnetic dipole: $\mathbf m=IS\,\mathbf n$; $\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf m\times\mathbf B$; $U=-\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B$; far field in $1/r^3$ of the same shape as the electric dipole.
- Uniform field: torque without force; force requires a gradient.
- Analogy $\mathbf p\leftrightarrow\mathbf m$ perfect in the distance, false at the heart of the source (inverted inner field, no magnetic charges).
-  In chemistry: the current loop is the classical model of orbital and spin moments — NMR, paramagnetism.

</KeyResults>
