# Ampère's Theorem

<LessonMeta id="phys-em-c06" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

What Gauss's theorem does for charges, Ampère's theorem does for currents: when the symmetry is high enough, a circulation integral replaces the entire Biot–Savart calculation.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="From an isolated charge to the hydrogen bond — currents, the other half of electromagnetism">

An ideal solenoid has $n=5{,}0\times10^4$ turns per meter and carries $I=150\ \mathrm A$. What field does it produce in its homogeneous region, and which tool avoids summing over all the turns? **Try first:** choose a loop that runs along the field inside and passes where it is negligible outside. This is the geometric principle of the magnet that prepares a NMR experiment.

</Mission>

*Do you feel comfortable? [Go straight to the TD (tutorial class)](../td-4/) — the theory will be here if you get stuck.*

## Intuition

Follow a field line around a wire: the field "pushes" you in the same direction all along — the circulation of $\mathbf B$ around this loop is not zero. It measures exactly *the current that passes through the loop*. If your loop encloses no current, the portions where $\mathbf B$ pushes you and those where it slows you down exactly cancel out: zero circulation. This is the magnetic counterpart of Gauss's flux: the flux of $\mathbf E$ counts the *charges enclosed*, the circulation of $\mathbf B$ counts the *currents linked*.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Ampère: $\oint_C\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\mu_0I_{\rm enc}$.
- Infinite wire: $B=\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$.
- Long solenoid: $B=\mu_0nI$ ; torus: $B(r)=\mu_0NI/(2\pi r)$ in the windings.

</KeyResults>

<Collapsible title="The rigorous version — definitions and conditions">

<Rappel title="Traffic and signage">

$\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=B\,d\ell$ only when the contour follows the field. The orientation of $C$ fixed, by the right hand, the sign of the currents that pass through the surface supported on this contour.

</Rappel>

## Statement

$$
\oint_C\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\mu_0\,I_{\rm enc}.
$$

- $C$: any oriented closed curve (the “Ampère contour”), chosen by you;
- $d\boldsymbol\ell$: length element along $C$, in meters;
- $I_{\rm enc}$: total **algebraic** current crossing a surface based on $C$, in amperes — counted positively in the direction given by the right-hand rule applied to the orientation of $C$;
- $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T·m·A}^{-1}$.

**Hypotheses**: steady-state (stationary currents) — in a time-varying regime the Maxwell term will be needed. The equality is always *true*, but it can *only be calculated* $\mathbf B$ if the symmetry allows it to be taken $B$ out of the integral.

<KeyResults title="The 4-step protocol">

1. **symmetry**: determines the direction of $\mathbf B$ and the variables on which its magnitude depends (invariances by translation/rotation, symmetry planes — not forgetting that the current is oriented).
2. **Contour**: choose $C$ following the field lines, so that
   $\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = B\,d\ell$ with $B$ constant on $C$
   (or none on certain sections).
3. **Circulation**: it becomes algebraic, e.g., $B\times 2\pi r$.
4. **Enclosed current**: compute $I_{\rm enc}$ with its sign, then isolate $B$.

</KeyResults>

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — the infinite wire in three lines

Infinite straight wire, current $I$ flowing in the $+z$ direction.

**Step 1 — symmetry.** Invariance by translation along the wire and by rotation around: $B$ depends only on $r$. Any plane containing the wire is a plane of symmetry for the currents, so $\mathbf B$ is perpendicular to it: $\mathbf B = B(r)\,\mathbf e_\varphi$.

**Step 2 — outline.** Circle with radius $r$ centered on the wire, in a plane perpendicular, oriented in the direction of $\mathbf e_\varphi$.

**Step 3 — circulation.** $\mathbf B$ is tangent to the contour and has constant magnitude:

$$
\oint_C\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = B(r)\cdot 2\pi r .
$$

**Step 4 — intertwined current.** $I_{\rm enc} = I$, whence

$$
\boxed{\ B(r)=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\ }.
$$

Exactly the result of the previous lesson — obtained here without the slightest Biot–Savart integral. All the difficulty has been moved into the analysis of symmetry: it is this that allows the 3 step.

## The other two canonical geometries

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — ideal long solenoid: B = μ₀ n I inside">

Solenoid along the $z$-axis, $n$ turns per meter, current $I$, and a length much greater than its radius. By symmetry (translation along the axis, rotation around), the field is axial: $\mathbf B = B(r)\,\mathbf e_z$, and the external field is negligible (the returning field lines are spread over effectively infinite distances).

Let's take a rectangular contour in a plane containing the axis: one side of length $L$ *inside*, parallel to the axis; the opposite side *outside*; two radial sides. Circulation:

-  inner side: $B_{\rm int}\,L$ ;
-  exterior side: $\approx 0$ (negligible exterior field);
-  radial sides: $\mathbf B\perp d\boldsymbol\ell$, zero contribution.

Linked current: the rectangle is crossed by $nL$ turns each carrying $I$, therefore $I_{\rm enc} = nLI$. Ampère gives

$$
B_{\rm int}\,L=\mu_0\,nLI
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
B_{\rm int}=\mu_0 n I ,
$$

**uniform** (independent of $r$: the same reasoning with two inner sides gives $B$ identical everywhere in the solenoid). $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — torus: B = μ₀ N I / 2πr between the windings">

Torus at $N$ with contiguous turns traversed by $I$. The rotational symmetry around the torus axis imposes $\mathbf B = B(r)\,\mathbf e_\varphi$ (circular field lines "inside the tube").

Contour: circle of radius $r$ coaxial with the torus.

- $r$ in the tube: the circle links the $N$ conductors that rise from the same side, $I_{\rm enc} = NI$, therefore $B(r)\,2\pi r = \mu_0 N I$ and

$$
B(r)=\frac{\mu_0 N I}{2\pi r}.
$$

- $r$ smaller than the inner radius: no induced current, $B = 0$.
- $r$ larger than the outer radius: each coil is intertwined once ascending and once descending, $I_{\rm enc} = 0$, $B = 0$.

The field is confined in the torus and varies in $1/r$ inside the tube — it is *not* uniform, unlike the solenoid. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Biot–Savart or Ampère? The decision rule

No widget for this lesson: the tool to build is a **method choice reflex**, not an additional image.

- **Ampère** calculates $\mathbf B$ only when symmetry makes the circulation algebraic — in practice only three families: symmetry **cylindrical** (wire, coaxial cable, current cylinder, even non-uniform as in TD 4), long **solenoid**, **torus**. One must be able *to guess the direction and the dependencies of $\mathbf B$ before any calculation*.
- **Biot–Savart** applies as soon as the symmetry is insufficient but the integral over the source remains feasible: finite segment, loop (even on its axis!), polygon, circular arc.
- School case: the **axis of a loop** looks very symmetrical — but no contour makes the algebraic circulation easy (the off-axis field is complicated). You need Biot–Savart. Conversely, the **non-uniform current cylinder** $J(r)$ seems complicated — but its symmetry is perfect: Ampère in three lines.

Ampère's theorem remains *true* in any case; it is simply *unusable as a calculating tool* without sufficient symmetry — exactly like Gauss in electrostatics.

## Connection to chemistry

The solenoid is the hidden instrument of structural chemistry: the superconducting magnets of **NMR** spectrometers are solenoids (coils) optimized to produce an intense and very homogeneous field — the homogeneity $B = \mu_0 n I$ of the ideal solenoid is precisely what one seeks to achieve. A "400 MHz NMR" corresponds to $B \approx 9{,}4\ \text{T}$: five orders of magnitude above the example wire, obtained by multiplying the turns ($n$) and the current.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Confusing the linked current with the total current.** $I_{\rm enc}$ is the current that passes through the surface bounded by *your* contour. For a current cylinder, at an interior radius $r$, it is necessary to integrate the density: $I_{\rm enc}(r)=\int_0^r J(r')\,2\pi r'\,dr'$ — not use the full current $I$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Use Ampère without sufficient symmetry.** Writing $B\cdot 2\pi r = \mu_0 I_{\rm enc}$ assumes that $\mathbf B$ is orthoradial and of constant magnitude along the contour. For a loop or a finite segment, this is false: Ampere's equality always holds, but one cannot extract $B$ from it.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the sign of $I_{\rm enc}$.** The orientation of the contour sets, by the right-hand rule, the positive direction of currents. In a coaxial cable, back and forth subtract: outside, $I_{\rm enc} = I - I = 0$ and the field is zero.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Conclude 'zero circulation therefore zero field'.** Zero circulation means that the contributions cancel each other along the contour — not that $\mathbf B$ cancels at every point. Outside of symmetry, no local conclusion is possible.

</Pitfall>

## Checks

**Dimensional analysis.** $[\mu_0 n I] = \text{T·m·A}^{-1}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}\cdot\text{A} = \text{T}$ ✔ ; $[\mu_0 N I/2\pi r]$: same with $N$ without dimension ✔.

**Limiting cases.**

-  large coil core of medium radius $R_0$ and of fine section: $r \approx R_0$ across the entire section, thus $B \approx \mu_0 N I/2\pi R_0 = \mu_0 n I$ with $n = N/2\pi R_0$ — the solenoid is found closed upon itself. ✔
-  Solid cylinder of uniform current: on the outside, $B = \mu_0 I/2\pi r$, indistinguishable from the thin wire — like Gauss's sphere seen from afar becomes a point charge again. ✔

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- $\displaystyle\oint_C\mathbf B\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=\mu_0 I_{\rm enc}$: the circulation of $\mathbf B$ counts the linked currents (sign = right hand).
- Always true in statics; useful for calculation **only** with enough symmetry: wire/cylinder ($B=\mu_0 I_{\rm enc}/2\pi r$), solenoid ($B=\mu_0 n I$ uniform inside, zero outside), torus ($B=\mu_0 N I/2\pi r$ inside the tube, zero elsewhere).
-  Protocol: symmetry → contour matching $\mathbf B$ → algebraic circulation → enclosed algebraic current.
- Decision: Ampère if the symmetry gives the direction of $\mathbf B$ in advance; otherwise Biot–Savart if the source geometry is directly integrable.

</KeyResults>
