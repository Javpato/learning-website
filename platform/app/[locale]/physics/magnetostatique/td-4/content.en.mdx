<Statement>

A straight wire extends from $z=-L$ to $z=+L$ along the $z$ axis and carries
a current $I$ (toward $+z$). Calculate the magnetic field at a point $M$
located a distance $a$ from the wire in the perpendicular bisector plane
($z=0$).

Then verify the two limits $L\to\infty$ and $L\ll a$.

</Statement>

<HintLadder>

<Hint>

Symmetry first: all the $d\mathbf B$ at $M$ lie along the same azimuthal
vector $\mathbf e_\varphi$ (cross product
$\mathbf e_z\times\widehat{\mathbf R}$). You can therefore add the
projected magnitudes—there is only one component.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Method: Biot–Savart (the wire is finite, so there is not enough symmetry
for Ampère's theorem). Parameterize the source by $z$:
$d\boldsymbol\ell'=dz\,\mathbf e_z$, $R=\sqrt{a^2+z^2}$, and the magnitude
of the cross product introduces the $\sin$ of the angle between
$\mathbf e_z$ and $\widehat{\mathbf R}$, namely
$a/\sqrt{a^2+z^2}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

This leaves $B=\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\displaystyle\int_{-L}^{L}
\dfrac{a\,dz}{(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}}$. Useful antiderivative:
$\displaystyle\int\frac{dz}{(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}}
=\frac{z}{a^2\sqrt{a^2+z^2}}+C$.

</Hint>

</HintLadder>

<FinalAnswer>

$$
B=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a}\,\frac{L}{\sqrt{L^2+a^2}},
$$

in the azimuthal direction (it circles around the wire). Limits:
$L\to\infty$ recovers $\mu_0 I/2\pi a$; $L\ll a$ gives
$B\approx\dfrac{\mu_0 I\,(2L)}{4\pi a^2}$, the field of a current element
of length $2L$.

</FinalAnswer>

<Solution>

**Symmetry and direction.** Each element gives
$d\boldsymbol\ell'\times\widehat{\mathbf R}$ along $\mathbf e_\varphi$:
the total field is azimuthal, with magnitude

$$
B=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{\sin\theta\,dz}{R^2},
\qquad
\sin\theta=\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2+z^2}},\quad R^2=a^2+z^2 .
$$

**Integral.**

$$
B=\frac{\mu_0 I a}{4\pi}\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{dz}{(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}}
=\frac{\mu_0 I a}{4\pi}
\left[\frac{z}{a^2\sqrt{a^2+z^2}}\right]_{-L}^{L}
=\frac{\mu_0 I a}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{2L}{a^2\sqrt{a^2+L^2}} .
$$

**Result.**

$$
\boxed{\ B=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a}\,\frac{L}{\sqrt{L^2+a^2}}\ }.
$$

**Checks.**

- *Dimensions*: the factor $L/\sqrt{L^2+a^2}$ is dimensionless, and
  $\mu_0 I/2\pi a$ is in teslas. ✔
- *Infinite-wire limit* ($L\to\infty$):
  $L/\sqrt{L^2+a^2}\to 1$, so $B\to\mu_0 I/2\pi a$. ✔
- *Short-wire limit* ($L\ll a$):
  $L/\sqrt{L^2+a^2}\approx L/a$, so
  $B\approx\mu_0 I(2L)/4\pi a^2$—exactly the Biot–Savart law for a single
  element $I\,(2L)$ perpendicular to the radius, with $1/a^2$ decay. ✔

</Solution>

<CommonErrors>

- Trying Ampère's theorem: the **finite** wire lacks translational
  symmetry, so the circulation around a circle is not $B\cdot2\pi a$ with
  the desired $B$ (the field also depends on $z$ outside the perpendicular
  bisector plane).
- Forgetting the factor $\sin\theta = a/\sqrt{a^2+z^2}$ from the cross
  product and integrating $dz/R^2$ as is.
- Making an endpoint error: integrating from $0$ to $L$ without doubling
  (the symmetry $z\to-z$ lets you write $2\int_0^L$, but the factor 2 is
  essential).

</CommonErrors>

<Statement>

A circular loop of radius $R$ carries a current $I$.

1. Establish the field on its axis:
   $B_z(z)=\dfrac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2\,(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}$.
2. Show that for $z\gg R$, the field agrees with that of a magnetic dipole
   with moment $m=I\pi R^2$.

</Statement>

<HintLadder>

<Hint>

Rotational symmetry: for two diametrically opposite elements, the
components of $d\mathbf B$ perpendicular to the axis cancel pairwise. Only
the axial component survives—project before integrating.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Every element is at the same distance $\rho=\sqrt{R^2+z^2}$ from the axial
point, and $d\boldsymbol\ell'\perp\mathbf R$:
$dB=\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\dfrac{d\ell'}{\rho^2}$. The projection factor
onto the axis is $\cos\beta = R/\rho$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For question 2: expand $(R^2+z^2)^{-3/2}\approx z^{-3}$ and compare it with
the formula for the dipole field on the axis,
$B=\dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{2m}{z^3}$.

</Hint>

</HintLadder>

<FinalAnswer>

$$
B_z(z)=\frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2\,(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}
\ \xrightarrow[z\gg R]{}\
\frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2z^3}
=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{z^3},
\qquad m=I\pi R^2 .
$$

</FinalAnswer>

<Solution>

**1. Field on the axis.** Every element is at distance
$\rho=\sqrt{R^2+z^2}$ from the point $(0,0,z)$, and
$d\boldsymbol\ell'$ (tangent to the loop) is perpendicular to
$\mathbf R$: the elementary magnitude is
$dB=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\frac{d\ell'}{\rho^2}$, with no angular factor.

By rotational symmetry, the transverse components cancel pairwise; the
axial component carries the projection factor $\cos\beta=R/\rho$:

$$
B_z=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi\rho^2}\cdot\frac{R}{\rho}\oint d\ell'
=\frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{R\cdot 2\pi R}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}
=\boxed{\ \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2\,(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\ }.
$$

*Checks*: $z=0$ gives $\mu_0I/2R$ (field at the center) ✔; dimensions in
teslas, as in the previous TD ✔.

**2. Far field and dipole identification.** For $z\gg R$:

$$
B_z\approx\frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2z^3}
=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{2\,(I\pi R^2)}{z^3}.
$$

Now the field of a dipole $\mathbf m$ on its axis
($\widehat{\mathbf r}=\widehat{\mathbf m}$) is

$$
\mathbf B=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi z^3}
\left[3(\mathbf m\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf m\right]
=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2\mathbf m}{z^3}.
$$

Term-by-term identification gives $m=I\pi R^2=IS$: seen from far away, the
loop **is** a magnetic dipole with moment $IS\,\mathbf n$. This concretely
justifies the definition $\mathbf m=IS\,\mathbf n$ from lesson 3.

</Solution>

<CommonErrors>

- Adding a factor $\sin\theta$ to $dB$: on the axis of a loop,
  $d\boldsymbol\ell'$ is **already** perpendicular to $\mathbf R$, so the
  magnitude of the cross product is the full $d\ell'$. The only angle
  involved is the one used for the *projection* onto the axis.
- Projecting with $\sin\beta = z/\rho$ instead of
  $\cos\beta = R/\rho$—the check $z=0\Rightarrow\mu_0I/2R$ detects the
  error immediately.
- In question 2, comparing with the *equatorial* dipole field
  ($-\mathbf m$ instead of $+2\mathbf m$) and concluding that there is a
  factor $-2$ discrepancy.

</CommonErrors>

<Statement>

An infinite conducting cylinder of radius $R$ carries a nonuniform axial
current density

$$
J(r)=J_0\left(1-\frac{r^2}{R^2}\right),
$$

and carries a total current $I$.

1. Express $J_0$ in terms of $I$ and $R$.
2. Determine $B(r)$ inside and then outside the cylinder.
3. At what radius is the inner field maximal?

</Statement>

<HintLadder>

<Hint>

Perfect cylindrical symmetry (invariance under translation and rotation,
with the density depending only on $r$):
$\mathbf B=B(r)\,\mathbf e_\varphi$. This is the textbook case for
Ampère's theorem.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Two integrals from the same family:
$I=\int_0^R J(r)\,2\pi r\,dr$ for $J_0$, then
$I_{\rm enc}(r)=\int_0^r J(r')\,2\pi r'\,dr'$ for Ampère's theorem.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Ampère's theorem on a circle of radius $r$:
$B(r)\cdot 2\pi r=\mu_0 I_{\rm enc}(r)$. To find the maximum, set the
derivative of the inner $B(r)$ to zero.

</Hint>

</HintLadder>

<FinalAnswer>

$$
J_0=\frac{2I}{\pi R^2};
\qquad
B(r)=\frac{\mu_0 I\,r}{\pi R^2}\left(1-\frac{r^2}{2R^2}\right)
\ \ (r\le R),
\qquad
B(r)=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\ \ (r\ge R),
$$

with an inner maximum at $r=R\sqrt{2/3}\approx0{,}82\,R$.

</FinalAnswer>

<Solution>

**1. Normalization of $J_0$.**

$$
I=\int_0^R J_0\left(1-\frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)2\pi r\,dr
=2\pi J_0\left[\frac{r^2}{2}-\frac{r^4}{4R^2}\right]_0^R
=2\pi J_0\cdot\frac{R^2}{4}
\ \Longrightarrow\
\boxed{\ J_0=\frac{2I}{\pi R^2}\ }.
$$

**2. Field from Ampère's theorem.** Symmetry imposes
$\mathbf B=B(r)\,\mathbf e_\varphi$; on a circular contour of radius $r$:

$$
B(r)\cdot2\pi r=\mu_0 I_{\rm enc}(r),
\qquad
I_{\rm enc}(r)=2\pi J_0\left(\frac{r^2}{2}-\frac{r^4}{4R^2}\right).
$$

*Inside* ($r\le R$):

$$
B(r)=\frac{\mu_0 J_0}{r}\left(\frac{r^2}{2}-\frac{r^4}{4R^2}\right)
=\mu_0 J_0\left(\frac r2-\frac{r^3}{4R^2}\right)
=\boxed{\ \frac{\mu_0 I\,r}{\pi R^2}\left(1-\frac{r^2}{2R^2}\right)\ }.
$$

*Outside* ($r\ge R$): $I_{\rm enc}=I$, so

$$
B(r)=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r},
$$

the same as for a thin wire—from outside, only the total current matters.

**3. Inner maximum.** Differentiating
$B\propto r-\dfrac{r^3}{2R^2}$ gives
$1-\dfrac{3r^2}{2R^2}=0$, hence

$$
r_{\max}=R\sqrt{\tfrac23}\approx0{,}82\,R,
$$

*inside* the cylinder: because the density collapses near the boundary,
the enclosed current grows ever more slowly, and the $1/r$ factor
eventually wins before $r=R$.

**Checks.**

- *Continuity at $r=R$*: the inner expression gives
  $\frac{\mu_0 I}{\pi R}\cdot\frac12=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi R}$ = the outer
  value. ✔
- *Small $r$*: $B\approx\frac{\mu_0 J_0}{2}r$, linear as in a uniform
  cylinder of density $J_0$ (at the center, the nonuniformity is not yet
  visible). ✔
- *Dimensions*: $[\mu_0 I r/\pi R^2]=\text{T·m·A}^{-1}\cdot\text{A}\cdot
  \text{m}^{-1}=\text{T}$. ✔

</Solution>

<CommonErrors>

- Taking $I_{\rm enc}=I\,(r/R)^2$ as for a **uniform** density—the
  nonuniformity of $J$ is precisely what changes the inner profile.
- Forgetting the Jacobian $2\pi r\,dr$ and integrating $J(r)\,dr$: current
  density is integrated over a **surface**.
- Looking for the field maximum at $r=R$, “at the boundary, obviously”:
  the derivative vanishes earlier, at $R\sqrt{2/3}$.

</CommonErrors>

<Statement>

A small loop with magnetic moment $\mathbf m$ is placed in the nonuniform
field

$$
\mathbf B(z)=B_0(1+\alpha z)\,\mathbf e_z ,
\qquad B_0 > 0,\ \alpha > 0 .
$$

1. Which orientation of $\mathbf m$ is stable?
2. Calculate the force on the loop when it is aligned with the field.
3. What happens to the torque and force if the field is uniform
   ($\alpha=0$)?

</Statement>

<HintLadder>

<Hint>

Everything follows from the energy $U=-\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf B$: the stable
orientation minimizes $U$ at fixed position, and the force is
$\mathbf F=-\nabla U$ at fixed orientation.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Aligned: $U(z)=-mB_0(1+\alpha z)$. Differentiate with respect to $z$.

</Hint>

</HintLadder>

<FinalAnswer>

Stable orientation: $\mathbf m$ **aligned** with $\mathbf B$ (along
$+\mathbf e_z$). Force when aligned:

$$
F_z=mB_0\alpha > 0
$$

—the loop is pulled toward regions of stronger field. If $\alpha=0$:
torque $\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf m\times\mathbf B$ (zero once aligned), but
the force is strictly zero.

</FinalAnswer>

<Solution>

**1. Stable orientation.** At fixed position,
$U(\theta)=-mB(z)\cos\theta$ is minimized at $\theta=0$: $\mathbf m$ is
parallel to $\mathbf B$, that is, along $+\mathbf e_z$ (the field is
positive). The torque $\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf m\times\mathbf B$ is
restoring around this position; $\theta=\pi$ is an unstable equilibrium.

**2. Force once aligned.** The energy becomes a function of position:

$$
U(z)=-mB_0(1+\alpha z),
\qquad
F_z=-\frac{dU}{dz}=\boxed{\ mB_0\alpha\ } .
$$

$F_z>0$: the aligned dipole is attracted **toward the increasing field**.
(An anti-aligned dipole, $U=+mB_0(1+\alpha z)$, would be pushed toward the
weak field—but this orientation is unstable.)

**3. Uniform field.** $\alpha=0$: $U$ no longer depends on $z$, so
$\mathbf F=\mathbf 0$—a uniform field exerts **no net force** on a dipole,
only the aligning torque $\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf m\times\mathbf B$ (which
vanishes upon alignment).

**Checks.**

- *Dimensions*: $[mB_0\alpha]=\text{A·m}^2\cdot\text{T}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}
  =\text{J·m}^{-1}=\text{N}$. ✔
- *Physical consistency*: this is the mechanism that holds iron filings
  against the poles of a magnet (strong gradient) and traps paramagnetic
  molecules in regions of intense field—liquid oxygen remains suspended
  between the poles of an electromagnet.
- *Rigorous note*: this model field does not exactly satisfy
  $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ by itself (transverse components of order
  $\alpha$ must exist off the axis); on the axis and to first order in
  $\alpha$, the result $F_z=m\,\partial B_z/\partial z$ is correct.

</Solution>

<CommonErrors>

- Confusing the two roles: the **torque** comes from the angle
  ($\mathbf m\times\mathbf B$), while the **force** comes from the
  **gradient** ($-\nabla U$). A uniform field produces the former, never
  the latter.
- Writing $F_z=-mB_0\alpha$ because of a sign error in $-dU/dz$: when
  aligned, the dipole moves toward the **strong** field.
- Forgetting to specify “at fixed orientation” before differentiating $U$:
  if the orientation also varied, you would need to treat $\theta(z)$.

</CommonErrors>
