<Statement>

A particle of mass $m$ and charge $q$ enters a uniform field
$\mathbf B = B\,\mathbf e_z$ with initial velocity
$\mathbf v_0 = v_\perp\,\mathbf e_x + v_\parallel\,\mathbf e_z$.

1. Justify that the motion splits into uniform circular motion in the
   $xy$ plane and uniform translation along $z$.
2. Determine the cyclotron angular frequency $\omega_c$ and the radius $R$
   of the circle.
3. Calculate the **pitch** of the helix (distance traveled along $z$ per
   revolution) and verify the limiting cases $v_\parallel = 0$ and
   $v_\perp = 0$.

</Statement>

<HintLadder>

<Hint>

Project $m\dot{\mathbf v} = q\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ onto the three
axes. What is the $z$ component of
$\mathbf v\times(B\,\mathbf e_z)$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the $xy$ plane, the force is perpendicular to $\mathbf v_\perp$ and has
constant magnitude $|q|v_\perp B$: it is a centripetal force. Write
$|q|v_\perp B = m v_\perp^2/R$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The pitch is the vertical distance traveled during **one period**
$T = 2\pi/\omega_c$, at the constant speed $v_\parallel$.

</Hint>

</HintLadder>

<FinalAnswer>

$$
\omega_c = \frac{|q|B}{m},
\qquad
R = \frac{m v_\perp}{|q|B},
\qquad
\text{pas} = v_\parallel T = \frac{2\pi m\, v_\parallel}{|q|B}.
$$

</FinalAnswer>

<Solution>

**1. Decoupling.** The magnetic force is

$$
q\,\mathbf v\times B\,\mathbf e_z
= qB\,(v_y\,\mathbf e_x - v_x\,\mathbf e_y).
$$

Its $z$ component is zero: $\dot v_z = 0$, so
$v_z = v_\parallel$ is constant—uniform translation along the field. In
the $xy$ plane, the force is perpendicular to $\mathbf v_\perp$ and has
constant magnitude $|q| v_\perp B$ (the magnetic force does no work, so
$v_\perp$ is constant): uniform circular motion.

**2. Radius and angular frequency.** The magnetic force provides the
centripetal force:

$$
|q|\,v_\perp B = \frac{m v_\perp^2}{R}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
R = \frac{m v_\perp}{|q| B},
\qquad
\omega_c = \frac{v_\perp}{R} = \frac{|q| B}{m}.
$$

Key observation: $\omega_c$ does **not** depend on speed—all particles with
the same $|q|/m$ rotate at the same rate (the principle of the cyclotron).

**3. Pitch of the helix.** During one period
$T = 2\pi/\omega_c = 2\pi m/(|q|B)$, the particle advances by

$$
\text{pas} = v_\parallel\,T = \frac{2\pi m\,v_\parallel}{|q|B}.
$$

**Checks.**

- *Units*: $[m v_\perp/(|q|B)] = \text{kg·m·s}^{-1}/\text{kg·s}^{-1}
  = \text{m}$ ✓ (because
  $1\ \text{T} = 1\ \text{kg·s}^{-2}\text{·A}^{-1}$, so $|q|B$ is in
  kg·s$^{-1}$).
- *Limits*: $v_\parallel = 0$ → zero pitch, so the helix degenerates into a
  plane circle; $v_\perp = 0$ → $R = 0$, uniform straight-line motion along
  $\mathbf B$; $B\to0$ → $R\to\infty$, so the trajectory tends to a
  straight line.

</Solution>

<CommonErrors>

- Putting the **total** speed $v$ in the radius instead of $v_\perp$: only
  the perpendicular component rotates.
- Writing $\omega_c = qB/m$ without an absolute value and then being
  surprised by a “negative angular frequency”: the magnitude is
  $|q|B/m$; the sign of $q$ encodes the **direction** of rotation.
- Thinking that the field accelerates the particle along $z$: the $z$
  component of the magnetic force is zero, so $v_\parallel$ is constant.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<Statement>

In a Bainbridge mass spectrometer, ions of mass $m$ and charge $q$ first
pass through a **velocity selector** (crossed electric field $E$ and
magnetic field $B_1$, perpendicular to one another and to the trajectory),
then enter an **analyzer** with a uniform magnetic field $B_2$
perpendicular to their velocity, where they trace a semicircle before
reaching the detector.

1. What speed $v$ must the ions have to pass through the selector in a
   straight line? Does this condition depend on $m$ or $q$?
2. Express the semicircle radius $R$ in the analyzer in terms of
   $m$, $E$, $|q|$, $B_1$, and $B_2$.
3. Two ions with the same charge and masses $m$ and $m + \delta m$ reach
   the detector. How far apart are they?

</Statement>

<HintLadder>

<Hint>

In the selector, write the balance of the two forces: the electric force
$q\mathbf E$ and the magnetic force $q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B_1$ must
cancel exactly.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the analyzer, the motion is circular: reuse
$R = mv/(|q|B_2)$ from exercise P7.1, with the speed imposed by the
selector.

</Hint>

<Hint>

After a semicircle, an ion lands a distance $2R$ from its entry point: the
separation of the impacts is $2\,\Delta R$.

</Hint>

</HintLadder>

<FinalAnswer>

$$
v = \frac{E}{B_1}
\quad(\text{indépendant de } m \text{ et } q),
\qquad
R = \frac{m E}{|q| B_1 B_2},
\qquad
\Delta = 2\,\frac{E\,\delta m}{|q| B_1 B_2}.
$$

</FinalAnswer>

<Solution>

**1. Selector.** For an ion to travel in a straight line, the total Lorentz
force must vanish:

$$
q\,\mathbf E + q\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf B_1 = \mathbf 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E = v B_1
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = \frac{E}{B_1}.
$$

The charge $q$ factors out, and the mass does not appear: the condition is
**independent of $m$, $q$, and even the sign of the charge** (the two
forces reverse together). The selector sorts by speed and nothing else.

**2. Analyzer.** Uniform circular motion of radius
$R = mv/(|q|B_2)$; substituting $v = E/B_1$:

$$
R = \frac{m E}{|q|\,B_1 B_2}.
$$

With the fields fixed, $R \propto m/|q|$: the analyzer measures the
mass-to-charge ratio—the $m/z$ of mass spectra.

**3. Isotope separation.** Each ion strikes the plate at $2R$ from the
entrance slit, so

$$
\Delta = 2R(m+\delta m) - 2R(m)
= \frac{2E\,\delta m}{|q| B_1 B_2}.
$$

The separation is **linear in $\delta m$**: the spectrometer is a balance
for nucleons.

**Checks.**

- *Units*: $E/B_1$ in
  $(\text{V·m}^{-1})/\text{T} = \text{m·s}^{-1}$ ✓; $R$ in
  $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m·s}^{-1}/(\text{kg·s}^{-1}) = \text{m}$ ✓.
- *Limits*: large $B_2$ → small $R$, a tight turn; large $m$ → large $R$,
  so heavy ions take a wider turn; $\delta m = 0$ → $\Delta = 0$, so two
  identical ions land at the same point, as they should.
- *Order of magnitude*: for $^{20}$Ne$^+$/$^{22}$Ne$^+$ with
  $E = 10^5$ V·m$^{-1}$ and $B_1 = B_2 = 0{,}5$ T, we obtain
  $\Delta \approx 1{,}7$ cm—visible to the naked eye.

</Solution>

<CommonErrors>

- Reversing the selector condition ($v = B_1/E$): a units check eliminates
  it immediately.
- Using $B_1$ in the analyzer or $B_2$ in the selector: each field has its
  role, which is why it helps to name them differently from the outset.
- Forgetting the factor $2$ from the semicircle: the detector measures
  **diameters**, not radii.
- Thinking that the selector sorts positive charges only: the two forces
  reverse together, so the condition $v = E/B_1$ holds for any charge.

</CommonErrors>

<Statement>

A particle of charge $q$ and mass $m$ moves in arbitrary fields
$\mathbf E(\mathbf r, t)$ and $\mathbf B(\mathbf r, t)$ under the Lorentz
force alone.

1. Prove the kinetic energy theorem in instantaneous form:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12 m v^2\right) = q\,\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v .
$$

2. Conclude that the magnetic part of the force never does work, even if
   $\mathbf B$ depends on time and position.
3. Application: an ion with charge $q$ starts from rest and crosses a
   potential difference $\Delta V$ in a region that also contains an
   arbitrary magnetic field. What is its final speed?

</Statement>

<HintLadder>

<Hint>

Take the dot product of the equation of motion
$m\,d\mathbf v/dt = q(\mathbf E + \mathbf v\times\mathbf B)$ with
$\mathbf v$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Recognize the exact derivative
$\mathbf v\cdot\dfrac{d\mathbf v}{dt} = \dfrac12\dfrac{d}{dt}(v^2)$, and
remember that a scalar triple product containing the same vector twice is
zero.

</Hint>

</HintLadder>

<FinalAnswer>

$(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf v = 0$ identically, so only the
electric force does work:
$\frac{d}{dt}(\tfrac12 mv^2) = q\,\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v$. For the
accelerated ion: $v = \sqrt{2q\Delta V/m}$, whatever magnetic field is
present.

</FinalAnswer>

<Solution>

**1. Kinetic energy theorem.** Take the dot product of the equation of
motion with $\mathbf v$:

$$
m\,\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}\cdot\mathbf v
= q\,\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v
+ q\,(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf v .
$$

On the left,

$$
m\,\frac{d\mathbf v}{dt}\cdot\mathbf v
= \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12\,m\,\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v\right)
= \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12 m v^2\right).
$$

On the right, the scalar triple product
$(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf v$ contains the vector
$\mathbf v$ twice: it is **identically zero**
($\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf v$ by
construction). Hence

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12 m v^2\right) = q\,\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf v .
$$

**2. The magnetic force does no work.** Its power
$q(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf v$ is zero **at every instant**,
without any assumption about $\mathbf B$: neither uniformity nor constancy
in time. The argument is purely geometric—perpendicular to the velocity,
so no work.

**3. Application.** Integrate from the start (at rest) to the end. The
magnetic field contributes nothing; the electric work is $q\Delta V$:

$$
\frac12 m v^2 - 0 = q\,\Delta V
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = \sqrt{\frac{2q\,\Delta V}{m}} .
$$

The result is **independent of the magnetic field crossed**: it may have
curved the trajectory, but it did not change the energy.

**Checks.**

- *Units*: $q\Delta V$ in C·V $=$ J ✓;
  $\sqrt{2q\Delta V/m}$ in $\sqrt{\text{J/kg}} = \text{m·s}^{-1}$ ✓.
- *Limits*: $\Delta V \to 0$ → $v \to 0$; at fixed $\Delta V$, heavier ions
  emerge more slowly ($v \propto m^{-1/2}$)—exactly what the time of flight
  in exercise P7.4 exploits.

</Solution>

<CommonErrors>

- Trying to prove the result *for one particular circle*: the theorem
  holds for **every** field $\mathbf B(\mathbf r,t)$—the scalar-triple-
  product argument is general, not a case-by-case geometric argument.
- Writing $(\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)\cdot\mathbf v = vB v$ and forgetting
  orthogonality: a scalar triple product with a repeated vector is always
  zero.
- In question 3, adding a “magnetic energy loss”: the magnetic field can
  neither give nor take kinetic energy.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<Statement>

In a **time-of-flight** (TOF) mass spectrometer, singly charged ions
(charge $+e$), initially at rest, are accelerated through a potential
difference $\Delta V$, then drift in a straight line, with no field, along
a tube of length $L$ to the detector.

1. Express the speed $v$ of the ions at the tube entrance.
2. Show that the time of flight in the tube is

$$
t = L\,\sqrt{\frac{m}{2q\,\Delta V}} .
$$

3. Numerical application: $\Delta V = 5{,}0$ kV, $L = 1{,}0$ m. Calculate
   $t$ for an ion of mass $100$ u and then $101$ u
   ($1\ \text{u} = 1{,}66\times10^{-27}$ kg,
   $e = 1{,}60\times10^{-19}$ C). What time resolution is needed to
   distinguish them?

</Statement>

<HintLadder>

<Hint>

The acceleration is purely an energy problem: reuse the result from
exercise P7.3, $\tfrac12 mv^2 = q\Delta V$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Inside the tube, there is no field: uniform straight-line motion,
$t = L/v$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For question 3, note that $t \propto \sqrt m$: calculate
$t(100\ \text{u})$, then use
$\Delta t/t = \tfrac12\,\Delta m/m$ to avoid recalculating.

</Hint>

</HintLadder>

<FinalAnswer>

$v = \sqrt{2q\Delta V/m}$ and $t = L\sqrt{m/(2q\Delta V)}$. Numerically,
$t(100\ \text{u}) \approx 10{,}2\ \mu\text{s}$ and
$\Delta t \approx 51$ ns: you need electronics that can resolve a few
dozen nanoseconds.

</FinalAnswer>

<Solution>

**1. Entrance speed.** Apply the kinetic energy theorem between the source
(at rest) and the tube entrance:

$$
\frac12 m v^2 = q\,\Delta V
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v = \sqrt{\frac{2q\,\Delta V}{m}} .
$$

**2. Time of flight.** Inside the tube there is no field, so the speed is
constant:

$$
t = \frac{L}{v}
= L\,\sqrt{\frac{m}{2q\,\Delta V}} .
$$

**Heavy ions fly more slowly**: $t \propto \sqrt{m}$ at fixed charge. A
simple stopwatch becomes a balance.

**3. Numerical application.** For
$m = 100\ \text{u} = 1{,}66\times10^{-25}$ kg:

$$
t = 1{,}0\times\sqrt{\frac{1{,}66\times10^{-25}}
{2\times1{,}60\times10^{-19}\times5{,}0\times10^3}}
= \sqrt{\frac{1{,}66\times10^{-25}}{1{,}60\times10^{-15}}}
\approx 1{,}02\times10^{-5}\ \text{s} \approx 10{,}2\ \mu\text{s}.
$$

For the difference between $100$ u and $101$ u, differentiate
$t \propto \sqrt m$:

$$
\frac{\Delta t}{t} = \frac12\,\frac{\Delta m}{m}
= \frac{1}{200}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Delta t \approx \frac{10{,}2\ \mu\text{s}}{200} \approx 51\ \text{ns}.
$$

The detector must resolve a few dozen nanoseconds—exactly the range of real
TOF electronics.

**Checks.**

- *Units*: $m/(2q\Delta V)$ in kg/J $=$ s²·m$^{-2}$, its square root in
  s·m$^{-1}$, times $L$ in m: seconds ✓.
- *Limits*: large $\Delta V$ → short flight (faster ions); large $m$ → long
  flight; doubling $L$ doubles $t$ and also doubles the separation
  $\Delta t$—a longer tube improves resolution, which is why modern TOF
  instruments use reflectrons.

</Solution>

<CommonErrors>

- Forgetting the factor $2$ from $\tfrac12 mv^2$ and writing
  $v = \sqrt{q\Delta V/m}$: the time of flight is then wrong by a factor
  $\sqrt 2$.
- Confusing $t \propto \sqrt m$ with $t \propto m$: the relative separation
  between neighboring masses is $\tfrac12\,\Delta m/m$, not $\Delta m/m$.
- Mixing units (mass in u in an SI formula): convert to kg **before** the
  numerical application.

</CommonErrors>
