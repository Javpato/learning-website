## Intuition

Bring a magnet close to a copper loop: as long as the magnet moves, a current flows in the loop; as soon as it stops, the current stops. It is not the magnetic field that pushes the charges — it is its **variation**. A changing magnetic flux induces an electric field which, in turn, can make charges move.

Maxwell saw the hidden symmetry: if variable $\mathbf B$ generates $\mathbf E$, then variable $\mathbf E$ must generate $\mathbf B$. Once the two couplings are in place, a disturbance in the field can sustain itself: each field regenerates the other as it propagates, no longer needing any source. It is a wave — and its speed, calculable from *electrical* constants measured on a bench, turns out to be that of light.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Faraday: $\mathcal E=-d\Phi_B/dt$ with $\Phi_B=\iint_S\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S$.
-  Maxwell couples the variations: $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+\mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf E$.
- In the void: $c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$.

</KeyResults>

<Collapsible title="The rigorous version — definitions and conditions">

<Rappel title="Flow through a surface">

For a uniform field perpendicular to a fixed surface, $\Phi_B=BA$. If it makes an angle $\theta$ with the normal, $\Phi_B=BA\cos\theta$; Faraday depends on the variation of this flux.

</Rappel>

## Faraday's law

$$
\mathcal E = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt},
\qquad
\Phi_B = \iint_S \mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf S .
$$

- $\Phi_B$: magnetic flux through the circuit (Wb $=$ T·m$^2$);
- $\mathcal E$: induced electromotive force (V), that is to say the circulation of the induced electric field along the circuit.

The sign « $-$ » is the **Lenz's law** : the induced current opposes, through its effects, the variation of the flux that gives rise to it. Assumptions: filamentary circuit, orientation of $S$ consistent with the direction of travel (right-hand rule).

Quick example: a rod of length $\ell$ slides at a speed of $v$ on rails in a $B$ field perpendicular to the plane. The swept flux varies by $B\ell v$ per second, so $|\mathcal E| = B\ell v$ — that's the principle of the alternator, and the source of almost all the electricity you consume.

## The four Maxwell equations

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0},
\qquad
\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0,
$$

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf E = -\frac{\partial \mathbf B}{\partial t},
\qquad
\nabla\times\mathbf B = \mu_0\mathbf J
+ \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial \mathbf E}{\partial t}.
$$

Let's read them line by line — each one is an old acquaintance, sometimes augmented with a new term:

1. **Maxwell–Gauss.** Charges are the sources of the electric field: it is the [Gauss's theorem](../../electrostatique/02-theoreme-de-gauss) in local form, unchanged.
2. **Conservative magnetic flux.** No « magnetic charge »: the lines of $\mathbf B$ neither begin nor end anywhere. It is the equation that justified [$\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$](../../magnetostatique/03-potentiel-vecteur-dipole-magnetique).
3. **Maxwell–Faraday.** The novelty: a $\mathbf B$ variable *creates* an electric field **with non-zero curl** — thus non-conservative. In statics ($\partial_t\mathbf B = \mathbf 0$), we recover $\nabla\times\mathbf E = \mathbf 0$, condition for the existence of the potential $V$.
4. **Maxwell–Ampère.** The [Ampère's theorem](../../magnetostatique/02-theoreme-d-ampere), plus the **displacement current** $\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t\mathbf E$ added by Maxwell: a variable electric field generates a magnetic field, even without any charge current.

In a static regime, the two time derivatives vanish and the system decouples exactly into our two semesters: electrostatics on one side, magnetostatics on the other. The entire course you followed is the particular case $\partial_t = 0$ of these four lines.

## The wave equation and the speed of light

In vacuum, without charges or currents ($\rho = 0$, $\mathbf J = \mathbf 0$), the combination of equations 3 and 4 gives (foldable demonstration):

$$
\nabla^2\mathbf E - \frac{1}{c^2}\,\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}
= \mathbf 0,
\qquad
c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\,\varepsilon_0}},
$$

and the same equation for $\mathbf B$. The fields propagate in waves at the speed $c$, determined by two constants measured in laboratory electricity.

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — the light comes out of the electrical constants

Let's calculate $c$ from the known values $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ T·m·A$^{-1}$ and $\varepsilon_0 = 8{,}854\times10^{-12}$ F·m$^{-1}$.

**Step 1 — the product.**

$$
\mu_0\,\varepsilon_0
= 4\pi\times10^{-7}\times 8{,}854\times10^{-12}
\approx 1{,}113\times10^{-17}\ \text{s}^2\text{·m}^{-2}.
$$

**Step 2 — the inverse of the root.**

$$
c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1{,}113\times10^{-17}}}
\approx 2{,}998\times10^{8}\ \text{m·s}^{-1}.
$$

It is the speed of light, found without ever talking about optics. When Maxwell obtained this number in 1865, he concluded that light *is* an electromagnetic wave — undoubtedly the most beautiful unification of classical physics.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — the wave equation from Maxwell">

In vacuum ($\rho = 0$, $\mathbf J = \mathbf 0$), let us take the curl of Maxwell–Faraday:

$$
\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E)
= -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\,(\nabla\times\mathbf B)
= -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2},
$$

where Maxwell–Ampère (without current) has been inserted on the right-hand side. On the left, the vector identity
$\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf E) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf E) - \nabla^2\mathbf E$
simplifies because $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = 0$ in the void:

$$
-\nabla^2\mathbf E = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,
\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\nabla^2\mathbf E - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,
\frac{\partial^2\mathbf E}{\partial t^2} = \mathbf 0 .
$$

This is d'Alembert's equation with $1/c^2 = \mu_0\varepsilon_0$. The same calculation, taking the curl of Maxwell–Ampère, gives the identical equation for $\mathbf B$: the two fields travel together, at the same speed $c = (\mu_0\varepsilon_0)^{-1/2}$. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Connection to chemistry

This bridge leads straight to spectroscopy:

- **UV-visible and IR**: light is the oscillating field $\mathbf E(\omega)$ that couples to electronic and vibrational transitions — the [dynamic polarizability $\alpha(\omega)$](../../multipoles-interactions/03-dipoles-induits-polarisabilite) describes the response of the molecule to this wave.
- **NMR**: coils detect, by Faraday induction, the precession of nuclear magnetic moments — the NMR signal *is* an induced e.m.f.
- **Refractive index**: in the material, $\varepsilon_0$ is replaced by $\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$ and the wave velocity decreases — the link between microscopic polarizability and macroscopic optics.

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Confusing flux and field in Faraday.** It's $d\Phi_B/dt$ that counts, not $\mathbf B$: an intense but constant field induces nothing, while a small field that varies quickly (or a circuit that deforms in a constant field) induces a lot.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forget Lenz's law or treat it as a sign detail.** "$-$" is physics: an induced current that *would reinforce* the flux change would provide free energy. Always check that your induced current opposes the cause that creates it.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Apply $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$ to an induced field.** The electric induction field has a non-zero curl: it is **not** conservative and does not derive from a scalar potential alone. The relation $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$ belongs to the static world.

</Pitfall>

## Checks

**Dimensions.** $[\mu_0\varepsilon_0] = (\text{T·m·A}^{-1})(\text{F·m}^{-1}) = \text{s}^2\text{·m}^{-2}$, so $(\mu_0\varepsilon_0)^{-1/2}$ is indeed in m·s$^{-1}$. For Faraday: $[\,d\Phi_B/dt\,] = \text{Wb·s}^{-1} = \text{V}$ ✓.

**Limiting cases.**

- $\partial_t = 0$: Maxwell–Faraday gives back
  $\nabla\times\mathbf E = \mathbf 0$ (electrostatics, potential $V$
  exists) and Maxwell–Ampère gives back Ampère's theorem: we fall exactly back on the two static semesters.
- Without the displacement current, no $\partial_t\mathbf E$ term: the system produces no wave — it is indeed Maxwell's addition that creates light.

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials (extension — outside the core program)">

- Faraday: $\mathcal E = -d\Phi_B/dt$ — it is the **variation** of the flux that induces; the sign is the law of Lenz.
- An induced electric field with $\nabla\times\mathbf E \neq \mathbf 0$: it is not conservative, no potential $V$ alone.
- The four Maxwell equations = Gauss + absence of monopole + Faraday + Ampère completed by the displacement current $\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t\mathbf E$.
-  In statics ($\partial_t = 0$), they reproduce exactly the electrostatics and magnetostatics of previous semesters.
- In the void: $\nabla^2\mathbf E - c^{-2}\,\partial_t^2\mathbf E = \mathbf 0$ with $c = (\mu_0\varepsilon_0)^{-1/2} \approx 3{,}0\times10^8$ m·s$^{-1}$ — light is an electromagnetic wave.
-  Entrance door of spectroscopy: light–molecule via $\alpha(\omega)$, NMR via induction.

</KeyResults>
