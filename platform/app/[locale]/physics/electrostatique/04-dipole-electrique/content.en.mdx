## Intuition

From a great distance, the charges $+q$ and $-q$ of a doublet appear to overlap: their potentials almost cancel each other out *almost*. The slight offset $\mathbf d$ between them leaves a residue that decreases faster than a single charge ($1/r^2$ instead of $1/r$ for $V$) but which has a **direction**: the electric landscape of a dipole has a "high" side (the $+$ side) and a "low" side (the $-$ side).

Placed in an external field, the dipole behaves like a compass: the field pulls the $+$ in one direction, the $-$ in the other — no net force if the field is uniform, but a **torque** that aligns it.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Dipole moment: $\mathbf p=q\mathbf d$ or $\mathbf p=\sum_iq_i\mathbf r_i$ for a neutral distribution.
- Distant potential: $V=\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$.
-  External field: $\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf p\times\mathbf E$ and $U=-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E$.

</KeyResults>

## Dipole moment

For two charges $\pm q$ separated by the vector $\mathbf d$ oriented **from the negative charge to the positive**:

$$
\mathbf p=q\,\mathbf d .
$$

For any *neutral* distribution:

$$
\mathbf p=\int\mathbf r'\,\rho(\mathbf r')\,dV' ,
\qquad\text{ou en discret}\qquad
\mathbf p=\sum_i q_i\,\mathbf r_i .
$$

- SI unit: C·m; in chemistry one uses the **debye**, $1\ \mathrm{D}=3{,}33564\times10^{-30}$ C.m.
- For a distribution **neutral**, $\mathbf p$ does not depend on the choice of origin (the total zero charge absorbs the change) — this is what makes it a property *intrinsic* of the molecule.
-  If the total charge is not zero, $\mathbf p$ depends on the origin: one only talks about the 'dipole moment of an ion' with an explicit origin convention.

## Potential and distant field

At a great distance ($r \gg d$), the potential of the dipole is

$$
V(\mathbf r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,
\frac{\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}}{r^{2}}
=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^{2}} ,
$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\mathbf p$ and the observation direction $\widehat{\mathbf r}$. The field is deduced from it ($\mathbf E = -\nabla V$):

$$
\mathbf E(\mathbf r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^{3}}
\Bigl[3(\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\,\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf p\Bigr].
$$

Reading of the formula:

- decreases: $V \sim 1/r^2$, $E \sim 1/r^3$ — one more power than the point charge, the signature of a neutral source;
- anisotropy: on the axis ($\theta = 0$), $E = \dfrac{2p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}$, along $\mathbf p$; in the equatorial plane ($\theta = \pi/2$), $E = \dfrac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}$, *opposite* to $\mathbf p$ — twice as weak and reversed;
- **validity domain**: $r \gg d$ only. Near the doublet, both exact charges are required (exercise P3.1 quantifies the error made).

## Dipole in an external field

<Rappel title="Dot product or cross product?">

$\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E=pE\cos\theta$ gives a scalar: the energy. $\mathbf p\times\mathbf E$ gives a vector perpendicular to the plane: the torque that rotates the dipole according to the right-hand rule.

</Rappel>

In a **uniform** field $\mathbf E$ :

$$
\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf p\times\mathbf E ,
\qquad
U=-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E=-pE\cos\theta .
$$

- The torque tends to **align** $\mathbf p$ with $\mathbf E$ ; it cancels itself in $\theta = 0$ (equilibrium **stable**, $U = -pE$ minimal) and in $\theta = \pi$ (equilibrium **unstable**, $U = +pE$ maximal);
- the *net* force is zero: the forces on $+q$ and $-q$ exactly cancel each other out.

In a **non-uniform** field, under the rigid dipole approximation ($\mathbf p$ fixed, small compared with the field variation scale) :

$$
\mathbf F=\nabla(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E) .
$$

A dipole aligned with the field is pulled toward the regions where the field is **strong** — it is the mechanism of ion–polar molecule attraction, and the subject of exercise P3.4.

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — from the exact doublet to the dipole

Charges $+q$ in $z = +a$ and $-q$ in $z = -a$; observation point on the axis $z > a$. Exact potential (scalar superposition):

$$
V(z)=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}
\left(\frac{1}{z-a}-\frac{1}{z+a}\right)
=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2a}{z^{2}-a^{2}} .
$$

**Distant development.** For $z \gg a$, $z^2 - a^2 \approx z^2$ :

$$
V(z)\approx\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2qa}{z^{2}}
=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{p}{z^{2}},
\qquad p=2qa
$$

— the general formula with $\theta = 0$ ($\cos\theta = 1$). ✔

**Field on the axis.**

$$
E_z=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}
\approx\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{2p}{z^{3}} ,
$$

the announced axial factor 2. **Tests**: units C·m/(F·m⁻¹·m³) = V ✔ for $V$; the sign is positive above the doublet ($+$ side) ✔; and if $a \to 0$ to $p$ fixed, the formula becomes *exact* — this is the definition of the point dipole.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — the far field from the potential">

Let's start from $V = \dfrac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^{2}}$ and take the gradient in spherical coordinates, where $\mathbf E = -\partial_r V\,\mathbf e_r - \frac1r\partial_\theta V\,\mathbf e_\theta$:

$$
E_r=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}
=\frac{2p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^{3}},
\qquad
E_\theta=-\frac1r\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta}
=\frac{p\sin\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^{3}} .
$$

Let's group in vector notation. With $\mathbf p = p\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_r - p\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta$ (the decomposition of $\mathbf p$ on the local basis), we verify that

$$
3(\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf p
=3p\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_r-(p\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_r-p\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta)
=2p\cos\theta\,\mathbf e_r+p\sin\theta\,\mathbf e_\theta ,
$$

exactly $4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3\,\mathbf E$ found component by component. Hence

$$
\mathbf E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^{3}}
\bigl[3(\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\widehat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf p\bigr].
\qquad\blacksquare
$$

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — torque and energy in a uniform field">

Forces on the two charges: $+q\mathbf E$ on $\mathbf r_+$ and $-q\mathbf E$ on $\mathbf r_-$. Zero sum (uniform field). Resulting moment, calculated with respect to any point:

$$
\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r_+\times(q\mathbf E)+\mathbf r_-\times(-q\mathbf E)
=q(\mathbf r_+-\mathbf r_-)\times\mathbf E
=\mathbf p\times\mathbf E .
$$

For energy: the torque work to turn from $\theta_0$ to $\theta$ gives $U(\theta) = -\int\tau\,d\theta$ with $\tau = -pE\sin\theta$ (sign: the torque brings back towards $\theta = 0$), hence

$$
U(\theta)=-pE\cos\theta=-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E
$$

up to a constant, set by choosing $U(\pi/2) = 0$. Minimum in
$\theta = 0$: stable alignment. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Connection to chemistry

A neutral molecule carries **partial charges** $q_i$ at the atomic positions $\mathbf r_i$, and its dipole moment is

$$
\mathbf p=\sum_i q_i\,\mathbf r_i .
$$

Two structural consequences:

- **Geometry is essential.** Polar bonds can perfectly cancel out by symmetry: CO₂ (linear, two opposing bond dipoles) and CCl₄ (tetrahedral) are nonpolar despite their polarized bonds. Any molecule possessing a center of inversion has a zero dipole.
- **Water, bent molecule.** With $+\delta$ on each H and $-2\delta$ On O, the two bonding dipoles add up along the bisector: $p = 2q\ell\cos(\theta/2)$ (exercise P3.2). Experimentally $p \approx 1{,}85$ D — it is this dipole that makes water the polar solvent par excellence, orients the molecules around the ions, and underlies hydrogen bonds.

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Guide $\mathbf d$ (therefore $\mathbf p$) from $+$ to $-$.** The physical convention is **from negative to positive**. (Warning: the historical 'chemistry' arrow sometimes points to the negative pole — in case of doubt, refer back to $\mathbf p = \sum q_i\mathbf r_i$, which never lies.) An inversion here reverses the sign of $U = -\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E$ and swaps stable and unstable equilibria.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Use the distant formulas near the doublet.** $V \propto 1/r^2$ and $E \propto 1/r^3$ assume $r \gg d$. At a distance comparable to $d$, only the exact two-charge calculation is valid — the dipole expansion is wrong there, sometimes by a lot.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Conclude 'zero torque therefore stable equilibrium'.** The torque cancels in $\theta = 0$ **and** in $\theta = \pi$; only $\theta = 0$ is stable (minimum of $U$). Always decide with energy, not with torque alone.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Waiting for a force from a uniform field.** A uniform field exerts *no* net force on a dipole — only a torque. The force $\mathbf F = \nabla(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E)$ requires a **field gradient**: no gradient, no force.

</Pitfall>

## Checks

- **Units**: $[p] =$ C·m; $p/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$ in volts ✔; $pE$ in (C·m)(V·m⁻¹) = J ✔ for torque (N·m) and energy.
- **Limits**: axial vs equatorial (factor 2 and inversion); $a \to 0$ to $p$ fixed restores the exact point dipole; far from any *neutral* molecule, the dipolar term dominates — unless it is zero by symmetry, in which case it is the quadrupole (Multipoles module).
- **Symmetry**: the equatorial plane of the doublet is equipotential $V = 0$ — consistent with $\cos(\pi/2) = 0$ in the distant formula.

<MissionSolved>

The transverse components of the two bond dipoles cancel each other out, but those of the bisector add up: $p=2\delta\ell\cos(\theta/2)$, directed from O to H. ## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- $\mathbf p = q\mathbf d$ ($\mathbf d$ from $-$ to $+$); neutral distribution: $\mathbf p = \sum q_i\mathbf r_i$, independent of origin.
- Loin ($r \gg d$): $V = \dfrac{\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$, $\mathbf E = \dfrac{3(\mathbf p\cdot\widehat{\mathbf r})\widehat{\mathbf r} -\mathbf p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3}$ — axis: $2\times$ equator, opposite directions.
- Uniform field: $\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf p\times\mathbf E$, $U = -\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E$; aligned stable, anti-aligned unstable, net force zero.
-  Non-uniform field (rigid dipole): $\mathbf F = \nabla(\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E)$ — attracted towards the strong field if it is aligned.
-  Chemistry: $1$ D $= 3{,}33564\times10^{-30}$ C·m; essential geometry (nonpolar CO₂, water $p = 2q\ell\cos(\theta/2) \approx 1{,}85$ D).

</KeyResults>
