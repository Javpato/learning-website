<ExerciseView id="phys-em-td1-01">

<Statement>

Two charges $q$ are placed at $(\pm a,0)$. Calculate the field on the $y$
axis, then its limit for $|y|\gg a$.

</Statement>

<HintLadder>

<Hint>

The $x$ components cancel by reflection symmetry.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Both distances are equal to $\sqrt{a^2+y^2}$.

</Hint>

</HintLadder>

<FinalAnswer>

$$
\mathbf E(0,y)=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}
\frac{2qy}{(a^2+y^2)^{3/2}}\mathbf e_y
\sim\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2q}{y^2}\mathbf e_y
$$

for distant $y>0$.

</FinalAnswer>

<Solution>

The vectors from the sources are $(\mp a,y)$. When the two fields are
added, the opposite horizontal components disappear and the vertical
components add, giving the exact formula. For $|y|\gg a$,
$(a^2+y^2)^{3/2}\sim|y|^3$: this is the field of a total charge $2q$ at
the origin, with its direction determined by the sign of $y$.

</Solution>

<CommonErrors>

- Adding the magnitudes before canceling the horizontal components.
- Writing the limit without accounting for the sign of $y$.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="phys-em-td1-02">

<Statement>

A semicircle of radius $R$, $0\le\theta\le\pi$, carries a uniform linear
charge density $\lambda$. Calculate the field at its center.

</Statement>

<HintLadder>

<Hint>

$dq=\lambda R\,d\theta$, and at the center the field of a positive charge
points along $-\mathbf e_r$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The horizontal components cancel; integrate the vertical component.

</Hint>

</HintLadder>

<FinalAnswer>

$$
\mathbf E=-\frac{2\lambda}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R}\mathbf e_y
=-\frac{Q}{2\pi^2\varepsilon_0R^2}\mathbf e_y,
\qquad Q=\lambda\pi R.
$$

</FinalAnswer>

<Solution>

$dE=k_e\,dq/R^2=k_e\lambda\,d\theta/R$. Its vertical component is
$dE_y=-k_e\lambda\sin\theta\,d\theta/R$. Therefore

$$
E_y=-\frac{k_e\lambda}{R}\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta
=-\frac{2k_e\lambda}{R}.
$$

For $\lambda>0$, the result points downward, as predicted by symmetry.

</Solution>

<CommonErrors>

- Forgetting the factor $R$ in $dq=\lambda R\,d\theta$.
- Keeping an $x$ component despite the symmetry.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="phys-em-td1-03">

<Statement>

A ring of radius $a$ and charge $Q$ is centered at the origin; a point
charge $-q$ is also at the origin. Write $V(z)$ on the axis and examine the
request for a Taylor expansion at $z=0$.

</Statement>

<HintLadder>

<Hint>

The potential of the ring is regular on its axis.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The point-charge term contains $1/|z|$.

</Hint>

</HintLadder>

<FinalAnswer>

$$
V(z)=\frac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}
\left(\frac Q{\sqrt{a^2+z^2}}-\frac q{|z|}\right),
$$

and no Taylor expansion exists at zero.

</FinalAnswer>

<Solution>

Every element of the ring is at distance $\sqrt{a^2+z^2}$, while the
central charge is at distance $|z|$. The second term diverges as $z\to0$:
the function is neither defined nor bounded at the expansion point. You
must therefore reject the request instead of formally expanding only the
regular term.

</Solution>

<CommonErrors>

- Replacing $|z|$ by $z$ on both sides of the origin.
- Hiding the singularity by expanding only the ring contribution.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="phys-em-td1-04">

<Statement>

A disk of radius $R$ carries a uniform surface charge density $\sigma$.
Derive the field on its axis at height $z>0$.

</Statement>

<HintLadder>

<Hint>

Split the disk into rings of radius $r$ and charge
$dq=\sigma2\pi r\,dr$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use the axial field of a ring, then integrate from $0$ to $R$.

</Hint>

</HintLadder>

<FinalAnswer>

$$
E_z(z)=\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}
\left(1-\frac z{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}\right).
$$

</FinalAnswer>

<Solution>

An elementary ring produces

$$
dE_z=k_e\frac{z\,dq}{(z^2+r^2)^{3/2}}
=\frac{\sigma z}{2\varepsilon_0}
\frac{r\,dr}{(z^2+r^2)^{3/2}}.
$$

Now $\int r(z^2+r^2)^{-3/2}dr=-(z^2+r^2)^{-1/2}$. Evaluating between $0$
and $R$ gives the stated formula. As $R\to\infty$, it tends to
$\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)$, the field of an infinite plane.

</Solution>

<CommonErrors>

- Integrating directly over the surface without the Jacobian
  $2\pi r\,dr$.
- Forgetting the axial projection of the field from each ring.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>
