## Intuition

Take the image of the relief again: $V$ is the altitude, $\mathbf E$ the slope that makes it go down. A positive charge released in the landscape 'rolls' toward decreasing potentials; a negative charge climbs the slope. The **equipotential** lines are the contour lines of this map: moving along them requires no work, and the line of steepest slope — the field — always crosses them at a right angle.

The very existence of an 'altitude' is not obvious: it is due to the fact that the electrostatic field is *conservative* (zero curl, lesson 00) — the work between two points does not depend on the path.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- $V(B)-V(A)=-\int_A^B\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell$ and $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$.
- Point charge: $V=q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R)$.
- Energy: $U=qV$; assembly: $U=\dfrac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i<j}q_iq_j/r_{ij}$.

</KeyResults>

## Definition by circulation

The potential difference between two points is defined by the circulation of the field:

$$
V(B)-V(A)=-\int_A^B\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell ,
\qquad\text{et localement}\qquad
\mathbf E=-\nabla V .
$$

- $d\boldsymbol\ell$: displacement element along an arbitrary path from $A$ to $B$ — the result does not depend on it (conservative field);
- the minus sign: move forward *in the direction of the field* causes the potential *to decrease*;
- unit: the volt (V = J·C⁻¹); the field is expressed in V·m⁻¹.

Only the *differences* of potential have a physical meaning: a reference is chosen. For localized distributions, the standard convention is $V(\infty) = 0$. **Attention** : this convention fails for infinite sources (wire, plane) whose potential diverges at infinity — a reference at a finite distance is then taken.

## Potential of distributions

Point charge $q$ (reference to infinity), at a distance $R$:

$$
V=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{R} .
$$

Decrease in $1/R$ — one power less than the field. Then, by *scalar* superposition :

$$
V=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_i\frac{q_i}{R_i}
\qquad\text{(discret)},
\qquad
V=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{dq}{R}
\qquad\text{(continu)}.
$$

This is the great comfort of the potential: no components, no unit vectors — signed numbers that add up.

## Electrostatic potential energy

**Charge in an external potential.** A charge $q$ placed at a point with potential $V$ has energy

$$
U=qV
\qquad\text{(en joules)},
$$

and undergoes the force $\mathbf F = -\nabla U = q\mathbf E$: it is pushed towards the regions that *decrease* its energy.

**Assembly energy.** Building a system of point charges costs (or yields) the work of bringing each charge from infinity against the field of the previous ones:

$$
U=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i<j}\frac{q_iq_j}{r_{ij}} .
$$

The sum concerns each **pair only once** ($i$ strictly less than $j$). Signs: pairs of the same sign → positive contribution (work had to be supplied); opposite pairs → negative (the assembly is bound). A total negative $U$ indicates a *stable* structure with respect to dispersion to infinity — exactly the cohesion criterion of an ionic crystal.

## Equipotential lines and field lines

On an equipotential, $dV = 0$, therefore for any displacement $d\boldsymbol\ell$ on the surface: $0 = dV = \nabla V\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell = -\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell$.

- The equipotentials are **orthogonal** to the field in every way;
-  points the field from high potentials to low potentials;
-  tightly spaced equipotential lines indicate an intense field (the 'slope' is steep) — this is the graphical reading to master.

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — the ring, by the potential this time

Let's take up the ring (radius $a$, charge $Q$) from lesson 01, at point $M(0,0,z)$. All elements $dq$ are at the same distance $R = \sqrt{z^2+a^2}$, so the integral is immediate:

$$
V(z)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{dq}{\sqrt{z^{2}+a^{2}}}
=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q}{\sqrt{z^{2}+a^{2}}} .
$$

No projection, no component — compare with the vector calculation from lesson 01! The field is deduced by derivation:

$$
E_z=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}
=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Qz}{(z^{2}+a^{2})^{3/2}} ,
$$

exactly the result of lesson 01. **Tests**: $V$ is maximal at $z=0$ (for $Q$ positive) where $E_z = 0$ — the top of the potential profile; far away, $V \to Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\lvert z\rvert)$, the potential of a point charge ✔; units: V = (C)/(F·m⁻¹·m) ✔.

**Attention to the converse**: $E_z = -\partial V/\partial z$ is only lawful here because $V$ is known *along the entire axis*. Knowing $V$ at a single point gives no field — and the transverse components would require $V$ off the axis.

## Poisson and Laplace

<ProvenanceBadge provenance="reconstruction" />

By combining $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$ and the local Gauss form $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$:

$$
\nabla^{2}V=-\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}
\qquad\text{(Poisson)},
\qquad
\nabla^{2}V=0\ \ \text{là où }\rho=0
\qquad\text{(Laplace)}.
$$

High-confidence extension: this is the equation solved by molecular electrostatics software, and the direct link with question 2 of CC (continuous assessment) (checking that a given potential implies a given charge density).

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — the potential of the point charge from circulation">

Let's take a charge $q$ at the origin, the reference $V(\infty) = 0$, and calculate $V$ at a distance $R$. The field is radial: $\mathbf E = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^{2}}\,\mathbf e_r$. Let's choose the simplest path — a radius coming from infinity — since the result does not depend on the path:

$$
V(R)-\underbrace{V(\infty)}_{0}
=-\int_\infty^R\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell
=-\int_\infty^R\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^{2}}\,dr .
$$

The integral of $r^{-2}$ is $-r^{-1}$:

$$
V(R)=-\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Bigl[-\frac1r\Bigr]_\infty^R
=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac1R .
$$

Reverse check: $-\dfrac{\partial}{\partial r}\Bigl(\dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\Bigr) =\dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^{2}}$ — the Coulomb field is indeed found. The circulation ↔ gradient coherence is the deep structure of this lesson. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Connection to chemistry

The **molecular electrostatic potential maps** (MEP) color a molecular surface according to the potential created by nuclei and electrons: red for electron-rich regions (attractive potential for an electrophile), blue for electron-poor regions. They guide reasoning about nucleophilic/electrophilic attack sites, hydrogen bonds, solvation.

Two essential precautions:

-  A MEP map is **dependent on the model** — calculation method, basis, chosen surface (often an isodensity) change the image;
- the potential **is not the electron density** : it's the greatness *electrostatic* created by the distribution, not the distribution itself. Confusing the two leads to misunderstandings about responsiveness.

In short: an excellent tool *qualitative*, to be read with the critical mind of a physicist.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Losing the minus sign** — in $V(B)-V(A) = -\int\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell$ as in $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$. Infallible marker: the field lowers the potentials, a positive charge accelerates towards the decreasing $V$. If your result says otherwise, a sign has been lost.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Treat $V$ as a vector** — or sum the potentials "by components." The potential is a *signed scalar*: the contributions are added algebraically, and a zero potential at a point does not imply a zero field (between two opposite charges, $V = 0$ where $\mathbf E$ is intense).

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Count each pair twice in the assembly energy.** The sum $\sum_{i<j}$ visits each interaction **once**. Sum over all pairs $(i,j)$ without the condition doubles the result — the classic error of exercise P2.4.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Use $V(\infty) = 0$ for an infinite source.** For the infinite wire, $V(r) = -\dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln(r/r_0)$: impossible to use the reference at infinity (the logarithm diverges). Reference at finite distance required — this is the framework of exercise P2.3.

</Pitfall>

## Checks

- **Units**: $V$ in volts = J·C⁻¹; $qV$ in joules; $\nabla V$ in V·m⁻¹ ✔.
- **As limits**: any localized distribution must give
  $V \to Q_{\mathrm{tot}}/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$ in the distance; the extremes of $V$
  on an axis must coincide with the zeros of the field component.
- **Internal consistency**: deriving the $V$ found must give back the known $\mathbf E$ (and vice versa) — a free round-trip that validates both.

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- $V(B)-V(A) = -\int_A^B\mathbf E\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell$ (independent of the path) and $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$.
- Point charge: $V = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R}$ (reference to infinity); **scalar** superposition for distributions.
- $U = qV$; assembly energy $U = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i<j}\dfrac{q_iq_j}{r_{ij}}$ (each pair once); $\mathbf F = -\nabla U$.
-  Equipotential lines ⟂ field; the field lowers the potentials; closely spaced equipotentials = strong field.
- Poisson $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$, Laplace $\nabla^2V = 0$ in vacuum (extension).
- MEP maps in chemistry: powerful but model-dependent, and the potential is not **not** the electronic density.

</KeyResults>
