## Intuition

A field is a defined quantity *at every point in space*.

- A temperature map: at each point, **a number** — field *scalar*.
- A wind map: at every point, **an arrow** — field *vector*.

Electrostatics deals precisely with these two types: the potential $V$ is the electrical 'altitude map,' the field $\mathbf E$ is the 'slope map.' Differential operators translate one into the other: the gradient transforms altitude into slope, the divergence measures whether the arrows 'exit' a point, the curl measures whether they 'swirl' around it.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=ab\cos\theta$ and $\|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\|=ab\sin\theta$.
- $\nabla V=(\partial_xV,\partial_yV,\partial_zV)$.
- $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$ and $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$.

</KeyResults>

## Dot product and cross product

For two vectors $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$ forming an angle $\theta$:

$$
\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = ab\cos\theta,
\qquad
\|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\| = ab\sin\theta .
$$

- $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b$ is a **scalar**: it measures alignment. Maximum when the vectors are parallel, zero when they are perpendicular. It is the one that appears in the $\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S$ flux and the $-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E$ energy.
- $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$ is a **vector**, perpendicular to the plane of $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$ (right-hand rule). Zero when the vectors are parallel, maximal when they are perpendicular. It is the one that appears in the $\mathbf p\times\mathbf E$ torque and the Biot–Savart law.

In Cartesian components:

$$
\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = a_xb_x + a_yb_y + a_zb_z,
\qquad
\mathbf a\times\mathbf b =
\begin{vmatrix}
\mathbf e_x & \mathbf e_y & \mathbf e_z\\
a_x & a_y & a_z\\
b_x & b_y & b_z
\end{vmatrix}.
$$

## Scalar fields and vector fields

The four fields of the semester, never to be confused:

| Field | Nature | SI Unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Electric potential $V(\mathbf r)$ | **scalar** | volt (V) |
| Electric field $\mathbf E(\mathbf r)$ | **vector** | V·m⁻¹ |
| Magnetic vector potential $\mathbf A(\mathbf r)$ | **vector** | T·m |
| Magnetic field $\mathbf B(\mathbf r)$ | **vector** | tesla (T) |

Be careful with the vocabulary trap: the electric *potential* is scalar, but the *vector* $\mathbf A$ potential is indeed a vector — the name says it.

## Differential operators in Cartesian coordinates

<Rappel title="Differentiate component by component">

A partial derivative freezes the other coordinates. The gradient groups the three derivatives of a scalar; divergence and curl combine those of the three components of a vector.

</Rappel>

All three are written with the nabla symbol $\nabla = (\partial_x,\ \partial_y,\ \partial_z)$.

### Gradient (scalar → vector)

$$
\nabla V = \bigl(\partial_x V,\ \partial_y V,\ \partial_z V\bigr).
$$

It points to the **steepest increase** of $V$ and is perpendicular to the level surfaces. Applies to a field *scalar*, produces a field *vector*. Unit: that of $V$ divided by a length.

### Divergence (vector → scalar)

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F = \partial_x F_x + \partial_y F_y + \partial_z F_z .
$$

It measures the **outflow per unit volume**: positive where the field 'emerges' (source), negative where it 'enters' (sink). Applies to a *vector* field, produces a *scalar*.

### Curl (vector → vector)

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf F =
\begin{vmatrix}
\mathbf e_x & \mathbf e_y & \mathbf e_z\\
\partial_x & \partial_y & \partial_z\\
F_x & F_y & F_z
\end{vmatrix}.
$$

It measures the **local circulation** of the field: a small mill placed at the point would rotate around the axis $\nabla\times\mathbf F$.

### The two structural relations of the semester

$$
\boxed{\ \mathbf E = -\nabla V\ }
\qquad\text{(électrostatique)},
$$

$$
\boxed{\ \mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A\ }
\qquad\text{(magnétostatique)}.
$$

The first says that the electric field goes down the slopes of the potential (the minus sign: $\mathbf E$ points toward *decreasing* $V$ values). The second says that $\mathbf B$ is the curl of a vector potential. Both of these formulas are valid *in the static regime*; in a time-varying regime, they complement each other (next semester).

</Collapsible>

## Worked example

Let's take the potential $V(x,y,z) = V_0\,e^{-z/L}\cos(x/L)$, where $V_0$ is in volts and $L$ is a length (so that the arguments of the functions are dimensionless — a habit to always check).

**Step 1 — gradient.**

$$
\partial_x V = -\frac{V_0}{L}\,e^{-z/L}\sin(x/L),
\qquad
\partial_y V = 0,
\qquad
\partial_z V = -\frac{V_0}{L}\,e^{-z/L}\cos(x/L).
$$

**Step 2 — electric field.**

$$
\mathbf E = -\nabla V
= \frac{V_0}{L}\,e^{-z/L}
\Bigl[\sin(x/L)\,\mathbf e_x + \cos(x/L)\,\mathbf e_z\Bigr].
$$

**Step 3 — control of units.** $V_0/L$ is in V·m⁻¹: it is indeed the unit of an electric field. The exponential and trigonometric functions are dimensionless. ✔

**Step 4 — field divergence.**

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E
= \frac{V_0}{L^2}e^{-z/L}\cos(x/L) - \frac{V_0}{L^2}e^{-z/L}\cos(x/L) = 0 .
$$

Zero divergence: this potential describes a region *empty of charges* (we will come back to it with Gauss's theorem). This example is a direct taste of question 2 of the CC (continuous assessment).

## The symmetry protocol in 5 steps

It is the most useful tool of the semester. **Before any integral**, in this order:

1. **Identify the transformations that leave the source unchanged** (translations, rotations, reflections through planes).
2. **Determine how a vector at the observation point transforms** under these same operations.
3. **Eliminate impossible components**: if a symmetry of the source inverts a component of the field without changing the point, this component is zero.
4. **Identify the coordinates on which the magnitude may depend**: each invariance by translation or rotation removes a dependence.
5. **Only then, choose the calculation tool** Coulomb (superposition), Gauss, Biot–Savart or Ampère.

Quick example: an infinitely long wire uniformly charged along $z$. Invariance under translation along $z$ and rotation around $z$ (step 1); the plane containing the wire and the observation point is a plane of symmetry, so $\mathbf E$ is in this plane; the plane perpendicular to the wire passing through the point is also, so $\mathbf E$ is radial (steps 2–3); the magnitude depends only on the distance $r$ to the wire (step 4); sufficient symmetry → Gauss's theorem with a cylinder (step 5).

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — why a field with a gradient has a zero curl">

Let's calculate $\nabla\times(\nabla V)$ component by component. The $x$ component is

$$
\bigl[\nabla\times(\nabla V)\bigr]_x
= \partial_y(\partial_z V) - \partial_z(\partial_y V).
$$

For a potential that is twice continuously differentiable, Schwarz's theorem guarantees that the mixed derivatives commute: $\partial_y\partial_z V = \partial_z\partial_y V$. The component $x$ is therefore zero, and the same argument applies to $y$ and $z$:

$$
\nabla\times(\nabla V) = \mathbf 0 .
$$

Major physical consequence: since $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$, the electrostatic field satisfies $\nabla\times\mathbf E = \mathbf 0$ — it never "swirls," and its circulation over any closed loop is zero. This is exactly what will allow the potential to be defined unambiguously in lesson 03. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Connection to chemistry

These tools are not a detour: they are the language of physical chemistry.

- The orientational energy of a polar molecule in a field is $-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E$ : a **dot product**.
- Molecular electrostatic potential maps (the red-blue surfaces in modeling software) are **scalar** fields $V(\mathbf r)$ plotted around a molecule.
- The force that attracts an ion to a polar molecule comes from a **gradient** : $\mathbf F = -\nabla U$.
- And the symmetry protocol is the direct cousin of the argument 'the bond dipole moments cancel out by symmetry' which explains why CO₂ is nonpolar even though its bonds are polar.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Confusing the scalar or vector nature of an object.** Writing « $\mathbf E = V/d$ » or adding potentials as if they were arrows are errors of *type*. Before any equation, ask yourself: on each side, scalar or vector? An equality between a scalar and a vector is false even before the first calculation.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forget the minus sign in $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$.** The field points to the potentials *decreasing* — like water flowing down the slope. A forgotten mark here reverses the direction of the field, and therefore the direction of the forces, and spreads throughout the entire exercise.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Apply the cross product without the right-hand rule.** $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b = -\,\mathbf b\times\mathbf a$: order matters. A torque $\mathbf p\times\mathbf E$ calculated in the wrong order makes the dipole rotate… in the wrong direction.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Integrate first, think later.** Skipping the symmetry protocol leads to three-component vector integrals of which two are zero. The five steps take one minute and remove the bulk of the calculation — this is the first reflex expected in TD and in exams.

</Pitfall>

## Checks

Two systematic reflexes, valid all year round:

- **Dimensional analysis.** Each spatial derivative divides by a length: $\nabla V$ is in V·m⁻¹ (an electric field ✔), $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E$ in V·m⁻² (proportional to a charge density, as we will see with Gauss).
- **Limiting cases.** A correct result must degenerate properly: the dot product of two perpendicular vectors must cancel out, a gradient must vanish at the top of a potential "dome", etc. If a simple limit fails, the calculation is wrong somewhere.

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = ab\cos\theta$ (scalar, alignment); $\|\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\| = ab\sin\theta$ (perpendicular vector, right hand).
- Scalar fields: $V$Vector fields: $\mathbf E$, $\mathbf A$, $\mathbf B$.
- $\nabla V$ (slope), $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F$ (sources/sinks), $\nabla\times\mathbf F$ (eddies) — all in Cartesian with $\nabla = (\partial_x, \partial_y, \partial_z)$.
- $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$ and $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$ (static).
- $\nabla\times(\nabla V) = \mathbf 0$: the electrostatic field is curl-free.
-  Symmetry protocol in 5 steps: source transformations → vector transformation → eliminated components → surviving coordinates → choice of tool. **Always before integrating.**

</KeyResults>
