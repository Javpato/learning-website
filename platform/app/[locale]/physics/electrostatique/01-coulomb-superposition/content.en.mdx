## Intuition

A point charge "radiates" a radial field that decreases like $1/r^2$: twice as far, four times weaker. If multiple charges are present, each radiates **as if it were alone**, and the fields add *vectorially* — arrow by arrow, point by point. There is no mysterious "shielding" between charges in a vacuum: the complexity of real fields comes solely from the geometry of this sum.

For a continuous distribution (a wire, a disk...), the sum becomes an integral: we divide the source into small pieces $dq$, each treated as a point charge.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Point charge: $\mathbf E=\dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2}\widehat{\mathbf R}$.
- Superposition: $\mathbf E(\mathbf r)=\dfrac1{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \sum_iq_i\dfrac{\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i|^3}$.
- Continuous distribution: $dq=\lambda d\ell$, $\sigma dS$ or $\rho dV$, then integrate $d\mathbf E$.

</KeyResults>

<Collapsible title="The rigorous version — definitions and conditions">

<Rappel title="Source–observation vector">

Always write $\mathbf R=\mathbf r-\mathbf r'$, from the source to the observation point, then $\widehat{\mathbf R}=\mathbf R/R$. The sign of the charge then decides whether the field follows or opposes this direction.

</Rappel>

## Coulomb's law and discrete superposition

For a set of point charges $q_i$ located at points $\mathbf r_i$, the field at point $\mathbf r$ is

$$
\mathbf E(\mathbf r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}
\sum_i q_i\,\frac{\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i}{\lvert\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i\rvert^{3}} .
$$

Let's read each symbol:

- $\mathbf r$ : point of **observation** (where the field is measured);
- $\mathbf r_i$: position of the **source** $q_i$;
- $\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i$: vector *from the source to the observation* — the cube in the denominator compensates its norm in the numerator, so that the decay remains in $1/r^2$;
- $\varepsilon_0 \approx 8{,}85\times10^{-12}$ F·m⁻¹: permittivity of vacuum, and $\dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 8{,}99\times10^{9}$ N·m²·C⁻².

The force on a $q$ charge placed in $\mathbf r$ is
$\mathbf F = q\mathbf E(\mathbf r)$ (in newtons if $E$ is in V·m⁻¹ and $q$
in coulombs).

**When does this apply?** Charges *motionless* (static), in the *empty* (or a similar environment). **When this does not apply:** charges in rapid motion (radiation), dense polarizable media (corrections will be needed $\varepsilon_0$), and of course at the scale where quantum mechanics takes over — the chemical bond is not *not* a simple classical Coulombic balance.

## Distributions continues

The source is described by a density, and the elementary piece carries

$$
dq=\lambda\,d\ell
\qquad\text{(fil, }\lambda\text{ en C·m}^{-1}),
$$
$$
dq=\sigma\,dS
\qquad\text{(surface, }\sigma\text{ en C·m}^{-2}),
$$
$$
dq=\rho\,dV
\qquad\text{(volume, }\rho\text{ en C·m}^{-3}).
$$

Each $dq$ creates an elementary Coulomb field:

$$
d\mathbf E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{dq}{R^{2}}\,\widehat{\mathbf R},
$$

where $\mathbf R = \mathbf r - \mathbf r'$ goes from the source element $\mathbf r'$ to the observation point $\mathbf r$, $R$ is its norm and $\widehat{\mathbf R} = \mathbf R/R$ its unit vector.

## The overlay strategy in 7 steps

This is the standard method, to be followed in order for each calculation:

1. **Define the source coordinate** $\mathbf r'$ (the one on which it will be integrated).
2. **Define the observation point** $\mathbf r$ (fixed during integration).
3. **Write $\mathbf R=\mathbf r-\mathbf r'$**, its magnitude and its unit vector.
4. **Write $dq$** with the correct density and the correct element ($d\ell$, $dS$, $dV$).
5. **Cancel components by symmetry** (lesson protocol 00).
6. **Integrate the surviving components** — now become scalar integrals *scalars*.
7. **Test**: units, edge cases, direction.

Step 5 is the core: it transforms a vector integral into one or two scalar integrals. Step 7 is the safety net — it catches the majority of calculation errors.

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — the ring loaded on its axis

A ring with radius $a$, with total charge $Q$ uniformly distributed, is in the $xy$ plane centered at the origin. Field at point $M(0,0,z)$ on the axis?

**Steps 1–2 — coordinates.** Source: $\mathbf r' = (a\cos\varphi,\ a\sin\varphi,\ 0)$, configured by angle $\varphi \in [0, 2\pi]$. Observation: $\mathbf r = (0, 0, z)$.

**Step 3 — vector $\mathbf R$.** $\mathbf R = (-a\cos\varphi,\ -a\sin\varphi,\ z)$, of norm $R = \sqrt{z^2+a^2}$ — **the same for all elements**: this is what makes the ring calculation so simple.

**Step 4 — charge element.** Linear density $\lambda = Q/(2\pi a)$, arc element $d\ell = a\,d\varphi$, therefore $dq = \dfrac{Q}{2\pi}\,d\varphi$.

**Step 5 — symmetry.** The ring is invariant under rotation around $z$: for each element in $\varphi$, the element in $\varphi + \pi$ creates an opposite transverse component. The components $x$ and $y$ cancel out in pairs — only $E_z$ survives.

**Step 6 — integration.** The projection on $z$ of $\widehat{\mathbf R}$ is $z/R$, therefore

$$
E_z=\int \frac{dq}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R^{2}}\cdot\frac{z}{R}
=\frac{z}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 (z^2+a^2)^{3/2}}\int dq ,
$$

and since everything is constant in the integral:

$$
\boxed{\ E_z(z)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,
\frac{Qz}{(z^{2}+a^{2})^{3/2}}\ }.
$$

**Step 7 — tests.**

- *Units* : $[Q z] / [(z^2+a^2)^{3/2}]$ = C·m/m³ = C·m⁻², multiplied by $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ in N·m²·C⁻² : we obtain N·C⁻¹ = V·m⁻¹. ✔
- *Center* ($z = 0$) : $E_z = 0$ — logical, each element is compensated by the diametrically opposite element. ✔
- *Distant limit* ($\lvert z\rvert \gg a$): $E_z \to \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 z^{2}}$ — the ring seen from afar is a point charge $Q$. ✔
- *Signs*: changing $Q$ to $-Q$ or $z$ to $-z$ reverses the field. ✔

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — the distant limit of the ring, properly">

For $\lvert z\rvert \gg a$, let's factor $z^2$ in the parentheses:

$$
(z^{2}+a^{2})^{3/2}
= \lvert z\rvert^{3}\left(1+\frac{a^{2}}{z^{2}}\right)^{3/2}
= \lvert z\rvert^{3}\left(1+\frac{3a^{2}}{2z^{2}}
+ O\!\left(\frac{a^4}{z^4}\right)\right),
$$

using $(1+u)^{3/2} \approx 1 + \tfrac32 u$ for $u \ll 1$. So for $z > 0$:

$$
E_z=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 z^{2}}
\left(1-\frac{3a^{2}}{2z^{2}}+\cdots\right).
$$

The dominant term is exactly the field of a point charge $Q$, and the first correction, in $1/z^4$, is *quadrupolar*: the ring is dipole neutral (by plane reflection symmetry $z \to$), so after the monopole, the next term jumps directly to the quadrupole. This type of expansion is generalized in the "Multipoles" module. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Connection to chemistry

A molecule is, in a first electrostatic approximation, a cloud of **partial charges** carried by the atoms ($\delta^+$ on H, $\delta^-$ on O in the water, etc.). The field it creates is obtained by superposition, exactly as above:

- **Up close**, detailed geometry matters — every partial charge, every bond angle.
- **From afar**, everything becomes simpler: it is the *first nonzero multipole moment* which dominates (total charge for an ion, dipole for water, quadrupole for CO₂).

It is the same logic as the distant edge of the ring: the details fade with distance, in a very precise order.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Add the magnitudes instead of the vectors.** Two fields of 100 V·m⁻¹ can combine in 200, in 0, or anything in between, depending on their directions. The superposition is *vector*: component by component, never magnitude by magnitude.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confuse $\mathbf r$, $\mathbf r'$, and $\mathbf R$.** The integral is over the source $\mathbf r'$; the observation point $\mathbf r$ is *fixed*. Writing the three explicitly (steps 1–3) before integrating avoids the classic trap: mistakenly integrating over a quantity that depends on the observation point.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Declaring a cancellation by symmetry without justifying it.** "It cancels by symmetry" should always be able to be said precisely: *which* transformation leaves the source invariant and reverses *which* component? Otherwise, one sometimes cancels a fully alive component—or one keeps a dead component and integrates for nothing.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forget that $dq$ carries the geometry.** On an arc, $d\ell = a\,d\varphi$ (not $d\varphi$!); on a disk, $dS = r\,dr\,d\varphi$ (not $dr\,d\varphi$). A forgotten Jacobian gives a result with the wrong units — which step 7 immediately catches, if it is done.

</Pitfall>

## Checks

The triple test of step 7, to be applied to *each* result of the semester:

- **Units** : a V·m⁻ field¹ (or N·C⁻¹), a potential in V; the combination $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 \ell^2)$ always has the dimension of a field if $\ell$ is a length.
- **Limiting cases**: far from any charge distribution *of non-zero total charge*, one must recover $Q_{\text{tot}}/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$; at the center of a symmetrical distribution, the field must cancel out.
- **Direction and signs**: the field points away from positive charges; changing the sign of the source should reverse the field.

<MissionSolved>

The $x$ components cancel and the $y$ components add: $\mathbf E(0,y)=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \dfrac{2qy}{(a^2+y^2)^{3/2}}\mathbf e_y$. For $|y|\gg a$, the pair details disappear and the field becomes that of a total point charge $2q$, in $1/y^2$. The superposition therefore preserves the near geometry and compresses it from afar.
