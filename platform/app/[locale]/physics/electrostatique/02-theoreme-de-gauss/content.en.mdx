## Intuition

Imagine the electric field as a flow: positive charges are **faucets**, negative ones are **drains**. Enclose a region of space in an imaginary bag (the *Gaussian surface*) and count the net flow coming out of the bag: it depends only on the faucets and drains *inside*. External sources bring the field in on one side and let it out on the other — net contribution zero.

This 'field flux' is called **flux**. Gauss's theorem makes it an exact equality: outgoing flux $=$ enclosed charge divided by $\varepsilon_0$.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Flux: $\Phi_E=\iint_S\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S$.
- Gauss: $\oiint_S\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S=Q_{\mathrm{enc}}/\varepsilon_0$.
- The theorem gives $E$ directly only if the symmetry makes its norm constant and its direction known on the chosen surface.

</KeyResults>

## Electric flux and statement of the theorem

The flux of the $\mathbf E$ field through a $S$ surface is

$$
\Phi_E=\iint_S\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S ,
$$

where $d\mathbf S = \widehat{\mathbf n}\,dS$ is the surface element oriented by its normal $\widehat{\mathbf n}$. The dot product only retains the component of the field that *passes through* the surface — a tangent field does not count. Unit: V·m.

**Gauss's theorem.** For any **closed** surface $S$ (normal oriented outward) :

$$
\boxed{\ \oiint_S\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S
=\frac{Q_{\mathrm{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}\ }
$$

- $Q_{\mathrm{enc}}$: **total charge enclosed** by $S$ — not the total system charge, not the "near" charge;
- The exact position of the *interior* charges does not change the flux; the *exterior* charges contribute to the local field but not to the total flux.

**When does the theorem really calculate $\mathbf E$?** Only when the symmetry fixes the *direction* of the field and makes its *magnitude* constant on the useful pieces of the surface — so that $\oiint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S$ factorizes into $E \times (\text{aire})$. Three symmetries are sufficient in practice: spherical, cylindrical (infinite), planar (infinite). Outside of these cases, Gauss remains true but only provides *global* information (a flux), not the field point by point: one must return to superposition.

## The three canonical results

### Uniformly charged sphere (radius $R$, density $\rho$, charge $Q$)

Spherical symmetry: $\mathbf E = E(r)\,\mathbf e_r$. Gauss surface: concentric sphere of radius $r$, area $4\pi r^2$.

In the **interior** ($r \le R$), the enclosed charge is $\rho\cdot\tfrac43\pi r^3$ :

$$
E(r)\,4\pi r^{2}=\frac{\rho\,\tfrac43\pi r^{3}}{\varepsilon_0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E(r)=\frac{\rho r}{3\varepsilon_0}
\qquad\text{(croît linéairement)}.
$$

In the **exterior** ($r \ge R$), $Q_{\mathrm{enc}} = Q$ :

$$
E(r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q}{r^{2}}
\qquad\text{(comme une charge ponctuelle au centre)}.
$$

Continuity check: in $r = R$, both expressions give $\rho R/3\varepsilon_0 = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2)$ (with $Q = \tfrac43\pi R^3\rho$). ✔

### Infinite wire (linear density $\lambda$)

Cylindrical symmetry (lesson 00): $\mathbf E = E(r)\,\mathbf e_r$, radial. Gaussian surface: coaxial cylinder of radius $r$ and height $h$. The flux exits only through the lateral surface ($2\pi r h$) — on the two disks, the field is tangent:

$$
E(r)\,2\pi rh=\frac{\lambda h}{\varepsilon_0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{\ E(r)=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\ }.
$$

Decrease in $1/r$ (and not $1/r^2$): the source is *infinite*, we never really 'move away' from it.

### Infinite plan (surface density $\sigma$)

Plane symmetry: field perpendicular to the plane, with a magnitude independent of the distance, inverted by reflection through the plane. Gaussian surface: "pillbox" crossing the plane, with a section $A$. The flow exits through the **two** faces:

$$
2\,E\,A=\frac{\sigma A}{\varepsilon_0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\boxed{\ E=\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\ }.
$$

**Uniform** field — surprising, but consistent: an infinite plane defines no length scale with which $E$ could vary.

## Worked example — unfold the complete method

Let's resume the infinite wire by writing as in an exam, with the symmetry–field–surface table required before any flux calculation:

**1. Symmetries of the source.** Translation along the axis, rotation around the axis, reflection by any plane containing the axis and by any plane perpendicular to it.

**2. Consequences on the field.** The observation point belongs to a plane containing the axis (symmetry → $\mathbf E$ in this plane) and to a plane perpendicular to the axis (symmetry → $\mathbf E$ in this plane as well). The intersection of the two planes is the radial direction: $\mathbf E = E(r)\,\mathbf e_r$, and $E$ depends only on $r$.

**3. Choice of the surface.** A coaxial cylinder follows this symmetry: field normal to the lateral face and of constant magnitude on the lateral face, tangent on the disks.

**4. Flow and conclusion.**

$$
\Phi_E = E(r)\cdot2\pi r h + 0 + 0 = \frac{\lambda h}{\varepsilon_0}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E(r)=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}.
$$

**5. Tests.** Units: $\lambda/(\varepsilon_0 r)$ = (C·m⁻¹)/(F·m⁻¹·m) = V·m⁻¹ ✔. The arbitrary height $h$ has been simplified ✔ (if it remains, the calculation is wrong). Direction: runs away from the wire if $\lambda$ is positive ✔.

## Local form of the theorem

<ProvenanceBadge provenance="reconstruction" />

By making the surface of Gauss tend towards a point, the theorem becomes an equation *local* linking the field to the charge density:

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} .
$$

This is the first Maxwell equation. High-confidence extension to the official program: it clarifies the meaning of divergence (lesson 00 — charges are the "taps" of the field) and will be used again with Poisson–Laplace in lesson 03.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — the spirit of Gauss's theorem for a point charge">

Let's place a charge $q$ at the center of a sphere with radius $r$. The Coulomb field is radial, with a magnitude of $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$, therefore

$$
\oiint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S
=\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^{2}}\times 4\pi r^{2}
=\frac{q}{\varepsilon_0}.
$$

The crucial point: the result **does not depend on $r$** — the decrease in $1/r^2$ of the field exactly compensates the increase in $r^2$ of the area. It is a *geometric* property of three-dimensional space.

For any closed surface around $q$, it is divided into small cones originating from the charge: each cone intercepts a flux that depends only on its solid angle, not on the shape of the surface — hence the same total $q/\varepsilon_0$. If $q$ is *external*, each cone pierces the surface an even number of times, with contributions that cancel out two by two: zero flux. The superposition then extends the result to any distribution: only $Q_{\mathrm{enc}}$ matters. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Connection to chemistry

The Gaussian sphere is the simplest model of **screening**. Seen from the outside, a spherical electronic cloud is equivalent to all its charge concentrated at the center: a valence electron "sees" the nucleus plus the inner shells as an effective charge $Z_{\mathrm{eff}}\,e$ — this is the electrostatic reading of Slater's rules. Conversely, in the *interior* of a uniformly charged spherical shell, the field of the shell is zero (no enclosed charge): the outer shells hardly shield the inner shells. The same 'enclosed charge' reasoning structures the models of ionized atmosphere (Debye–Hückel) around an ion in solution.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Choose a Gaussian surface "because it encloses the charge."** Enclosing the charge is not enough: the *symmetry* must make the flux calculable ($E$ constant and normal on the useful parts). Around a dipole, no surface allows this — Gauss's law is true but unusable for extracting $\mathbf E$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Replace the enclosed charge with the total charge.** Inside a charged sphere, only the charge within the radius $r$ counts: $Q_{\mathrm{enc}}(r) = \rho\cdot\tfrac43\pi r^3$, not $Q$. It is this mistake that causes the entire interior profile — and exercise P2.1 of TD 2 — to fail.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forget faces in the flux.** The pill box of the plan has **two** crossed faces (factor 2!); the wire cylinder has a lateral face *and* two disks (zero flux, but it must be *said*). Always inventory all faces and justify each contribution, even if zero.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believing that the field is zero inside any charged body.** This is true in a *conductor at equilibrium*, and inside a *spherical shell* — but in a uniformly charged solid sphere, $E = \rho r/3\varepsilon_0 \ne 0$. "Inside" does not mean "zero field": only the enclosed charge matters.

</Pitfall>

## Checks

- **Units**: $Q_{\mathrm{enc}}/\varepsilon_0$ is in C/(F·m⁻¹) = V·m — a flux ✔. And $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$, $\lambda/(\varepsilon_0 r)$, $\rho r/\varepsilon_0$ are all in V·m⁻¹ ✔.
- **Extreme limits**: the sphere seen from afar restores the point charge; the inner and outer profiles must connect in $r = R$; the field of the plane does not depend on distance (no length scale).
- **Counting of decreases**: point source → $1/r^2$; infinite line source → $1/r$; infinite plane source → constant. One more dimension of invariance, one less power of $r$.

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- Flux: $\Phi_E = \iint_S\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S$; Gauss's theorem:
  $\oiint_S\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf S = Q_{\mathrm{enc}}/\varepsilon_0$
  (closed surface, outward normal).
- A calculation tool **only** if the symmetry fixes the direction and makes $E$ constant on the surface: spherical, infinite cylindrical, infinite plane.
- Uniform sphere: $E = \dfrac{\rho r}{3\varepsilon_0}$ inside, $E = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$ outside, continuous matching at $R$.
- Infinite wire: $E = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}$ ; infinite plane: $E = \dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$ (uniform, factor 2 from the two faces).
- Local form (extension): $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$.
- Expected solution structure: symmetries → field direction → adapted surface → inventory of all faces → flux → tests.

</KeyResults>
