# Filling the pipe without overflowing it — windows, RTT and RTO

<LessonMeta id="cs-net-c12" />

<Toc depth={2} />

<Accroche>
Between a sender in a hurry and a receiver that can overflow, TCP permanently negotiates two numbers: how many bytes in flight, and how long to wait before worrying.
</Accroche>

<Mission fil="Exercise 5 of TD 6 (tutorial sheet) — a window too small for a T3 link">
**Situation.** A TCP connection crosses a **T3 link at 44.736 Mbit/s**
with a round trip of **50 ms**. The window field of the TCP header is
coded on **16 bits** — at best 65,536 bytes in flight. **Question.** What
proportion of its time does the sender spend **blocked**, waiting for
acknowledgements instead of sending — and what would have to change to
fill this link? **The obstacle.** The intuition "big rate = fast
transfer" collapses here: the bottleneck is neither the link nor the
receiver, but a 16-bit field inherited from 1981 — and it still has to be
**proved** by calculation. **The contract.** This chapter gives you 3
tools: the sliding window and its flow control, the bound
rate = window/RTT with the bandwidth-delay product, and the adaptive
timer (SRTT, Jacobson, Karn). At the end, we put a number on the
blocking — 76.6% — and on its remedy.
</Mission>

*Already comfortable with this? [Go straight to TD 6](../td-6/), exercises
4 to 8.*

## The battle plan

To pilot the bytes in flight, we need 3 tools:

1. **the sliding window** — how many bytes may fly, and who sets the limit;
2. **the window/RTT bound** — a connection's maximum rate, and the bandwidth-delay product that sizes everything;
3. **the adaptive timer** — measuring the RTT, smoothing it, distrusting it (Jacobson, Karn).

## 1. The sliding window — flow control

Problem: lesson 11 acknowledges byte by byte, but never said **how many**
bytes the sender may send without a reply. Too few: the link sleeps.
Too many: the receiver overflows. The limit must come from the receiver itself.

**Definition 1 (sliding window).** The <Def id="fenetre-glissante">**sliding
window (TCP)**</Def> is the range of bytes the sender is allowed
to have "in flight" (sent, unacknowledged). Each arriving
<Terme id="acquittement-cumulatif">cumulative acknowledgement</Terme>
slides the window to the right: the validated bytes
free their places. It is the <Terme id="fenetre-d-anticipation">sliding
window (link layer)</Terme> of lesson 06 — recounted in bytes.

**Definition 2 (flow control).** <Def id="controle-de-flux">**flow
control**</Def> keeps the sender from drowning the **receiver**: the size
of the window is dictated by the space left in the receive buffer
— not by the state of the network (that is congestion, lesson 13).

**Definition 3 (advertised window).** The <Def id="fenetre-annoncee">**advertised
window (rwnd)**</Def> — the window field of every segment — publishes that
remaining space. On the sender's side the discipline reads, with the
course's names (*FenêtreUtilisable* the usable window, *DernierÉmis* and
*DernierAcquitté* the last bytes sent and acknowledged):

$$
\text{FenêtreUtilisable} = \text{rwnd} - (\text{DernierÉmis} - \text{DernierAcquitté}),
$$

and on the receiver's side (*TailleBuffer* the buffer size, *DernierReçu*
and *DernierLu* the last bytes received and read):

$$
\text{rwnd} = \text{TailleBuffer} - (\text{DernierReçu} - \text{DernierLu}).
$$

**Example 1.** An 8 KB receive buffer, a slow application: 6 KB received
but unread → rwnd = 2 KB. The sender, which already has 1 KB in flight,
can push only 1 KB more — whatever the link's rate.

**Remark (the zero window and the probe).** If rwnd falls to 0, the sender
goes quiet — but an ACK reopening the window can get lost, and nobody
would ever speak again. So TCP probes periodically with a
**1-byte** segment: the reply carries the rwnd of the moment. A block
without a probe is a deadlock — a classic course question.

### Mini-exercise

1. DernierAcquitté = 5000, DernierÉmis = 6200, advertised rwnd = 4000. How many more bytes can the sender push?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $4000 - (6200 - 5000) = 2800$ bytes (Definition 3). The 1200 in flight
   count against the window as long as they are unacknowledged.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Window / RTT — the bound that governs everything

Problem: the window limits the bytes **per round trip** — so it
limits the rate, even on an infinite link. We need this bound as a formula;
it is what three TD exercises test.

**Definition 4 (RTT).** The <Def id="rtt">**RTT**</Def> (Round Trip Time)
is the time between sending a segment and the return of its
acknowledgement — outbound and return propagation, transmissions, queues
and processing included.

**Proposition 1 (a connection's maximum rate).** A connection with
window $W$ (bytes) and round trip RTT cannot exceed

$$
D_{\max} = \frac{W \times 8}{\text{RTT}} \quad \text{bit/s}.
$$

**Why?** The sender pushes at most $W$ bytes, then waits for the
first acknowledgement — which cannot come back before one RTT. At best,
then, $W$ bytes flow per RTT slice; the link's rate does not even appear
in the bound. $\blacksquare$

**Definition 5 (bandwidth-delay product).** The
<Def id="produit-delai-bande-passante">**bandwidth-delay
product**</Def> (BDP) of a path, $\text{RTT} \times D_{\text{lien}}$,
is the volume of data that **fills the pipe**: the minimum window
for sending without ever stopping. The full-throughput condition reads
both ways (*le lien ne dort jamais*: the link never sleeps):

$$
W \ge \text{RTT} \times D_{\text{lien}} \iff \text{le lien ne dort jamais}.
$$

**Example 2 (TD exercise 6).** Window 64 KB, RTT 300 ms, link
10 Mbit/s: $D_{\max} = 65\,536 \times 8 / 0{,}3 = 1{,}75$ Mbit/s — and
moving the access rate to 100 Mbit/s **changes nothing**: the bound comes
from $W$/RTT, not from the link.

**Definition 6 (window scale factor).** The <Def id="facteur-d-echelle">**window
scale factor**</Def> (the window scale option, negotiated in the
<Terme id="ouverture-en-trois-temps">three-way handshake</Terme>) shifts the window by $n$
bits ($n \le 16$): it is read as $\text{champ} \times 2^n$ (*champ*: the
field's value), taking the maximum from $2^{16}$ to $2^{32}$ bytes —
enough to cover modern BDPs.

**Example 3 (exercise 6, continued).** BDP $= 10^7 \times 0{,}3 = 3$ Mbit
$= 375$ KB. With $n = 2$: window $2^{18} = 262\,144$ bytes $< 375$ KB →
7 Mbit/s (70% of the link). With $n \ge 3$: $2^{19} = 524\,288 \ge 375$ KB →
the link is full. The TD's model answer runs exactly this case-by-case.

## 3. The adaptive timer — SRTT, Jacobson, Karn

Problem: loss recovery (lesson 11) rests on a timer —
but setting its duration is a dilemma: too short, you retransmit for
nothing; too long, you mourn losses in silence. And the RTT **varies** —
from path to path, from hour to hour.

**Definition 7 (RTO).** The <Def id="rto">**RTO**</Def> (Retransmission
TimeOut) is the duration of the retransmission timer, continuously
recomputed from the measured RTTs. The historical recipe (RFC 793): a
smoothed mean

$$
\text{SRTT} = \alpha \cdot \text{SRTT} + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \text{RTT}_{\text{mesuré}},
\qquad \alpha \approx 0{,}8 \text{ à } 0{,}9,
$$

then $\text{RTO} = \min(\text{borne haute},\ \max(\text{borne basse},\ \beta \cdot \text{SRTT}))$ with $\beta \approx 2$ (*borne haute/basse*: upper/lower bound).

**Example 4 (TD exercise 4).** SRTT = 30 ms, $\alpha = 0{,}9$, three
measurements: 26, 32, 24 ms.
$0{,}9 \times 30 + 0{,}1 \times 26 = 29{,}6$;
$0{,}9 \times 29{,}6 + 0{,}1 \times 32 = 29{,}84$;
$0{,}9 \times 29{,}84 + 0{,}1 \times 24 = 29{,}256$ ms. The mean barely
moves: α close to 1 gives a long memory — insensitive to one unlucky
segment, slow to follow a real change; α close to 0, the opposite.

**Remark (Jacobson: adding the variance).** A fixed β ignores the
**spread** of the RTTs: on a jittery path, 2 × SRTT fires wrongly.
Van Jacobson's improvement also estimates the mean deviation $D$:

$$
D = \alpha D + (1-\alpha)\,\lvert \text{SRTT} - \text{RTT}_{\text{mesuré}} \rvert ,
\qquad \text{RTO} = \text{SRTT} + 4D .
$$

The factor 4 is experimental; the idea is that the margin follows the
path's jitter instead of being a blind percentage.

**Definition 8 (Karn's algorithm).** <Def id="algorithme-de-karn">**Karn's
algorithm**</Def> settles the ambiguity of retransmissions: when a segment
has been resent, its acknowledgement does not say whether it answers the
original or the copy — the measurement would be wrong either way. Karn
imposes: **never measure the RTT on a retransmitted segment**, and
**double the RTO** on every retransmission (exponential backoff), until
the next clean measurement.

### Mini-exercise

1. A segment is retransmitted, and its ACK comes back 10 ms after the copy. May SRTT be fed with 10 ms?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No (Definition 8): if the ACK answers the **original**, the true RTT is
   much longer; if it answers the copy, 10 ms is right — but there is no
   way to know. Karn throws the measurement away and doubles the RTO in
   the meantime.

   </Collapsible>

## Worked example — the T3 link, blocked 76.6% of the time

Let us unroll the mission: T3 = 44.736 Mbit/s, RTT = 50 ms, a 16-bit
window (the model answer takes $2^{16} = 65\,536$ bytes).

**Step 1 — the time to send one full window.**

$$
t_W = \frac{65\,536 \times 8}{44{,}736 \times 10^6} = 11{,}7 \text{ ms}.
$$

*Why?* It is a <Terme id="temps-de-transmission">transmission
time</Terme> (lesson 02) applied to the whole window — the only
thing the sender is allowed to push in one go (Definition 1).

**Step 2 — the waiting cycle.** After its 11.7 ms of sending, the sender
has exhausted its window; the first acknowledgement comes back at the
earliest at RTT = 50 ms. So it stays blocked $50 - 11{,}7 = 38{,}3$ ms per cycle:

$$
\text{fraction bloquée} = 1 - \frac{11{,}7}{50} = 76{,}6\ \%.
$$

*Why?* Proposition 1 seen in time: W bytes per RTT, and the rest of the
cycle is silence. The useful rate is only
$65\,536 \times 8 / 0{,}05 = 10{,}5$ Mbit/s on a link that offers 44.7.

**Step 3 — the diagnosis in one number.** The path's BDP is

$$
44{,}736 \times 10^6 \times 0{,}05 = 2{,}24 \text{ Mbit} \approx 280 \text{ Ko}.
$$

*Why?* Definition 5: 280 KB would need to be in flight to fill the
pipe — the 16-bit window offers 65: it is 4.3 times too
small, and no sending trick will change that.

**Step 4 — the remedy.** The window scale option with $n = 3$:
$65\,536 \times 2^3 = 524\,288$ bytes $\ge 280$ KB → the sender never
waits again; $n = 2$ (262 KB) would not quite be enough.

*Why?* Definition 6: choose the smallest $n$ such that
$2^{16+n} \ge$ BDP — the exact reasoning of exercise 6's model answer,
transposed to the T3.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**Would the problem have changed if the receiver had a one-gigabyte
buffer?**

No: the bottleneck is not the space at the receiver but the **field** that
announces it — 16 bits cannot say more than 65,535 (Definition 3). A
big buffer without a scale factor announces 65,535, full stop. That is the
whole difference between a resource limit and a protocol limit.

</Collapsible>

## Guided visualisation — the efficiency table

No simulator here: the bound is best seen in a table you can
recompute row by row (window × 8 / RTT, then compare with the link).

| Window | Max rate (RTT 50 ms) | Share of the T3 (44.7 Mbit/s) | Sender blocked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 16 KB | 2.6 Mbit/s | 6% | 94% |
| 64 KB | 10.5 Mbit/s | 23% | 76.6% |
| 128 KB | 21 Mbit/s | 47% | 53% |
| 280 KB (BDP) | 44.7 Mbit/s | 100% | 0% |
| 512 KB | 44.7 Mbit/s (link ceiling) | 100% | 0% |

- **Predict** before reading the last row: what do you gain by announcing
  512 KB rather than 280 KB? — **Observe**: nothing: beyond the BDP, the
  link is the bound (Definition 5); the window stops being the limiting
  factor, and the surplus only serves to absorb jolts.
- **Predict**: what happens to the "max rate" column if the RTT doubles? —
  **Observe**: it is halved, row by row (Proposition 1) —
  while the BDP doubles: long links demand large windows
  twice over.

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the ×8.** The window is counted in **bytes**, rates in
bits: $65\,536 \times 8$ first (Step 1). The mistake divides every
result by 8 — and plausibility gives it away: 1.3 Mbit/s on a
T3 should have alerted you.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Improving the rate by speeding up the link.** As long as $W <$ BDP, the
connection is bounded by W/RTT (Proposition 1): TD exercise 6 sets
exactly this trap by moving the access from 10 to 100 Mbit/s — answer:
nothing changes.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Measuring the RTT on a retransmission.** The ACK is ambiguous (Definition 8):
Karn throws the measurement away and doubles the RTO. Feeding SRTT with
that value skews the timer in the dangerous direction — too short → a
storm of retransmissions.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confusing flow control with congestion control.** Flow protects
the **receiver** (rwnd, Definition 2); congestion protects the
**network** (cwnd, lesson 13). TCP obeys both at once:
real window = min(rwnd, cwnd) — the next lesson starts there.

</Pitfall>

## Summary — what you can do now

<KeyResults title="The three numbers of a connection">

| If the paper gives… | You compute… | Tool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a buffer and bytes in flight | usable window = rwnd − (sent − acked) | Definition 3 |
| W and RTT | max rate = W × 8 / RTT — the link does not appear | Proposition 1 |
| a link and an RTT | BDP = RTT × D: the full-throughput window | Definition 5 |
| "which scale factor?" | smallest n such that 2^(16+n) ≥ BDP | Definition 6 |
| RTT measurements | smoothed SRTT (α), then RTO = SRTT + 4D (Jacobson) | §3, Example 4 |
| a retransmission | no measurement, RTO doubled (Karn) | Definition 8 |

**The reflex that saves you:** facing any TCP rate exercise, compute
the BDP first — it says immediately which of the window or the link is
the bottleneck.

</KeyResults>
