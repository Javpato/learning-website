# Making a wire reliable — acknowledgements, windows and HDLC

Between two machines joined by an imperfect cable, everything rests on a dialogue of book-keepers: I number, you acknowledge receipt, and at the slightest gap we replay.

<Mission fil="Exercise 2 of the one-hour partiel (midterm) — error recovery, stopwatch in hand">
**Situation.** Two stations talk to each other at the same time over the
same link. A must send **4 blocks** numbered 0 to 3, B must send **2**;
each block (data or service) occupies the line for **3T**; A starts at
$t_0$, B at $t_0 + 4T$; travel times are negligible. On the way, **A's
block no. 2 arrives damaged** at B. **Question.** Draw the complete
exchange under the two proposed recovery strategies — replay everything
from the error, or replay only the faulty block — and compare: how many
blocks are sent in total, and when does each exchange end?
**The obstacle.** You must know *when* B notices the problem (a damaged
block does not even state its number), *what* to resend, and how the
confirmations circulate while both stations are transmitting at the same

acknowledgement-timer-retransmission trio, the sliding window, and

## The battle plan

To make the link reliable, we need 4 tools:

1. **the frame** — delimiting verifiable blocks in the stream of bits (with the transparency trick);
2. **the ARQ trio** — acknowledgement, timer, retransmission: the loop that catches losses;
3. **the sliding window** — sending several frames without waiting, without losing count;
4. **HDLC and its two rejects** — the standardised protocol, its I/S/U frames, and the REJ and SREJ recoveries.

## 1. The frame — delimit in order to verify

Problem: the physical layer delivers a stream of bits with no beginning
and no end. To apply a <Terme id="crc">CRC</Terme>, you must know
**where** the block to verify starts and finishes.

**Definition 1 (frame).** A <Def id="trame">**frame**</Def> is the unit
of the data-link layer: a **delimited** block of bits (by flags, by
physical-layer markers or by a length counter), carrying a header
(addresses, numbers), the data, and an error-check field.

Problem: if the flag is the pattern `01111110`, nothing stops the data
from containing it by chance — and cutting the frame in two.

**Definition 2 (transparency).** <Def id="transparence">**Transparency
(bit stuffing)**</Def> requires the sender to insert a **0 after five
consecutive 1s** of the data; the receiver removes any 0 that follows
five 1s. Thus `01111110` can now appear only at the boundaries — the
data become free again, "transparent".

**Example 1.** Data `0110111111011111 1…`: the sender transmits
`011011111`**0**`1011111`**0**`1…` — two 0s inserted, which the receiver
will know how to remove without ambiguity.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. The ARQ trio — acknowledge, time out, retransmit

Problem: lesson 05 knows how to **denounce** a damaged frame — but
denouncing repairs nothing. The second chance has to be organised.

**Definition 3 (acknowledgement).** An <Def id="acquittement">**acknowledgement**</Def>
(ACK, or RR "Receive Ready" in HDLC) is a short message from the
receiver confirming what has arrived safely — by convention, it carries
the number of the **next** frame expected.

**Definition 4 (timer).** The <Def id="temporisateur">**timer**</Def> is
a countdown armed when each frame is sent: if it expires before the
acknowledgement, the sender assumes the frame (or its acknowledgement)
lost and retransmits.

**Definition 5 (ARQ).** An <Def id="arq">**ARQ**</Def> protocol
(Automatic Repeat reQuest) combines frame numbering, acknowledgements,
timers and retransmissions to make **reliable** a link that is not. Its
minimal version, **stop-and-wait**, sends one frame then waits for its
acknowledgement before the next.

**Example 2.** Why number, even in stop-and-wait? If the
**acknowledgement** is lost, the sender retransmits a frame the
receiver already holds: without a number, the duplicate cannot be
recognised — "reliability" would deliver data twice.

**Proposition 1 (efficiency of stop-and-wait).** On a link with
transmission time $t_t$ per frame and propagation delay $t_p$ (short
acknowledgement), the sender is active only

$$
U = \frac{t_t}{t_t + 2\,t_p}
$$

of the time.

**Why?** Each cycle lasts $t_t$ (pushing the frame) $+\;t_p$ (it
travels) $+\;t_p$ (the acknowledgement comes back) — and only the first
part transmits. On a long or fast link ($t_p \gg t_t$), $U$ collapses.
$\blacksquare$

**Remark (blind spot).** The formula neglects the acknowledgement's
transmission time and the processing — the mission's past paper, for its
part, counts 3T for **every** frame, data or service: always read the
statement's assumptions before applying a formula.

### Mini-exercise

1. Satellite link: t_t = 1 ms, t_p = 125 ms. Efficiency of stop-and-wait?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $U = 1/(1+250) \approx 0{,}4\ \%$. Waiting for each acknowledgement
   wastes 99.6% of the link — hence the next tool.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. The sliding window — sending without waiting

Problem: Proposition 1 condemns "one frame at a time". Several frames
must be sent ahead — without losing track of who is acknowledged.

**Definition 6 (sliding window).** The
<Def id="fenetre-d-anticipation">**sliding window (link layer)**</Def>
is the maximum number of frames a sender may have transmitted **without
an acknowledgement**. Each arriving acknowledgement makes the window
"slide" and frees a place; window full, the sender goes silent.

**Example 3.** A window of 4 on the mini-exercise's satellite link: the
sender strings together 4 frames (4 ms) then waits; the efficiency is
multiplied by 4. A window wide enough to cover the round trip fills the
link continuously — the idea TCP will reuse to the byte (lesson 12).

**Remark.** The window also bounds the numbering: with numbers on $n$
bits, the window must stay $< 2^n$ (in go-back-N) so that a reused
number is never ambiguous. Exercise cs-net-td2-05 lets you touch the
blocking with your own hands.

## 4. HDLC — the protocol, and its two ways of replaying

Problem: it remains to standardise all this — formats, counters,
recoveries — so that any two devices understand each other.

**Definition 7 (HDLC).** <Def id="hdlc">**HDLC**</Def> (High-level Data
Link Control) is the standardised link protocol: **I** frames
(Information: numbered data), **S** frames (Supervision: RR, REJ, SREJ)
and **U** frames (Unnumbered: management — SABM opens the link, UA
confirms). An I frame carries **two counters**: N(S), its own number,
and N(R), the next frame expected from the other side — written
I N(S),N(R), for instance `I20` for "my frame 2, I expect your frame 0".

**Definition 8 (piggybacking).** <Def id="piggybacking">**Piggybacking**</Def>
consists in letting the acknowledgement travel **inside** the N(R) field
of an I frame that was leaving in the other direction anyway — instead
of paying for a separate RR frame. That is what the mission does: B
acknowledges A without ever sending an RR.

Problem: when an I frame arrives damaged, the receiver cannot even read
its number. It only detects the gap when the **next** frame arrives, out
of sequence — and it then has two strategies.

**Definition 9 (go-back-N reject).** The <Def id="rejet-simple">**go-back-N
reject (REJ)**</Def> (REJ n) asks again for frame $n$ **and everything
that follows it**: the receiver discards whatever arrives out of
sequence, the sender replays from $n$. Simple, but cascading
retransmissions.

**Definition 10 (selective reject).** The <Def id="rejet-selectif">**selective
reject (SREJ)**</Def> (SREJ n) asks again **only** for frame $n$: the
receiver keeps in memory the following frames, correct but out of
sequence, and stitches everything together when $n$ arrives. Fewer
retransmissions, more memory.

**Method (the exam timing diagram).** The rules of the game, as the past
paper applies them:
1. each station sends its I frames **back to back**, as soon as its
   transmit line is free, labelling I N(S),N(R) — N(R) being its receive
   counter **at the moment of sending**;
2. a damaged frame is **ignored** (unreadable: neither REJ nor SREJ at
   that instant);
3. the gap is detected on the complete arrival of the **first
   out-of-sequence frame**; the receiver then sends REJ n or SREJ n as
   soon as its line is free;
4. on receiving the reject, the sender replays — everything from $n$
   (REJ) or only $n$ (SREJ) — as soon as its current transmission ends.

## Worked example — the past paper's two timing diagrams

Let us unroll the mission (3T frames, zero propagation, B starts at 4T,
A's frame 2 arrives damaged), first in **go-back-N reject**.

**Step 1 — cruising speed.** A sends `I00` (0→3T), `I10` (3→6),
`I20` (6→9, **damaged**), `I31` (9→12). B sends `I01` (4→7) and `I12` (7→10).

*Why?* Step 1 of the Method: each side strings frames as soon as its
line frees up; the N(R)s photograph the reception at each frame's
departure — A moves to N(R)=1 from 9T because `I01` arrived at 7T; B,
who received `I00` before sending, starts at `I01`.

**Step 2 — the error goes unnoticed… for a while.** At 9T, B receives
an unreadable block: it ignores it. Nothing else happens.

*Why?* Step 2: a damaged frame carries no usable number
— B cannot yet ask for anything again.

**Step 3 — detection, at 12T.** `I31` arrives complete at 12T: number 3
while B expected 2 → out of sequence. B sends `REJ2` (12→15T).

*Why?* Step 3: it is the **next** frame that betrays the gap. B
had finished `I12` at 10T, its line is free: the reject leaves at once.

**Step 4 — replay everything from 2.** A receives `REJ2` at 15T and
replays `I22` (15→18) **then** `I32` (18→21) — N(R)=2 from now on,
because `I12` has arrived.

*Why?* Definition 9: REJ 2 = "number 2 and everything after it". Frame
3, though it arrived safely a first time, leaves again — that is the
price of go-back-N. End of the exchange: **21T**, with 9 frames sent in
total (6 from A, 2 from B, 1 REJ).

**Step 5 — the SREJ variant.** Same first four frames; at 12T, B sends
`SREJ2` (12→15); A replays **only** `I22` (15→18) — B had kept `I31` in
memory and stitches 2 then 3 back together. End: **18T**, 8 frames sent.

*Why?* Definition 10: only the gap is replayed. The gain — 3T and one
frame — would grow with the number of frames sent after the error: that
is the whole point of the REJ/SREJ choice.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**Why does B not send its reject as early as 10T, at the end of `I12`?**

Because at 10T, B still **knows** nothing: the damaged frame received
at 9T is unreadable (step 2), and nothing out of sequence has arrived.
The proof of the gap only exists at 12T, when `I31` shows up with a
number ahead of time (step 3). A receiver never rejects on a hunch —
only on a complete out-of-sequence frame.

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Having the "damaged frame" asked for by its number.** B cannot read a
damaged frame — not even its number. Detection waits for the first
**out-of-sequence** frame (Method, steps 2–3): on the past paper, that
is 3T later, and this delay is part of the marking scheme.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**REJ n = replay only n.** No: REJ n replays n **and everything after
it** (Definition 9) — on the past paper, `I31`, though well received,
leaves again anyway. Replaying only n is SREJ, and it is paid for in
memory on the receiver's side.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Reading N(R) as "last frame received".** N(R) is the **next
expected** — so "everything before it is validated". B's `I12` means
"my frame 1, and I already have your 0 and 1". A one-unit offset here
falsifies the whole timing diagram.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting that the window blocks.** "The window is assumed
sufficient", says the past paper — when a statement does not say so,
check: window full, the sender stays silent until the next
acknowledgement (Definition 6), and the timing diagram changes
completely (cs-net-td2-05).

</Pitfall>

## Summary — what you can do now

<KeyResults title="The HDLC timing diagram in golden rules">

| Situation | Rule | Tool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| labelling an I frame | I N(S),N(R) — N(R) = next expected at the moment of sending | Definition 7 |
| damaged frame received | ignore it: unreadable, undetectable on the spot | Method s.2 |
| out-of-sequence frame received | send REJ n (replay everything) or SREJ n (replay n alone) | Definitions 9–10 |
| no reverse traffic | the acknowledgement travels as RR; the loss is caught by the timer | Definitions 3–4 |
| long or fast link | sliding window ≈ cover the round trip | Definition 6, Prop. 1 |
| data containing 111110… | transparency: a 0 inserted after five 1s | Definition 2 |

**The question to ask at every frame of the timing diagram:** "what does
this station *know* at this instant?" — everything else follows from it.

</KeyResults>
