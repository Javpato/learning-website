# Talking process to process — ports, UDP and the TCP connection

<LessonMeta id="cs-net-c11" />

<Toc depth={2} />

<Accroche>
IP delivers parcels to houses; transport hands them to the right person — and TCP, on top of that, numbers every byte so no sentence ever arrives out of order.
</Accroche>

<Mission fil="Exercise 7 of TD 6 (tutorial sheet) — sequence numbers, to the byte">
**Situation.** A TCP connection is established (maximum segment size:
512 bytes). A's application pushes **300, 200 then 12 bytes** in
succession, shipped in three segments whose first bytes carry the
numbers **1024, 1324 and 1524**. The **first segment is lost** en route;
the other two arrive. **Question.** Which acknowledgement numbers does B
send back at each arrival, what does A do when its timer
expires — number, size and content of the recovery segment — and which
final acknowledgement closes the incident? **The obstacle.** Everything
turns on precise arithmetic: TCP numbers **bytes**, not messages, its
acknowledgements speak of the **future** (the next byte expected), and the
recovery glues everything back in one block. **The contract.** This
chapter gives you 5 tools: ports, UDP, the TCP segment, SEQ/ACK
numbering, and the three-way handshake. At the end, we fill in the TD's
timeline, number by number.
</Mission>

## The battle plan

To talk process to process, we need 5 tools:

1. **ports** — the (de)multiplexing that tells apart the applications of one machine;
2. **UDP** — the minimal transport: ports, a checksum, nothing else;
3. **the TCP segment** — the format that carries everything else;
4. **SEQ/ACK numbering** — bytes counted one by one, cumulative acknowledgements;
5. **the three-way handshake** — why three, and why the counter never starts from zero.

## 1. Ports — several conversations per machine

Problem: IP brings the datagram to the **machine** (lessons 08–10) —
but ten applications run on it. Whom to deliver to?

**Definition 1 (port).** A <Def id="port">**port**</Def> is a
**16-bit** number identifying an application (a communication point) on
a machine. Ports **0–1023** are reserved for well-known services:
21 (FTP), 23 (Telnet), 25 (SMTP), **53 (DNS)**, **80 (HTTP)**; above that,
registered ports, then free ones for the clients.

**Definition 2 ((de)multiplexing).** <Def id="multiplexage">**(de)multiplexing**</Def>
is transport's basic job: on sending, let every application share
IP; on receiving, read the destination port and hand the message
**to the right process**.

**Definition 3 (socket).** A <Def id="socket">**socket**</Def> is a
communication endpoint, the pair (IP address, port). A TCP connection
is identified by the **quadruple** of the two sockets — (IP A, port A,
IP B, port B): two browsers on the same web server differ by
their client port, and the server never mixes them up.

**Remark.** The port is the transport layer's <Terme id="sap">SAP</Terme>
— the access door promised by lesson 03, concrete at last.

### Mini-exercise

1. Two tabs of your browser load the same site. What distinguishes the two connections?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The **client** port: same local IP, same server (IP, 80), but two
   different source ports — two quadruples, hence two watertight
   connections (Definition 3).

   </Collapsible>

## 2. UDP — the minimal transport

Problem: some traffic (a DNS question, a shot in a game) just wants to
reach the right process, **fast** — no connection, no recovery.

**Definition 4 (UDP).** <Def id="udp">**UDP**</Def> (User Datagram
Protocol) extends IP's service into a **process-to-process** service, and
that is all: an 8-byte header — source port, destination port,
length, checksum — with no connection, no reliability, no flow or
congestion control. What the TD calls "UDP's only function: the
(de)multiplexing".

**Definition 5 (pseudo-header).** UDP's checksum (optional in IPv4,
mandatory in IPv6) is computed over the datagram **preceded by a
pseudo-header**: a fictitious <Def id="pseudo-en-tete">**pseudo-header**</Def>
holding the IP addresses, the protocol and the length — never
transmitted, only summed. It ties the content to its addresses: a datagram
delivered to the wrong machine fails the checksum.

**Remark (blind spot).** "UDP is faster" does not mean
"better": any UDP application that needs reliability must
**rebuild it itself** (timers, numbers — exercise
cs-net-td6-10 walks all round the problem). UDP is a choice, not a shortcut.

### Mini-exercise

1. Why does DNS choose UDP for its questions?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   One question, one answer, often in a single round trip: setting up a
   connection would cost more than the exchange itself. On a loss, the
   client simply asks again — minimal reliability is easier to rebuild
   than to rent.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. The TCP segment — the format that carries everything

Problem: at the other extreme, the web, mail, files want a
**reliable, ordered byte stream** on top of an IP that loses, duplicates
and reorders. The tool: a rich header, with a mechanism behind
every field.

**Definition 6 (TCP).** <Def id="tcp">**TCP**</Def> (Transmission Control
Protocol) offers a **reliable** byte stream, in **connection-oriented**
mode, point-to-point and simultaneously two-way, with error, flow and
congestion control. The application writes bytes; TCP cuts them up,
numbers them, has them acknowledged — and the application opposite reads
the same bytes, in order.

**Definition 7 (TCP segment).** The <Def id="segment-tcp">**TCP
segment**</Def> (TPDU, in the TD's OSI vocabulary) is TCP's control
unit — a 20-byte header:

| Field | Role |
| --- | --- |
| source, destination ports | the multiplexing (Definition 2) |
| **sequence number** | number of the segment's first data byte |
| **acknowledgement number** | next byte expected from the other direction |
| header length | in 32-bit words (options included) |
| flags | **SYN** (open), **ACK** (acknowledgement number valid), **FIN** (end of sending), RST, PSH, URG |
| **window** | flow control (lesson 12) |
| checksum | over the whole segment + pseudo-header (Definition 5) |
| options | including the MSS negotiation |

The **roles** of a segment (open, acknowledge, carry, close) are
told apart by its **flags** — a ritual TD question.

**Definition 8 (MSS).** The <Def id="mss">**MSS**</Def> (Maximum Segment
Size) is the largest data load of a segment, negotiated at
opening — typically <Terme id="mtu">MTU</Terme> − 40 (20 of IP + 20
of TCP), to avoid <Terme id="fragmentation">fragmentation</Terme>.
A segment leaves when the MSS is full, when the application pushes
(PSH flag), or on a timer.

### Mini-exercise

1. What is the OSI name for the TCP segment, and why does TCP cut into segments a service that is a byte stream?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The **TPDU** (Transport PDU). The stream is the **service** rendered to
   the application; the segment is the internal **control unit** — it is
   what checksums, numbers and retransmissions operate on
   (questions 2.4 and 2.5 of TD 6).

   </Collapsible>

## 4. SEQ and ACK — counting bytes, not segments

Problem: to glue a stream back together from lost, duplicated or
reordered segments, you need a reference immune to the cutting. TCP picks
the finest one possible: the byte.

**Definition 9 (sequence number).** The <Def id="numero-de-sequence">**sequence
number**</Def> (SEQ) of a segment is the number, within the stream, of its
**first data byte**. A segment SEQ = 1024 carrying 300 bytes
covers bytes 1024 to 1323 — the next will leave at 1324.

**Definition 10 (cumulative acknowledgement).** The <Def id="acquittement-cumulatif">**cumulative
acknowledgement**</Def> (ACK field) announces the **next byte expected**:
everything before it is received, in one go. After receiving bytes
1024–1323, B replies ACK = 1324. The acknowledgement travels in the
segments of the reverse traffic — the <Terme id="piggybacking">piggybacking</Terme>
of lesson 06, generalised.

**Example 1 (the course's own).** A sends SEQ = 25 with 5 bytes (25 to
29): B replies ACK = 30. Always "last received + 1" — that is, the
first byte that is missing.

**Remark.** Cumulative = robust but terse: if bytes
1324–1523 arrive while 1024–1323 are missing, B **repeats** ACK = 1024
(it cannot "skip" the hole). These duplicate acknowledgements are
precious information — lesson 13 will turn them into a loss detector.

## 5. Opening in three steps — and never counting from zero

Problem: on a network that reorders and lets old datagrams wander,
"hello? — yes!" is not enough to open cleanly: an
old delayed SYN could reopen a dead conversation, and data
could arrive before the agreement.

**Definition 11 (three-way handshake).** The <Def id="ouverture-en-trois-temps">**three-way
handshake**</Def>: A sends **SYN, SEQ = x**;
B replies **SYN, SEQ = y, ACK = x + 1**; A concludes **ACK = y + 1**. Each
side picks its initial number and proves it has seen the other's;
SYN consumes one sequence number — hence the "+ 1"s.

**Example 2 (the course's own).** SYN 78 → SYN 1005, ACK 79 → ACK 1006. The
connection is open for A on receiving the 2nd segment, for B on
receiving the 3rd — never "at the same time", and that is normal.

**Definition 12 (ISN).** The <Def id="isn">**ISN**</Def> (Initial Sequence
Number) is that starting number, drawn from a **clock** (a counter
incremented every 4 µs, wrapping in ≈ 4.55 h) — never zero, and
different for every connection: late segments from a previous connection
between the same ports would carry implausible numbers,
and will be rejected. After closing, TCP in fact stays in quarantine
(the TIME_WAIT state) for **2 × MSL** (two maximum segment
lifetimes, ≈ 120 s) to let the stragglers die.

**Remark (closing, in four steps).** Each direction closes
independently: FIN from A, ACK from B — B may still send — then FIN from
B, ACK from A. Four segments, two half-closes: the full duplex of
Definition 6 right into death.

### Mini-exercise

1. Why are two steps ("SYN" then "ACK") not enough?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because an **old** wandering **SYN** can arrive on its own: in two
   steps, B would open a connection A never wanted, and ghost data would
   follow. The third step forces A to confirm it has seen B's **fresh**
   reply (ACK = y + 1) — an old SYN will never manage to produce that
   particular y. (Question 2.2 of TD 6, the model answer's argument.)

   </Collapsible>

## Worked example — TD 6, number by number

Let us unroll the mission: MSS 512, sends of 300, 200 and 12 bytes, first
bytes 1024, 1324, 1524; the **first segment is lost**.

**Step 1 — the three segments leave.** SEQ = 1024 (300 bytes,
lost ✗), SEQ = 1324 (200 bytes), SEQ = 1524 (12 bytes). A arms its
timer on the oldest unacknowledged byte: 1024.

*Why?* Definition 9: each SEQ = previous + previous size;
and the <Terme id="temporisateur">timer</Terme> watches over the start
of the stream, not over each segment.

**Step 2 — B replies with repeated acknowledgements.** On the arrival of
1324 (bytes 1324–1523): the hole 1024–1323 gapes → **ACK = 1024**. On
the arrival of 1524 (bytes 1524–1535): still **ACK = 1024**. B keeps
the received bytes in memory.

*Why?* Definition 10: cumulative, the ACK can only name the
first missing byte — hence its repetition, twice the same value.

**Step 3 — the timer expires: grouped recovery.** A retransmits
**SEQ = 1024, 512 bytes** — a single segment covering 1024–1535, that
is, the three original sends glued back together (300 + 200 + 12 = 512 = MSS).

*Why?* TCP numbers bytes, not segments (Definition 9):
the recovery resends "everything since the hole", and nothing forces it
to respect the old cutting — the TD's model answer notes that bytes
1324–1535 are received **twice**.

**Step 4 — B glues and acknowledges everything.** The duplicates 1324–1535 are
recognised by their numbers and thrown away; the stream is complete up to 1535 →
**ACK = 1536**, which settles the incident.

*Why?* This is the cumulative acknowledgement at full power
(Definition 10): a single ACK validates 512 bytes, duplicates neutralised
by the numbering — the "reliable stream" service of Definition 6 is
delivered.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**Why does B not send back "ACK = 1524" on the arrival of segment
1324, since it has just received 200 perfectly valid bytes?**

Because ACK = 1524 would state "I have everything up to 1523" — false: bytes
1024–1323 are missing. The cumulative acknowledgement can only say
where the complete prefix ends (Definition 10). Signalling "I also have
block 1324–1523" would demand a richer vocabulary — selective
acknowledgements (SACK), the modern extension the course cites in its
overview.

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Reading the ACK as "last byte received".** ACK = **next expected**
(Definition 10). After 300 bytes from 1024, you reply 1324 — never
1323. A one-byte slip here throws off the whole rest of the timeline.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting that SYN and FIN consume a number.** That is why the
reply to SYN x is ACK x + 1 **although no data has travelled**
(Definition 11). In exams, the forgotten "+1" is the most frequent
mistake on opening diagrams.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Justifying the three steps with "it is more reliable".** The expected
argument is precise: **old duplicates and reordering** — a wandering SYN
from a dead connection must not be able to open a new one, and
the called party must not send before authorisation (mini-exercise §5). The
vague sentence earns nothing in the TD.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Retransmitting the three original segments.** The recovery is
**grouped**: one segment SEQ = 1024 of 512 bytes (Step 3). TCP manages
bytes; the cutting into segments is a delivery detail, never a
commitment.

</Pitfall>
