# The IP parcel — datagram, fragmentation, ARP and ICMP

<LessonMeta id="cs-net-c09" />

<Toc depth={2} />

<Accroche>
An IP datagram is a parcel that accepts being cut up again en route, re-stamped at every counter — and discarded without remorse at the twentieth desk.
</Accroche>

<Mission fil="Exercise 2 of the exam — fragmentation in cascade">
**Situation.** A machine A sends B a message of **2048 bytes**
(handed down by the transport layer). The path crosses two ever-narrower
networks: the first accepts only frames of **1024 bytes**
(14 of which are frame header), the second frames of **512 bytes** (8 of
which are header). Each IP parcel itself carries 20 bytes of header.
**Question.** Exactly which parcels does B's IP module receive — how many,
of what lengths, with which "more to come" indicators and which
positions? **The obstacle.** Every router re-cuts whatever does not fit,
pieces of pieces pile up, and a hidden alignment rule forbids naive
cuts — without a method, the final table is unfeasible.
**The contract.** This chapter gives you 4 tools: the datagram header
field by field, the fragmentation algorithm, ARP and ICMP. At
the end, we fill in the exam's table of **7 fragments**, line by
line.
</Mission>

*Already comfortable with this? [Go straight to TD 5 (tutorial sheet)](../td-5/) —
exercises 3, 4, 9, 12 and 13.*

## The battle plan

To follow the parcel, we need 4 tools:

1. **the IPv4 header** — the fields, and what a router is allowed to touch in it;
2. **fragmentation** — cutting up cleanly: the multiple-of-8 rule, the offsets, the MF flag;
3. **ARP** — finding the hardware address matching an IP address, at every hop;
4. **ICMP** — the bad-news channel, which keeps ping and traceroute alive.

## 1. The IPv4 header — the parcel's route card

Problem: for an autonomous parcel (a
<Terme id="datagramme">datagram</Terme>, lesson 01) to cross routers
that know nothing about it, all the transport information must fit in
its header — no more, no less.

**Definition 1 (IP datagram).** The <Def id="datagramme-ip">**IP
datagram**</Def> is the IP protocol's unit: a header of **20 bytes**
(without options) followed by the data. The fields you must know how to read:

| Field | Bits | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Version | 4 | 4 (IPv4) |
| IHL | 4 | header length in 32-bit words (5 without options) |
| Type of service | 8 | priorities (rarely used in exercises) |
| **Total length** | 16 | header + data, in bytes (max 65,535) |
| **Identification** | 16 | number of the original datagram — identical on all its fragments |
| Flags | 3 | reserved, **DF** (do not fragment), **MF** (more fragments follow) |
| **Offset** | 13 | position of the fragment's data, in **units of 8 bytes** |
| **TTL** | 8 | lifetime counter, decremented at every router |
| Protocol | 8 | whom to hand over to: 6 = TCP, 17 = UDP, 1 = ICMP |
| Header checksum | 16 | <Terme id="somme-de-controle">checksum</Terme> of the header alone |
| Source, destination addresses | 32 + 32 | the end-to-end <Terme id="adresse-ip">IP address</Terme> pair |

**Definition 2 (TTL).** The <Def id="ttl">**TTL**</Def> (Time To Live) is
decremented by 1 at **every** router crossed; at zero, the datagram is
**destroyed** and an error message goes back to the source. It is
the network's life insurance against routing loops.

**Example 1 (the TD 5 question).** What does a router modify when relaying a
datagram? Exactly **two fields**: the TTL (−1) and the header
checksum (recomputed, since the TTL changed). The source and
destination addresses, for their part, **never** change en route.

**Remark.** The checksum covers only the header: recomputing 20 bytes at
each hop is cheap; recomputing 1500 bytes would be ruinous — and
checking the data belongs to the end points anyway
(exercise cs-net-td6-08 makes a full topic of it).

## 2. Fragmentation — cutting up without losing anything

Problem: every network crossed bounds the size of its frames, and the
datagram does not know the path in advance. When it does not fit, it
must be cut up **en route** — by rules the destination will know how
to invert.

**Definition 3 (MTU).** A network's <Def id="mtu">**MTU**</Def> (Maximum Transfer
Unit) is the largest payload one of its frames can
carry — hence the largest datagram that travels on it (1500 bytes on
Ethernet; in problem statements, sometimes given via the frame size minus
its header).

**Definition 4 (fragmentation).** The <Def id="fragmentation">**fragmentation**</Def>
mechanism cuts an oversized datagram into fragments — each a complete
datagram, with a copied header (same Identification, same addresses) —
reassembled **only at the final destination**.

**Definition 5 (fragment offset).** The
<Def id="decalage-de-fragment">**fragment offset**</Def> gives the
position of the fragment's first data byte within the original
datagram, in **units of 8 bytes** — the field has only 13 bits to
cover 65,535 bytes, hence the unit.

**Method (fragmenting through a network of given MTU).**
1. **Raw maximum payload**: MTU − 20 (each fragment's IP header).
2. **Round down to a multiple of 8**: the data of every
   fragment — except the last — must be a multiple of 8 (the offset
   is coded in units of 8).
3. **Cut**: full fragments of that size, the last one takes the
   remainder.
4. **Label**: same Identification everywhere; offset = bytes already
   passed ÷ 8 (counting from the **original** datagram);
   MF = 1 everywhere, **except** the piece that ends the original datagram
   — that one inherits its parent's MF.
5. **Refragment** as needed: a fragment is a datagram like any
   other — a narrower network downstream re-cuts it by the same method.

**Example 2 (the course slide).** 1400 bytes of data, next MTU
620: max payload $620 - 20 = 600$, already a multiple of 8. Fragments
of 600, 600 and 200 bytes of data, offsets 0, 75 ($600/8$) and 150,
MF = 1, 1, 0.

**Remark (the three ritual questions).** *Where is reassembly done?* At the
destination only — fragments may follow different paths, and no
router is sure to see them all. *A fragment gets
lost?* Reassembly has a timer: on expiry, the **whole**
datagram is discarded (and it is the transport layer that will replay).
*DF = 1 and it does not fit?* Destruction, and an ICMP "fragmentation
needed" message goes back — that is how a source discovers the path MTU.

### Mini-exercises

1. A datagram of 129 bytes (20 of which are header), next MTU 128. Fragments?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Data to carry: 109 bytes. Max payload $128-20 = 108$, rounded down
   to a multiple of 8: **104**. Fragments: $104 + 20 = 124$ bytes
   (MF = 1, offset 0) then $5 + 20 = 25$ bytes (MF = 0, offset 13).
   This is exercise cs-net-td5-04, down to the details of its model
   answer.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why is the offset of the 2nd fragment of Example 2 equal to 75 and not 600?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The field counts in units of 8 bytes (Definition 5): $600/8 = 75$.
   Writing 600 is the most frequent mistake in TD 5.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. ARP — from IP address to hardware address

Problem: over the last metre, the datagram travels inside a frame — which
addresses by <Terme id="adresse-mac">MAC address</Terme>. Yet the sender
knows only the next hop's IP address. A directory is missing.

**Definition 6 (ARP).** <Def id="arp">**ARP**</Def> (Address Resolution
Protocol) resolves an IP address into a MAC address **on the local network**:
the sender broadcasts "who has IP address x?" (a frame sent to
`FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF`), the machine concerned replies in unicast with its
MAC address, and the result is kept in a **cache** for a few minutes.

**Example 3 (through a router — the TD 5 mechanism).** A
(`194.148.208.26`) writes to B (`194.148.210.9`), on another network, via the
router R:
1. A sees that B is not on its network (the lesson 08 calculation) → the
   next hop is R; A resolves **R's address** by ARP.
2. The frame carries source MAC = A, destination MAC = **R** — but the
   datagram inside carries source IP = A, destination IP = **B**.
3. R relays: a new frame, source MAC = R (side 2), destination MAC =
   B (resolved by ARP on the second network) — the datagram, for its part,
   keeps its end-to-end IP addresses (Example 1).

MAC addresses change at **every hop**; IP addresses, never.
That is the sentence that solves exercise cs-net-td5-08 — and the frame
decoding of cs-net-td5-12.

**Remark (proxy ARP).** A router may answer ARP requests **on behalf** of
machines sitting behind it (proxy ARP): the senders believe everyone is
local, and the router forwards — an interconnection trick that
leaves the tables untouched, discussed in cs-net-td5-07.

## 4. ICMP — the bad-news channel

Problem: IP destroys without a qualm (TTL at zero, blocking DF, unreachable
destination) — but a mute network would be undebuggable. A reporting
channel is needed.

**Definition 7 (ICMP).** <Def id="icmp">**ICMP**</Def> (Internet Control
Message Protocol) is IP's control protocol: error and probe
messages, carried **inside** IP datagrams
(protocol = 1). The types to know:

| Type | Message | Trigger |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | destination unreachable | no route, closed port, or blocking DF (code 3) |
| 11 | time exceeded | TTL fallen to zero |
| 8 / 0 | echo request / reply | the ping tool |
| 17 / 18 | mask request / reply | configuration |

Golden rule: **never ICMP about ICMP** (nor about any fragment other than
the first) — otherwise errors would breed in a loop.

**Definition 8 (ping).** <Def id="ping">**ping**</Def> sends an ICMP echo
(type 8) and measures the round trip on the reply (type 0): the network's
simplest sign-of-life test.

**Definition 9 (traceroute).** <Def id="traceroute">**traceroute**</Def>
discovers the routers on the path by sending probes with **growing
TTL**: TTL = 1 dies at the first router, which gives itself away with a
type 11; TTL = 2 at the second, and so on. Three tries per TTL; a star `*`
marks a probe left unanswered — that is the whole reading art of
exercise cs-net-td5-13.

## Worked example — the exam's 7 fragments

Let us unroll the mission: 2048 bytes of data, network 1 (frames of 1024,
header 14), network 2 (frames of 512, header 8), IP header 20.

**Step 1 — each network's useful MTU.** Network 1: the frames offer
$1024 - 14 = 1010$ bytes to the datagram, i.e. $1010 - 20 = 990$ bytes of
data. Network 2: $512 - 8 = 504$, i.e. $504 - 20 = 484$ bytes of
data.

*Why?* Two thicknesses of wrapping to subtract — the frame
(given in the statement), then the IP header (Method, move 1). Mixing the
two up is the classic mistake at the start.

**Step 2 — round down to a multiple of 8.** $990 \to 984$; $484 \to 480$.

*Why?* Move 2: the offset is coded in units of 8 bytes
(Definition 5) — an unaligned fragment would be unrepresentable in the
field.

**Step 3 — first fragmentation (router 1).** $2048 = 984 + 984 + 80$:

| Fragment | Total length | MF | Offset |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1004 | 1 | 0 |
| 2 | 1004 | 1 | 123 |
| 3 | 100 | 0 | 246 |

*Why?* Moves 3–4: offsets $0$, $984/8 = 123$, $1968/8 = 246$;
MF = 1 except the last piece, which ends the original datagram.

**Step 4 — second fragmentation (router 2).** Each 984-byte
fragment is re-cut into $480 + 480 + 24$; the 80-byte fragment goes
through untouched:

| Fragment | Total length | MF | Offset |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.1 | 500 | 1 | 0 |
| 1.2 | 500 | 1 | 60 |
| 1.3 | 44 | 1 | 120 |
| 2.1 | 500 | 1 | 123 |
| 2.2 | 500 | 1 | 183 |
| 2.3 | 44 | 1 | 243 |
| 3 | 100 | 0 | 246 |

*Why?* Move 5: a fragment refragments like a datagram,
**offsets counted from the origin** (123 + 60 = 183…). And move 4
sets a trap: pieces 1.3 and 2.3 end their *fragment* but not the
*datagram* — they keep MF = 1; only fragment 3 carries MF = 0.

**Step 5 — check like the marker.** Data:
$480 \times 4 + 24 \times 2 + 80 = 2048$ ✓. Contiguous offsets:
$0,60,120 \to 123$? $120 \times 8 + 24 = 984 = 123 \times 8$ ✓. A single
MF = 0 ✓.

*Why?* Three mechanical checks that catch 90% of mistakes
— sum of the data, continuity of the bytes, uniqueness of the final MF = 0.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**Why does router 2 not glue fragments 1 and 2 back together before
re-cutting — would that not be simpler?**

Because nothing guarantees it sees them both: fragments
may take different paths (Remark of section 2). Reassembly
demands waiting, storing, timing out — everything a
router refuses to do. Only the destination, which must receive everything
anyway, reassembles.

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Writing the offset in bytes.** The field counts in **units of 8
bytes** (Definition 5): 600 bytes of data → offset **75**. On your
script, write both ("984 bytes = 123") — the marker sees the
conversion, and you never miss it again.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Cutting at MTU − 20 without rounding.** 990 is not a multiple of 8:
full fragments carry **984** bytes (move 2). The 2024 exam
set exactly this trap — and the model answer underlines it in red.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Giving MF = 0 to the last piece of each fragment.** MF = 0 means
"I end the **original** datagram" — only one copy of it
exists (move 4). Pieces 1.3 and 2.3 of the exam carry MF = 1.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Making a router reassemble.** Never: multiple paths, memory,
timing out (Remark). Reassembly = final destination, full stop. The
question comes up as a one-liner and is worth a whole mark.

</Pitfall>
