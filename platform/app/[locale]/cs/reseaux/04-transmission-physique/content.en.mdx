A cable does not carry 0s and 1s: it carries a voltage that rises and falls — the whole job is agreeing on what its movements mean.

<Mission fil="Exercise 1 of the one-hour partiel (midterm) — codings and capacity">
**Situation.** A machine A must transmit the sequence
`011110011001100` (15 bits) over a line. Two ways of writing it are

and is the line even capable of transporting it?
**The obstacle.** "Speed" here hides two different quantities — the
rhythm of the levels and the rhythm of the bits — and the line's limit
can be read in neither of them without a theorem. **The contract.** This
chapter gives you 4 tools: baseband codings, valence, the channel's
limits (Nyquist and Shannon), and digitisation. By the end, we draw, we
compute and we settle it — that is exercise 1 of the past paper, model
answer included.
</Mission>

## The battle plan

To answer, we need 4 tools:

1. **baseband codings** — NRZ, Manchester, differential Manchester: writing bits with voltage levels;
2. **valence** — how many bits each level carries, and the baud / bit/s difference;
3. **the channel's limits** — what physics allows: Nyquist (perfect channel) and Shannon (noisy channel);
4. **digitisation** — the reverse journey: from an analog signal to bits.

## 1. Writing bits with voltage levels — baseband codings

Problem: the line only knows continuous physical quantities. A
bits → voltages translation must be agreed on — and it is not unique.

**Definition 1 (signal).** The <Def id="signal">**signal**</Def> is the
physical quantity (voltage, light, wave) that varies over time to carry
the information.

**Definition 2 (baseband coding).** A
<Def id="codage-en-bande-de-base">**baseband coding**</Def>
transmits the bits **directly** as voltage levels on the
medium, with no carrier wave — the case of local networks.

**Definition 3 (NRZ coding).** The <Def id="codage-nrz">**NRZ coding**</Def>
(No Return to Zero) holds a level **high for the whole of a 1 bit**,
low for the whole of a 0 bit.

**Example 1.** `0111` in NRZ: a low level for one bit time, then a high
level held for three bit times — no transition across the three 1s.

Problem: precisely — on `1111111…`, the NRZ signal stops moving, and the
receiver loses count: is that seven 1s or eight? The **clock** must
travel with the data.

**Definition 4 (Manchester coding).** The
<Def id="codage-manchester">**Manchester coding**</Def> imposes a
**transition in the middle of every bit**: the direction of the
transition codes the bit (in this lesson's convention: 1 = rising edge,
0 = falling edge — the statement always fixes its own). **Differential
Manchester** keeps the clock transition in the middle, and codes the bit
at the **start** of the interval: a 0 adds a transition there, a 1 adds
none.

**Method (drawing a differential Manchester).** The statement fixes the
first bit ("initialisation bit, rising direction"); then, for each bit:
1. if it is a **0**, invert the level **at the start** of the interval; a
   **1**, touch nothing;
2. in every case, invert the level **in the middle** (the clock);
3. check at the end of the trace: there must be exactly one mid-bit
   transition per bit, no more, no fewer.

**Remark (the price of the clock).** At equal level rate, Manchester
carries **half as many** bits as NRZ: each bit consumes two
half-levels. Synchronisation is paid for in throughput — a trade-off,
not a free upgrade.

### Mini-exercises

1. Draw `1100` in Manchester (convention 1 = rising edge).

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Bit 1: low then high (rising edge in the middle); bit 1: low then
   high; bit 0: high then low; bit 0: high then low. Four mid-bit
   transitions, one per bit — the clock is visible.

   </Collapsible>

2. In differential Manchester, which bits create a transition at the **start** of an interval?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Only the 0s (Method, step 1). That is what makes the differential
   variant robust: no matter whether the cable inverts the polarities,
   only the *presence* of transitions counts.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Valence — levels that carry several bits

Problem: if the channel distinguishes more than two levels, each level
can carry more than one bit — and "speed" splits into two quantities
that must be separated once and for all.

**Definition 5 (valence).** The <Def id="valence">**valence (number of
levels)**</Def> $V$ is the number of distinct states of the signal. With
$V$ levels, one level codes $\log_2 V$ bits.

**Definition 6 (modulation rate).** The
<Def id="rapidite-de-modulation">**modulation rate (baud)**</Def> $R$ is
the number of levels (symbols) emitted per second, in **bauds**:
$R = 1/\Delta$, where $\Delta$ is the duration of one level.

**Proposition 1 (bit rate and valence).** The <Terme id="debit-binaire">bit
rate</Terme> obtained is

$$
D = R \cdot \log_2 V .
$$

**Why?** $R$ levels per second, each carrying $\log_2 V$
bits (Definition 5): the product counts bits per second. For $V = 2$,
$D = R$ — bauds and bit/s coincide, and **only** in that case.
$\blacksquare$

**Example 2 (the past paper's table).** With $V = 8$, each level codes
3 bits. The partiel's model answer uses the scale: `000` → −8 V,
`001` → −5 V, `010` → −3 V, `011` → −1 V, `100` → +1 V, `101` → +3 V,
`110` → +5 V, `111` → +8 V. The mission's sequence groups as
`011 | 110 | 011 | 001 | 100` → −1, +5, −1, −5, +1 V: five levels for
fifteen bits.

## 3. The channel's limits — Nyquist and Shannon

Problem: can $V$ and $R$ be increased without end? No — the channel has
a finite bandwidth, and noise. Two theorems bound the dream.

**Definition 7 (bandwidth).** The <Def id="bande-passante">**bandwidth**</Def>
$W$ of a channel is the width of the frequency range it lets through, in
hertz.

**Proposition 2 (Nyquist criterion, perfect channel).** Without noise, a
channel of bandwidth $W$ cannot exceed

$$
D_{\max} = 2W \log_2 V .
$$

A signal that flips more than $2W$ times per second comes out distorted
to the point of being unreadable — even perfect, the channel bounds the
**modulation rate**.

Problem: in real life, noise blurs the levels: too many tightly packed
levels become indistinguishable. We need a bound that accounts for
noise — and it will no longer mention the valence.

**Definition 8 (Shannon capacity).** The
<Def id="capacite-de-shannon">**Shannon capacity**</Def> of a channel of
bandwidth $W$ and signal-to-noise ratio $S/B$ is

$$
C = W \log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{S}{B}\right).
$$

No system, whatever its coding or valence, transmits more than
$C$ bits per second without error over this channel.

**Method (converting decibels).** Statements give $S/B$ in dB:

$$
(S/B)_{\text{dB}} = 10 \log_{10}(S/B)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
S/B = 10^{(S/B)_{\text{dB}}/10}.
$$

So 30 dB → $10^{3} = 1000$; 20 dB → 100; 10 dB → 10. The reflex:
divide by 10, then take the power of 10.

**Example 3 (the past paper).** $W = 50$ kHz, 30 dB:
$C = 50\,000 \times \log_2(1001) \approx 5 \times 10^4 \times 10 = 500$ kbit/s — because $1001 \approx 2^{10}$.

**Remark (blind spot).** Shannon is an **existence bound**, not a
recipe: it says that below $C$ an error-free coding exists, without
saying which one. And comparing $D$ to $C$ only validates feasibility —
not the quality of the chosen coding.

### Mini-exercise

1. A telephone channel: 3.1 kHz, 30 dB. Capacity?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $C = 3100 \times \log_2(1001) \approx 3100 \times 10 \approx 31$ kbit/s
   — the historical reason why modems plateaued around
   30 kbit/s.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. Digitising — the reverse journey

Problem: voice, for its part, is born analog. To send it over a network
of bits, it must be sampled — and at the right rhythm.

**Definition 9 (sampling).** The <Def id="echantillonnage">**sampling**</Def>
operation measures the signal at regular intervals, then codes each
measurement on a fixed number of bits. The sampling theorem imposes a
frequency of at least **twice** the signal's highest useful frequency.

**Example 4 (PCM, the one from TD 1).** Telephone voice is limited to
4 kHz → we sample at $2 \times 4000 = 8000$ measurements/s, each on
8 bits:

$$
D = 8000 \times 8 = 64 \text{ kbit/s} .
$$

That is the historical digital telephone channel — and a TD question.

## Worked example — the one-hour past paper, in full

Let us solve the mission on `011110011001100`.

**Step 1 — the differential Manchester trace.** Initialisation bit `0`:
rising mid-bit transition (imposed by the statement). Then, Method: `1` →
nothing at the start, flip in the middle; `1` → same; `1` → same; `1` →
same; `0` → flip at the start **and** in the middle; and so on to the
end.

*Why?* Every bit must exhibit its clock transition (Definition 4)
— the marker checks at a glance: 15 bits, 15 mid-bit
transitions.

**Step 2 — the 8-level trace.** Group by 3 bits:
`011 | 110 | 011 | 001 | 100`, i.e. the levels −1, +5, −1, −5, +1 V of
Example 2, each held for 5 ms.

*Why?* $V = 8 = 2^3$: Definition 5 imposes groups of
$\log_2 8 = 3$ bits — and the grouping runs left to right, with no
remainder here.

**Step 3 — modulation rate.** One level every 5 ms:

$$
R = \frac{1}{5 \times 10^{-3}} = 200 \text{ bauds}.
$$

*Why?* Definition 6: $R = 1/\Delta$ — the rhythm of the **levels**,
not of the bits.

**Step 4 — bit rate.** Proposition 1:

$$
D = R \log_2 V = 200 \times 3 = 600 \text{ bit/s}.
$$

*Why?* Each level carries 3 bits: the bit rate is triple the
modulation rate — exactly the distinction the statement tests.

**Step 5 — the channel's verdict.** Example 3: $C \approx 500$ kbit/s.
Since $D = 600$ bit/s $\ll C$, the channel carries the signal without
difficulty.

*Why?* Definition 8 bounds every system on this channel; comparing
$D$ to $C$ is the only conclusion Shannon authorises — and it is enough
to answer "yes" cleanly.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**Why does the answer of Step 5 not depend on the chosen coding
(8-level NRZ, Manchester…)?**

Because the Shannon capacity bounds the channel **whatever** the
system (Definition 8): it mentions neither valence nor coding. The
coding decides how close you get to the bound, never where the bound
sits.

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Confusing bauds and bit/s.** The baud counts **levels**, the bit/s
counts **bits**. They coincide only at valence 2 (Proposition 1). Always
write the unit after every number — the past paper marks the two
separately.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Taking decibels for a ratio.** 30 dB is not
$S/B = 30$: it is $10^{30/10} = 1000$ (decibel Method). The oversight
divides the computed capacity by ~30 — and the conclusion can flip.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believing Manchester is free.** The mid-bit transition provides the
clock but consumes half the rhythm: at equal modulation rate, half as
many bits as NRZ (Remark of section 1). Every coding is a trade-off.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Drawing with the wrong convention.** "1 = rising edge" or the
opposite: both exist in the books. The statement **always** fixes its
own (the past paper even specifies the direction of the initialisation
bit) — re-read it before drawing, not after.

</Pitfall>
