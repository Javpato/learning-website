# Connecting machines — circuits, messages or packets?

<LessonMeta id="cs-net-c01" />

<Toc depth={2} />

<Accroche>
Before carrying a single byte, a network must answer an architect's question: do we reserve a path, or do we cut the journey into pieces?
</Accroche>

<Mission fil="The 2023 partiel (midterm) transfer — picking the right technique before computing">
**Situation.** Two machines A and B must exchange a 30 KB file. A
single intermediate device R stands between them; the A–R leg is twice
as fast as the R–B leg, and each leg is 6600 km long.
**Question.** Should we reserve an end-to-end path before sending,
ship the file as one block, or cut it into pieces — and what does that
change for R, which has to store and retransmit? **The obstacle.** Without
precise vocabulary there is no comparing: "send the file" covers
at least three techniques with very different costs — in time, in memory and
in failures. **The contract.** This chapter gives you 4 tools: the map of
networks, the three great switching techniques, cell switching, and the
two delivery philosophies. By the end, you choose the partiel's technique
with arguments to back it — lesson 02 will price that choice to the millisecond.
</Mission>

## The battle plan

To choose with full knowledge of the facts, we need 4 tools:

1. **the map of networks** — what are we talking about: sizes, transmission modes, shapes;
2. **the three historical switching techniques** — circuits, messages, packets, and what each one costs;
3. **cells** — the fixed-size refinement;
4. **the two delivery philosophies** — connection-oriented mode (virtual circuit) versus connectionless mode (datagram).

## 1. The map of networks — what are we talking about

Problem: "network" names two computers in a bedroom just as well as the
worldwide Internet. Before comparing techniques, we need a precise
word and three axes of classification.

**Definition 1 (computer network).** A <Def id="reseau-informatique">**computer
network**</Def> is a set of interconnected **autonomous**
machines, able to exchange information. "Autonomous" rules out
the mere terminal plugged into a big computer: every machine on the network
lives its own life.

Why interconnect at all? The course gives four reasons: to **share**
resources (printers, data, computing power), to **improve reliability**
(duplicate across several machines), to **cut costs** (several small
machines rather than one central monster), and to **grow gradually** (you
add a machine, not a building).

**Example 1 (classification by size).** The standard scale, with the orders
of magnitude from the course:

| Acronym | Reach | Example | Typical rates |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| PAN | ~1 m | your watch and your phone | ~1 Mbit/s |
| LAN | building, campus (10 m – 1 km) | the TP room (lab class) | 10 Mbit/s – 10 Gbit/s |
| MAN | a city (~10 km) | urban cable network | ~100 Mbit/s |
| WAN | country, continent | an operator's network | highly variable |

**Example 2 (classification by transmission).** Two families:
**broadcast** — a single shared channel, every transmission is heard by
all, each station sorts out its own (this is the world of the
<Terme id="reseau-local">local area network (LAN)</Terme>) — and
**point-to-point** — individual links between
pairs of machines, and intermediate devices that relay (this is the
world of large networks, and of everything else in this chapter).

Problem: we still need to name the **shape** of the wiring — it governs
failures and performance.

**Definition 2 (topology).** The <Def id="topologie">**topology**</Def>
of a network is the shape of its wiring (**physical** topology) or of the
path the information follows (**logical** topology): **bus** (one shared
cable), **ring** (each machine linked to the next, in a loop), **star**
(everything goes through a centre). The two topologies can differ: a
logical ring can be wired as a star around a hub.

**Remark.** Broadcast and topology overlap without coinciding: a bus
is naturally a broadcast network, while a star can imitate either one
depending on its centre (repeater or switch).

## 2. The three switching techniques — reserve, store or cut

Problem: in a point-to-point network, an intermediate machine receives
data on one input and must redirect them to the right output. We
need a word for that gesture, then strategies to organise it.

**Definition 3 (switching).** The job of an intermediate node (the
switch) is <Def id="commutation">**switching**</Def>: **steering** what
arrives on one of its inputs toward the right output.

### Reserving: circuit switching

**Definition 4 (circuit switching).** In
<Def id="commutation-de-circuits">**circuit switching**</Def>, a physical
path reserved end to end is established **before any exchange** (like
a telephone line); the data follow it without thinking, then the
path is released.

**Example 3.** The historical telephone: three phases — pick up and
dial (establishment), talk (transfer), hang up (release).

**What you gain / what you lose.** Gained: a constant, tiny delay
after establishment (no decisions along the way), a guaranteed
rate. Lost: the path is **paid for even when silent** (computers
transmit in bursts: the circuit sits empty 90% of the time); establishment
takes time; a failure on the path kills the communication.

### Storing: message switching

Problem: to reserve nothing, each node must agree to **hold**
the data while it decides on the output.

**Definition 5 (message switching).** In
<Def id="commutation-de-messages">**message switching**</Def>, the
**whole** message is sent node by node: each node receives it completely,
stores it, chooses the output, then retransmits it.

**Definition 6 (store-and-forward).** This behaviour — waiting until the
**latest** unit has been received in full before starting to retransmit it —
is called <Def id="store-and-forward">**store-and-forward**</Def>. Remember it:
it is the assumption spelled out in black and white in every partiel problem
statement.

**What you gain / what you lose.** Gained: no more reservation, the
links are shared among everyone. Lost: each node must be able to store an
**entire** message (unbounded memory!); a long message monopolises
each link for its whole transmission; one error starts everything over.

### Cutting: packet switching

Problem: whole-message storage does not scale. The decisive idea:
bound the size of what travels.

**Definition 7 (packet switching).** In
<Def id="commutation-de-paquets">**packet switching**</Def>, the message is
cut into **packets** of bounded size, sent independently; each
node does store-and-forward **per packet**, and the destination reassembles.

**Example 4 (the gain, by eye).** A 3-unit message, two links, one
intermediate node:

| Technique | A–R link busy | R–B link busy | End |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| whole message | units 1-2-3 | then units 1-2-3 | after 6 time units |
| 3 packets | p1, p2, p3 | p1 as soon as received, etc. | after 4 time units |

While R retransmits p1, A is already sending it p2: the links work
**at the same time**. This is the pipeline effect — lesson 02 turns it into
a formula ($T = (k+n)\,t_t$) and the partiel makes you compute it.

**What you gain / what you lose.** Gained: bounded memory per node,
finely shared links, packet-by-packet error recovery, several possible
paths. Lost: every packet pays for a **header** (the address
travels with it), packets can arrive **out of order** or get lost —
and someone will have to restore order (that will be TCP, lesson 11).

## 3. Cells — cutting at a fixed size

Problem: packets of wildly varying sizes make delays
irregular — a nuisance for voice or video, where regularity matters more
than raw rate.

**Definition 8 (cell switching).** The technique of
<Def id="commutation-de-cellules">**cell switching**</Def> is
packet switching with a **fixed, small** size — the
**53-byte** cells of ATM (48 of data + 5 of header).

**Example 5.** A fixed size allows very fast hardware switches
(no length field to read) and fine interleaving of flows: no
cell waits long behind a giant one. The price: almost 10%
of header, and permanent cutting and reassembly.

**Remark (blind spot).** Small size does not mean best size:
the smaller the units, the larger the share taken by headers. Exercise
cs-net-td4-01 computes the **optimal** size — it exists, and it depends
on the network.

## 4. Two delivery philosophies — virtual circuit or datagram

Problem: within packet switching, one architectural choice
remains: must the packets of one conversation follow the
same path?

**Definition 9 (connection-oriented mode).** In <Def id="mode-connecte">**connection-oriented
mode**</Def>, every exchange goes through three phases — **establishment**,
transfer, **release** — and the data follow the path negotiated at
establishment.

**Definition 10 (virtual circuit).** The path marked at establishment
is called a <Def id="circuit-virtuel">**virtual circuit**</Def>: each
switch writes in a table "what arrives with label 26 on
input 1 leaves with label 11 on output 3". Packets carry
only a **short label**, translated at every hop — the full
address travelled only once, in the establishment packet.

**Definition 11 (connectionless mode).** In <Def id="mode-non-connecte">**connectionless
mode**</Def>, each transmission stands alone: no establishment, no
state in the switches — but every packet must carry the destination's
**full address**.

**Definition 12 (datagram).** A <Def id="datagramme">**datagram**</Def>
is that autonomous packet of connectionless mode, routed independently
of the others. It is the Internet's choice: the IP protocol (lessons 08–09)
is a datagram service.

**Example 6 (the course's table).** The comparison to know:

| | Virtual circuit | Datagram |
| --- | --- | --- |
| establishment | mandatory | none |
| address in each packet | short label | full address |
| path | identical for all packets | free for each packet |
| arrival order | preserved | not guaranteed |
| node failure | kills the circuits through it | bypassed from the next packet on |
| state in the switches | one table entry per circuit | none |

**Remark (blind spot).** "Connection-oriented" does not mean "reliable":
a virtual circuit can lose packets, and a datagram service
can be made reliable **on top** (that is exactly the Internet's bet:
unreliable IP + reliable TCP at the endpoints).

## Worked example — arguing the partiel's choice

Back to the mission: 30 KB from A to B via R, links of different rates.
Let us compare the three candidates — without timing anything yet
(lesson 02), but arguing the way the exam statement asks.

**Step 1 — eliminate circuit switching.** A file transfer
is a single burst: establishing a circuit (a full round trip before the
first useful byte) then releasing it costs two traversals for a single
send, and the reservation brings nothing that cannot be had another way.

*Why?* The circuit's strength (Definition 4) is a constant delay for
a **continuous flow** — voice. Our traffic is the opposite: one burst.

**Step 2 — set aside the whole message.** With message switching, R
must store 30 KB, and the R–B link sits **idle** during the entire
A→R reception: the two links never work at the same time.

*Why?* Store-and-forward on the entire unit (Definition 6): the total
time is the **sum** of the two transmissions — no overlap possible.

**Step 3 — choose packets, and anticipate the objection.** By cutting
(Definition 7), R relays packet 1 **while** A sends packet 2:
the links overlap, the time drops. The objection to see coming: each
packet adds a header, so cutting "too fine" ends up costing.

*Why?* This is the pipeline effect of Example 4; the limit comes from the
Remark in section 3 — and exercise cs-net-td4-01 computes where to stop.

**Step 4 — choose the philosophy.** Two packets between two machines via
a single R: no point in setting up a virtual circuit; datagrams
are enough, even if it means renumbering on arrival.

*Why?* The table of Example 6: per-circuit state is only justified
for long flows or ordering guarantees — not for two packets.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**The partiel asks: "will the throughput increase if the file is divided
into smaller packets?" What should you answer in one sentence?**

Yes at first — overlapping the links shortens the total time, hence
raises the effective throughput — but less and less so, and only as long
as headers are neglected: cut too fine, the file pays more in headers
than it gains in pipelining. (The numerical answer: lesson 02 and cs-net-td4-01.)

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**"Connection-oriented" = "reliable".** No: the virtual circuit guarantees
the **order** and the path, not the packets' survival. Reliability is a
separate service, built on top (lesson 11).

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confusing message switching with packet switching.** The difference is not
cosmetic: it is the **bounded size** that bounds the nodes' memory and
makes the pipeline possible. Without it, no Internet.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believing the datagram is carelessness.** Guaranteeing nothing inside the
network is a **design choice**: the switches stay simple and
stateless, and the intelligence concentrates at the endpoints. The whole
module unfolds the consequences of that choice.

</Pitfall>

<MissionSolved>
For the partiel's 30 KB: **packet switching, in datagram mode.**
The circuit (Definition 4) would pay an establishment for a mere burst;
the whole message (Definitions 5–6) would force R to store 30 KB and
forbid the two links from working at the same time; packets
(Definition 7) overlap the links — and two datagrams are enough, with no
state at R (Definition 12). The exam statement even gives the split: p1 =
10 KB, p2 = 20 KB. How many milliseconds exactly, and what effective
throughput? That is precisely the mission of [lesson 02](../02-delais-et-debits/).
</MissionSolved>

## Summary — what you can do now

<KeyResults title="Choosing a technique in three questions">

| Question | If yes… | If no… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is the traffic a continuous, delay-critical flow (voice)? | circuits, or cells | keep going |
| Can the nodes store the entire unit? | messages possible (never optimal) | packets mandatory |
| Do you need guaranteed order and a stable path? | virtual circuit (labels, per-circuit state) | datagram (full address in every packet) |

**The three definitions that come back in every problem statement:**
store-and-forward (the node waits for the last bit), datagram (autonomous,
full address), virtual circuit (label translated hop by hop).

</KeyResults>
