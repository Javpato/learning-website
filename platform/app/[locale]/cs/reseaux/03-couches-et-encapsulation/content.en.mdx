# Slicing up complexity — layers, services and encapsulation

<LessonMeta id="cs-net-c03" />

<Toc depth={2} />

<Accroche>
Nobody knows how to write a program that does "the network" in one block — so it was cut into levels, and each level talks only to its two neighbours.
</Accroche>

<Mission fil="The 1000-byte request — counting what really leaves on the cable">
**Situation.** Your browser sends a **1000-byte** request to a web
server. On the way, the request crosses several pieces of software stacked
inside your machine, and each one adds a few bytes of bookkeeping
information: 20, then 20, then 14 at the front and 4 at the end. **Question.**
How many bytes actually leave on the cable, what share of the transfer
is "real" content — and what exactly does the software on the far side
receive at each level? **The obstacle.** Without a model, there is no saying
who adds what, in which order, nor why the server recovers the 1000 bytes
intact. **The contract.** This chapter gives you 4 tools: the notions of
layer and service, encapsulation, the OSI model and the TCP/IP model.
By the end, we count the request's bytes one by one — and answer the
four QCM (multiple-choice) questions that come up every year.
</Mission>

*Already comfortable with this? [Go straight to TD 5 (tutorial sheet)](../td-5/) — its
exercise 8 walks through an entire gateway.*

## The battle plan

To follow the request, we need 4 tools:

1. **layers, services, protocols** — the vocabulary that separates "what is offered" from "how we talk to each other";
2. **encapsulation** — the mechanism that nests the messages (SDU + header = PDU);
3. **the OSI model** — the 7-level reference, for naming things;
4. **the TCP/IP model** — the Internet's real architecture, with 4 levels.

## 1. Layers, services, protocols — who talks to whom

Problem: connecting two applications across the world piles up dozens
of tasks (signals, errors, paths, reliability, formats…). A single
program that did everything would be impossible to write and impossible to
stabilise. We must **decompose** — and decide where the borders go.

**Definition 1 (layer).** A <Def id="couche">**layer**</Def> is one
level of the architecture: it **renders a service** to the layer above by
**using** the service of the layer below — and nothing else. Each
layer contains one or more *entities* (the programs that do the
work).

**Definition 2 (service).** The <Def id="service">**service**</Def> of a
layer is what it offers the level above, described **independently of
how it is achieved** — like a menu, not a recipe.

**Definition 3 (protocol).** A <Def id="protocole">**protocol**</Def>
is the set of dialogue rules between **two same-level entities**
on two machines: message formats and behaviours. The distinction
is crucial: the service is **vertical** (between neighbouring layers of
one machine), the protocol is **horizontal** (between machines).

**Example 1.** Your machine's transport layer offers your browser the
"reliable stream" service; to deliver it, it runs the TCP protocol
with the server's transport layer. The browser never sees TCP — it
sees the menu, not the kitchen.

**Remark (virtual communication).** Two same-level entities
**believe** they are talking directly — in reality, everything goes down
the stack on one side and back up on the other. The course illustrates this
with two philosophers debating through their translators and their
secretaries: each one talks only to their immediate neighbour, and yet the
debate really does happen from one philosopher to the
other.

Problem: to describe a service without revealing the recipe, we need a
vocabulary of elementary operations — and a doorway where to request them.

**Definition 4 (service primitives).** The
<Def id="primitive-de-service">**service primitive**</Def>s are the
four standard operations of a service: **request** (I ask),
**indication** (I am notified), **response** (I answer),
**confirmation** (I get confirmation).

**Example 2.** Opening a connection: A issues CONNECT.request → B receives
CONNECT.indication → B issues CONNECT.response → A receives
CONNECT.confirmation. Four primitives, two machines, one service rendered.

**Definition 5 (SAP).** The <Def id="sap">**SAP**</Def> (Service Access
Point) is the doorway through which a layer reaches the service of the layer
below — concretely, a local per-level address. You already know
one without knowing it: the <Terme id="port">port</Terme>, the transport
layer's SAP.

## 2. Encapsulation — the nesting that makes everything work

Problem: each layer must attach its bookkeeping information (numbers,
addresses, checks) to the message — without ever touching the content
entrusted to it by the level above. We need disciplined nesting.

**Definition 6 (SDU).** The <Def id="sdu">**SDU**</Def> (Service Data Unit)
is the block of data the upper layer hands over to be carried
**as-is** — opaque, untouchable.

**Definition 7 (PDU).** The <Def id="pdu">**PDU**</Def> (Protocol Data Unit)
is the unit the layer exchanges with its peer: its **own
header** (the PCI, the bookkeeping information) glued in front of the SDU:

$$
\text{PDU} = \text{PCI} + \text{SDU}.
$$

**Definition 8 (encapsulation).** What we call <Def id="encapsulation">**encapsulation**</Def>
is this mechanism applied in cascade: on the way **down**, each layer
takes the PDU from above as its SDU and glues its header in front; on the
way **up**, each layer removes the header its peer put on,
processes it, and passes the SDU to the level above.

**Example 3 (the course's real sizes).** The mission's request:
1000 bytes, + 20 (transport) + 20 (network) + 14 in front and 4 behind
(network access, the only layer that also adds an **end** of frame, for
error checking). Each PDU has a name: **segment** (transport),
**datagram** (network), **frame** (network access).

**Example 4 (the yearly QCM).** "A 30-byte message goes down the stack;
each of the 6 layers below the application adds 5 bytes. Size on the
cable?" — $30 + 6 \times 5 = 60$ bytes. "And what does B's application
layer receive?" — 30 bytes: each header was removed by the
peer layer that had put it on.

**Remark (blind spot).** Pure encapsulation is not always enough:
if the SDU is too big for the level below, the layer **segments** it
(several PDUs for one SDU); if it is tiny, it may **group**
several SDUs into one PDU. IP fragmentation (lesson 09) is exactly
a segmentation — with rules of its own.

## 3. The OSI model — the 7-level reference

Problem: for manufacturers to understand one another, we need a
**standardised** layering — level names common to all.

**Definition 9 (OSI model).** The <Def id="modele-osi">**OSI model**</Def>
(Open Systems Interconnection, standardised by the ISO) cuts
communication into **7 layers**:

| N | Layer | Role in one line | Unit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7 | Application | the useful services (web, mail, files) | message |
| 6 | Presentation | formats, encodings, encryption | — |
| 5 | Session | organising the dialogue (turn-taking, resumption) | — |
| 4 | Transport | end to end, process to process, reliability | segment |
| 3 | Network | addressing, routing, avoiding congestion | datagram / packet |
| 2 | Data link | making one link reliable: frames, errors, flow | frame |
| 1 | Physical | transmitting raw bits (signals) | bit |

**Example 5.** The lessons of this module climb this stack: physical
transmission (lesson 04), data link (05–06), local networks (07),
network (08–10), transport (11–13).

**Remark (blind spot).** OSI is a **reading grid**, not
the real Internet: layers 5 and 6 almost never exist as separate
programs. Reciting OSI does not say where IP lives — that is the next
model's job.

## 4. The TCP/IP model — the real architecture

Problem: the Internet was built before the OSI standard, with a shorter
layering — and that is the one you must know to place IP, TCP and UDP.

**Definition 10 (TCP/IP model).** The <Def id="modele-tcp-ip">**TCP/IP
model**</Def> organises the Internet into **4 layers**: **network access**
(≈ OSI 1+2), **IP** (≈ OSI 3), **transport** (OSI 4: TCP and UDP),
**application** (≈ OSI 5+6+7).

**Example 6 (the course's protocol graph).** Web, mail and DNS
rest on TCP or UDP; TCP and UDP rest on IP; IP rests on
any network access (Ethernet, Wi-Fi, fibre…). IP is the hourglass's
**deliberate neck**: a single protocol in the middle, everything else free
to vary above as below.

**Remark.** In the QCMs: "IP is a **network**-level protocol", "TCP
is a **transport**-level protocol" — statements love to offer
"TCP at the network level" or "IP at the link level". The correspondence
OSI 1-2 ↔ network access, OSI 5-6-7 ↔ application also comes up exactly as is.

## Worked example — the request, byte by byte

Let us follow the mission: a 1000-byte request, the TCP/IP stack, real sizes.

**Step 1 — at the application layer.** The PDU is the message itself:
1000 bytes. For the layer below, this block becomes an opaque SDU.

*Why?* Definitions 6–7: each level relabels "PDU from above →
SDU here"; the content itself never changes.

**Step 2 — the transport layer adds 20 bytes.** A segment of
$1000 + 20 = 1020$ bytes: ports, numbers, checks (lesson 11 will
detail them).

*Why?* Definition 8: transport PCI (20 bytes for TCP) + SDU. The
segment is the PDU the transport layer "addresses" to its peer.

**Step 3 — the IP layer adds 20 bytes.** A datagram of
$1020 + 20 = 1040$ bytes: IP addresses, TTL, checks (lesson 09).

*Why?* Same mechanism, one level down — and the whole segment has
become an SDU, transport header included: IP does not read it.

**Step 4 — network access frames it: 14 in front, 4 behind.** A frame of
$14 + 1040 + 4 = 1058$ bytes on the cable.

*Why?* Example 3: the lowest layer also adds an **end of
frame** (the error check — lesson 05 will explain why it is easier to
compute at the end).

**Step 5 — the tally, and the way back up.** On the cable: **1058 bytes**,
of which 1000 useful — an overhead of $58/1058 \approx 5{,}5\ \%$. At the
server, each layer removes the header put on by its peer: the application
receives exactly **1000 bytes**.

*Why?* Definition 8, upward direction — and that is the answer to the two
QCM questions of Example 4, transposed to the real sizes.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**Can the server's IP layer read the port number placed by the
sender's transport layer?**

In principle no: the port sits in the transport header, which is part of the
SDU that IP carries without reading it (Definition 6). It is a
**design** border — each layer processes only its own header — and it is
what makes the layers independently replaceable.

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Confusing SDU and PDU.** The SDU is what you **receive from above** (opaque);
the PDU is what you **send to your peer** (header included). The
question "a level-N PDU is…" tests exactly that arrow —
revise Example 4 before every QCM.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believing the layers of one machine "talk" to each other in a protocol.**
Between neighbouring levels of one machine, there are only service calls
(primitives, Definition 4). The protocol connects **peers** on
different machines — virtually.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Reciting OSI as if it were the Internet.** The Internet is TCP/IP: 4
layers, and IP alone in the middle. OSI is for **naming** (layer 2, layer 3…),
TCP/IP for **placing** the real protocols. Both questions come up, and they
are not interchangeable.

</Pitfall>
