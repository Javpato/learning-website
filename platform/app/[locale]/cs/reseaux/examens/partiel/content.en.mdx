<ExamProblem title="Exercise 1 — Distance-vector routing" points={4}>

A router R is connected to three neighbours A, B and C by links of cost
**3** each. Its routing table reads:

| Destination | Next hop | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Network_A | B | 9 |
| Network_B | B | 5 |
| Network_C | A | 6 |
| Network_D | C | 4 |

R receives over the link from **B** the vector
DV₁ = [(Network_A, 4), (Network_B, 6), (Network_C, 2)], then over the link from
**A** the vector DV₂ = [(Network_B, 1), (Network_C, 3), (Network_E, 5)].

1. Write R's routing table after receiving DV₁. **(1,5 pt)**
2. Write it after receiving DV₂. **(1 pt)**
3. Give the content of the vector R then sends on **each** of its
   three links with the “split horizon with poisoned reverse” technique.
   **(1,5 pt)**

<Collapsible title="Solution and indicative marking scheme">

**1. After DV₁ (1,5 pt).** Candidates = announced + 3. Network_A:
$4+3 = 7 < 9$ → **(B, 7)**. Network_B: $6+3 = 9 > 5$, **but** B is already
the next hop — its new value is authoritative → **(B, 9)** (0,5 pt
on this line alone: it is the rule the paper is testing). Network_C:
$2+3 = 5 < 6$ → **(B, 5)**. Network_D unchanged (not announced).

| Destination | Next hop | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Network_A | B | 7 |
| Network_B | B | 9 |
| Network_C | B | 5 |
| Network_D | C | 4 |

**2. After DV₂ (1 pt).** Network_B: $1+3 = 4 < 9$ → **(A, 4)**.
Network_C: $3+3 = 6 > 5$ and A is not the next hop → unchanged.
Network_E: unknown → **created (A, 8)**.

| Destination | Next hop | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Network_A | B | 7 |
| Network_B | A | 4 |
| Network_C | B | 5 |
| Network_D | C | 4 |
| Network_E | A | 8 |

**3. Split horizon with poisoned reverse (1,5 pt — 0,5 per vector).** On
each link, the routes **learned over that link** are advertised at
infinity:

- towards **A** (routes via A: Network_B, Network_E):
  [A : 7, B : ∞, C : 5, D : 4, E : ∞];
- towards **B** (routes via B: Network_A, Network_C):
  [A : ∞, B : 4, C : ∞, D : 4, E : 8];
- towards **C** (routes via C: Network_D):
  [A : 7, B : 4, C : 5, D : ∞, E : 8].

Three **different** vectors — the poisoned reverse is a targeted lie,
link by link (lesson 10, Definition 8).

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>

<ExamProblem title="Exercise 2 — Packet switching and queueing" points={4}>

At instant $t = 0$, three nodes each send one packet towards E, via the
router **D** (store-and-forward, FIFO policy): A sends **20 000
bits** on an A–D link at **10 Mbit/s**; B sends **200 000 bits** on a
B–D link at **50 Mbit/s**; C sends **20 000 bits** on a C–D link at
**1 Mbit/s**. The D–E link runs at **5 Mbit/s**. Propagation and
processing delays are negligible.

For each of the three packets, compute the arrival instant of its
**first** and its **last** bit at E, and draw the time diagram
of the service order at D.

<Collapsible title="Solution and indicative marking scheme">

**Transmission times (1,5 pt).** Towards D: A:
$2 \times 10^4 / 10^7 = 2$ ms; B: $2 \times 10^5 / (5 \times 10^7) = 4$ ms; C: $2 \times 10^4 / 10^6 = 20$ ms. On D–E (5 Mbit/s): A:
4 ms; B: 40 ms; C: 4 ms.

**FIFO order at D (1 pt).** Complete arrivals: A at 2 ms, B at 4 ms, C
at 20 ms — D serves them in that order: A from 2 to 6 ms; B from
$\max(4, 6) = 6$ to 46 ms; C from $\max(20, 46) = 46$ to 50 ms (26 ms
of queueing for C).

**Arrivals at E (1,5 pt).** Zero propagation: first bit = start of
re-emission, last bit = end.

| Packet | First bit at E | Last bit at E |
| --- | --- | --- |
| from A | 2 ms | **6 ms** |
| from B | 6 ms | **46 ms** |
| from C | 46 ms | **50 ms** |

C's small packet (4 ms of emission) waits 26 ms behind B's giant:
the FIFO queue plays no favourites — it is the archetypal exercise of
the 2024-25 midterm, and of lesson 02 (chronogram Method, Definition 5).

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>

<ExamProblem title="Exercise 3 — IP addressing and subnetting" points={7}>

A site owns the prefix `137.194.0.0/17` and must organise **120
physical subnets**.

1. Give the address's class and the site's mask in dotted
   decimal notation. **(1 pt)**
2. Give the subnet mask (dotted decimal and CIDR) and the address
   of the subnet with identifier **77**. **(1,5 pt)**
3. For that same subnet 77: broadcast address, number of
   addressable machines, range of machine addresses. **(1,5 pt)**
4. Subnet 77 is itself subdivided into **4 sub-subnets**.
   Give the new mask, and the address and broadcast of the sub-subnet
   with identifier 2. **(1,5 pt)**
5. The administrator wants to place there a machine with identifier (Host-Id)
   **300**. Possible? Justify. **(0,5 pt)**
6. Write the address of the first machine of subnet 77 in
   translated IPv6 format. **(1 pt)**

<Collapsible title="Solution and indicative marking scheme">

**1.** $137 \in [128, 191]$ → **class B**; site mask /17 =
`11111111.11111111.10000000.0` = **`255.255.128.0`**.

**2.** $2^6 = 64 < 120 \le 128 = 2^7$ → **7 subnet bits**;
prefix $17 + 7 = 24$: mask **`255.255.255.0` (/24)**. Identifier
77 on 7 bits: $77 = 1001101_2$. The 3rd octet is
[site bit 0][1001101] = `01001101` = 77: address
**`137.194.77.0`**. (A coincidence engineered by the /17: the site bit
is 0 — checking in binary remains the only safe reflex.)

**3.** Broadcast: host part (8 bits) all 1s → **`137.194.77.255`**;
machines: $2^8 - 2 = $ **254**; range **`137.194.77.1` →
`137.194.77.254`**.

**4.** 4 sub-subnets → 2 bits: mask **/26 =
`255.255.255.192`**. Identifier 2 = `10`: 4th octet `10 000000` = 128 →
address **`137.194.77.128`**, broadcast `10 111111` = 191 →
**`137.194.77.191`** (62 machines).

**5.** **No**: in a /26, the machine identifier fits in 6 bits
(max 62) — and even in the whole /24, 8 bits cap it at 254.
“Host-Id 300” would require 9 bits: impossible without changing the mask.

**6.** First machine: `137.194.77.1` → **`::ffff:137.194.77.1`**
(80 bits at 0, 16 bits at 1, then the IPv4 address — lesson 08, Method §5;
in full hexadecimal: `::ffff:89c2:4d01`).

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>
