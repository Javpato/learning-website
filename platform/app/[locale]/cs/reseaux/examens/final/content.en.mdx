<ExamProblem title="Exercise 1 — CIDR allocation" points={4}>

An Internet provider owns a large block of contiguous addresses
starting at `195.60.0.0`. Four customers ask for: **A: 400 hosts**,
**B: 950 hosts**, **C: 1900 hosts**, **D: 3800 hosts**. The provider
allocates each of them a “class-C equivalent” block of addresses
(multiples of 256 addresses), contiguous and aligned.

1. How many blocks of 256 addresses must be allocated to each customer?
   **(1,5 pt)**
2. Propose a contiguous, aligned allocation starting at `195.60.0.0`,
   giving each customer's CIDR prefix. **(1,5 pt)**
3. Would a single class B address have sufficed for the four
   customers? What utilisation efficiency would it have had? **(1 pt)**

<Collapsible title="Solution and indicative marking scheme">

**1. Number of blocks.** Each block of 256 addresses offers 254 machines;
the block counts must be powers of 2 to stay alignable:
A: 400 → **2 blocks** (508 addresses); B: 950 → **4 blocks** (1016);
C: 1900 → **8 blocks** (2032); D: 3800 → **16 blocks** (4064). Total:
30 blocks.

**2. Aligned allocation** (largest first, the golden rule —
every block of $2^p$ class Cs must start on a multiple of $2^p$):

| Customer | Range | CIDR prefix | Mask |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| D (16) | 195.60.0.0 → 195.60.15.255 | **195.60.0.0/20** | 255.255.240.0 |
| C (8) | 195.60.16.0 → 195.60.23.255 | **195.60.16.0/21** | 255.255.248.0 |
| B (4) | 195.60.24.0 → 195.60.27.255 | **195.60.24.0/22** | 255.255.252.0 |
| A (2) | 195.60.28.0 → 195.60.29.255 | **195.60.28.0/23** | 255.255.254.0 |

Each customer fits in **a single row** of the worldwide routing
table: that is aggregation in its entirety (lesson 08, Definition 8).
Alignment check: 0, 16, 24, 28 are indeed multiples of 16, 8, 4, 2.

**3. A class B?** Yes in capacity: $400 + 950 + 1900 + 3800 = 7050 \le 65\,534$. But the efficiency would be

$$
e_B = \frac{7050}{2^{16}} \approx 10{,}8\ \%
$$

— nine addresses wasted out of ten (the past paper found 14 %: same
order of magnitude, same lesson). That is precisely the waste that
killed the classes and imposed CIDR.

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>

<ExamProblem title="Exercise 2 — Cascading fragmentation" points={5}>

A machine A sends B a message of **1800 bytes** (handed to IP by the
transport layer; IP header: 20 bytes, identification 4321). The
path crosses a network 1 whose frames are capped at **1024 bytes**
(frame header: 14 bytes), then a network 2 whose frames are capped
at **512 bytes** (frame header: 8 bytes).

1. What IP data load does each network accept per fragment?
   **(1,5 pt)**
2. Give the fragments travelling on network 1 (total length, MF,
   offset). **(1,5 pt)**
3. Give the complete sequence of fragments received by B's IP module,
   in a table (total length, MF, offset), justifying the
   calculations. **(2 pts)**

<Collapsible title="Solution and indicative marking scheme">

**1. Payloads.** Network 1: $1024 - 14 = 1010$ bytes of
datagram, i.e. $1010 - 20 = 990$ bytes of data, rounded down to a
multiple of 8: **984**. Network 2: $512 - 8 = 504$, i.e. $484 \to$
**480**. (0,75 pt per network — the multiple-of-8 rule is the heart of
the marking scheme.)

**2. Network 1.** $1800 = 984 + 816$:

| Fragment | Total length | MF | Offset |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1004 | 1 | 0 |
| 2 | 836 | 0 | 123 |

($984/8 = 123$; identification 4321 on both.)

**3. Network 2.** Fragment 1 (984 bytes) becomes $480 + 480 + 24$;
fragment 2 (816 bytes) becomes $480 + 336$:

| Fragment | Total length | MF | Offset |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1.1 | 500 | 1 | 0 |
| 1.2 | 500 | 1 | 60 |
| 1.3 | 44 | 1 | 120 |
| 2.1 | 500 | 1 | 123 |
| 2.2 | 356 | **0** | 183 |

Expected justifications: offsets counted **from the original
datagram** ($480/8 = 60$; $120 \times 8 + 24 = 984 = 123 \times 8$;
$123 \times 8 + 480 = 1464 = 183 \times 8$); MF = 1 everywhere **except**
the piece that ends the original datagram (1.3 ends fragment 1
but not the message: MF = 1); mass check:
$480 + 480 + 24 + 480 + 336 = 1800$ ✓. Reassembly: **at B
only** (lesson 09).

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>

<ExamProblem title="Exercise 3 — Subnetting and routing table" points={6}>

The site `129.20.0.0` (default mask of its class) is carved into
**6 subnets**. The subnet with identifier **6** is itself
subdivided into **5 sub-subnets** served by the internal routers
R1 to R5; router R6 connects the site to the outside.

1. Give the site's subnet mask (dotted decimal and CIDR).
   **(1 pt)**
2. Give the address of subnet 6, then the mask of its
   sub-subnets. **(1,5 pt)**
3. Give the prefixes of sub-subnets 1 to 5. **(1,5 pt)**
4. R1 is connected to sub-subnet 1; R4 (Host-Id 292) and R2
   (Host-Id 316) are reachable there. Knowing that R4 leads to
   sub-subnets 2 and 4, R2 to sub-subnets 3 and 5, and R6 to the
   rest of the world, write R1's routing table (destination, mask,
   next hop). **(1,5 pt)**
5. Give the machine range and the broadcast of the sub-subnet to which
   `129.20.211.71` belongs. **(0,5 pt)**

<Collapsible title="Solution and indicative marking scheme">

**1.** $129 \in [128, 191]$: class B, default mask `255.255.0.0`.
6 subnets → $2^2 = 4 < 6 \le 8 = 2^3$ → 3 bits: mask
**`255.255.224.0` (/19)**.

**2.** Identifier 6 = `110`: 3rd octet `110 00000` = 192 → subnet
**`129.20.192.0/19`**. 5 sub-subnets → 3 more bits: mask
**`255.255.252.0` (/22)**.

**3.** The 3rd octet is `110` + identifier (3 bits) + 2 machine bits:

| Sub-sub-net | 3rd octet | Prefix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 110 001 00 = 196 | **129.20.196.0/22** |
| 2 | 110 010 00 = 200 | **129.20.200.0/22** |
| 3 | 110 011 00 = 204 | **129.20.204.0/22** |
| 4 | 110 100 00 = 208 | **129.20.208.0/22** |
| 5 | 110 101 00 = 212 | **129.20.212.0/22** |

**4.** Addresses of R1's neighbours on sub-subnet 1 (Host-Id on
10 bits): R4: $292 = 01\,0010\,0100_2$ → `129.20.197.36`; R2:
$316 = 01\,0011\,1100_2$ → `129.20.197.60`. R1's table:

| Destination | Mask | Next hop |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 129.20.196.0 | /22 | direct delivery |
| 129.20.200.0 | /22 | 129.20.197.36 (R4) |
| 129.20.204.0 | /22 | 129.20.197.60 (R2) |
| 129.20.208.0 | /22 | 129.20.197.36 (R4) |
| 129.20.212.0 | /22 | 129.20.197.60 (R2) |
| 0.0.0.0 | — | R6 (default route) |

**5.** $211 = 11010011_2$; AND with `11111100` → `11010000` = 208:
sub-subnet **`129.20.208.0`**, range **`129.20.208.1` →
`129.20.211.254`**, broadcast **`129.20.211.255`** — the given address
belongs to sub-subnet 4 even though its 3rd octet shows 211: the
binary reflex, one last time.

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>
