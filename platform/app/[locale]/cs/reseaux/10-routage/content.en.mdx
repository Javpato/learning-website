# Finding the path — tables, distance vectors and Dijkstra

A router never knows the whole path: it knows only the next step — the whole genius of routing lies in how that step is learned.

<Mission fil="Exercise 1 of the 2024-25 partiel (midterm) — distance vectors and poisoned reverse">
**Situation.** A router R is linked to three neighbours A, B, C by links
that cost **2** each. Its table reads, for four distant networks:
Network_A via B cost 8, Network_B via B cost 6, Network_C via A cost 5,

R's table become after each announcement — and which announcements must R
then emit on **each** of its three links if it applies "split horizon

micro-argument (compare, add the link, know when an announcement

round: what must be *kept quiet*, and how? **The contract.** This chapter
gives you 5 tools: table lookup, the distance-vector rule, count to
infinity and its antidote, Dijkstra's algorithm, and the Internet's
hierarchy. At the end, we fill in the partiel's three answers, line by
line.
</Mission>

## The battle plan

To learn the next step, we need 5 tools:

1. **looking up a table** — logical AND, longest prefix, default route: every datagram's gesture;
2. **distance vectors** — building the tables while listening only to your neighbours;
3. **count to infinity** — the distance-vector pathology, and the split horizon that treats it;
4. **link state and Dijkstra** — building the complete map, then computing on it;
5. **autonomous systems** — how the Internet assembles all this into a hierarchy.

## 1. Looking up the table — every datagram's gesture

Problem: for every <Terme id="datagramme-ip">IP datagram</Terme> received, the
router must decide on an output within a few microseconds. That gesture
does not "think": it looks up.

**Definition 1 (forwarding).** <Def id="acheminement">**forwarding**</Def>
is the local, fast gesture: look up the table and push
the datagram toward the next hop. **Routing** proper is
the other half — building and maintaining that table (sections 2–4). The
two live on different time scales: microseconds versus
seconds.

**Definition 2 (direct delivery).** <Def id="remise-directe">**direct
delivery**</Def> is delivery without a router: if destination and sender
share the same network prefix (the <Terme id="masque">subnet mask</Terme>
calculation of lesson 08), the frame goes straight to the target —
otherwise delivery is **indirect**: head for the gateway.

**Definition 3 (routing table).** The <Def id="table-de-routage">**routing
table**</Def> is the list (destination, mask, next hop,
interface): one row per known prefix — never one row per machine,
which is the whole virtue of hierarchical addressing.

**Definition 4 (longest-prefix rule).** When several rows
match, the <Def id="plus-long-prefixe">**longest-prefix
match**</Def> rule keeps the one whose mask is longest — the most
specific. A route to `132.174.110.0/23` beats a route to
`132.174.0.0/17`.

**Definition 5 (default route).** The <Def id="route-par-defaut">**default
route**</Def> is the row `0.0.0.0` (null mask): it matches
everything, so — by the longest-prefix rule — it serves only when nothing
else matches. It is the small site's "otherwise, send everything to the
provider".

**Method (looking up a table, MCQ style).**
1. For each row: the datagram's destination **AND** the row's
   mask; compare with the row's prefix.
2. Keep every row that matches; choose the longest
   mask.
3. No row? Default route. No default? Datagram destroyed,
   <Terme id="icmp">ICMP</Terme> "destination unreachable".

**Example 1 (the MCQ trap question).** Table: (destination
`128.96.40.0`, mask `255.255.255.128`, gateway R2). A datagram for
`128.96.40.129`: AND with the mask → `128.96.40.128` ≠ `128.96.40.0` — the
row does **not** match, the datagram does not go to R2. The last octet
129 sits in the upper half of the /25.

## 2. Distance vectors — learning from your neighbours

Problem: who fills the table? By hand (**static** routing) for
three routers, fine — but a changing network demands that the tables
maintain **themselves**: a dynamic routing protocol. The
first family asks only one thing of a router: talk to its neighbours.

**Definition 6 (distance-vector routing).** In
<Def id="vecteur-de-distances">**distance-vector routing**</Def>
(the distributed Bellman-Ford algorithm), each router periodically sends
**to its neighbours only** its vector — the (destination,
distance) list from its table. Whoever receives it updates.

**Method (the update rule — the one in the model answers).** On receiving
V's vector over the link of cost $c$, for each destination $d$
announced at distance $D_V(d)$:
1. compute the **candidate** $D_V(d) + c$;
2. adopt it if it is **strictly better** than the current distance —
   new next hop: V;
3. adopt it **even if it is worse, when V is already your next hop**
   toward $d$: V is your source of information, its new value replaces
   the old one (this is how a degradation propagates);
4. unknown destination? Create the row.

**Example 2 (the 2021 partiel, first exchanges).** Five routers, links
of cost 1. At $T_1$, A sends (B: 1, D: 1): B learns D at $1+1 = 2$ via
A, D learns B at 2 via A. At $T_2$, B sends its vector: A learns C at 2
and E at 2 via B; C learns D at 3 via B… Eight exchanges later, all
the tables are stable: the protocol has **converged**.

**Remark (RIP, the incarnation).** The RIP protocol applies exactly
this: a vector every 30 s, metric = hop count, and **16 means
infinity** (a destination at 16 is unreachable) — that tiny ceiling
sets up the next section.

## 3. Count to infinity — and split horizon

Problem: distance vectors announce only **distances**,
never **paths**. A router can therefore learn a route that —
without its knowing — passes back through itself. After a failure, this
blindness becomes a pathology.

**Definition 7 (count to infinity).** <Def id="comptage-a-l-infini">**count
to infinity**</Def> (the "bouncing effect" in the TD's model answer):
after a failure, two routers bounce a dead route between
them — each believing the other knows — inflating its cost step by step,
exchange after exchange, up to the ceiling
(16 in RIP).

**Example 3 (the TD 4 scenario).** Triangle A–B (3), B–C (2), A–C (8).
C reaches A via B for $2 + 3 = 5$. The A–B link breaks: B sets A to ∞… but
C, not yet in the know, announces "A at 5" — and B concludes: "A via C at
$5 + 2 = 7$"! Datagrams for A bounce between B and C while
the following announcements correct things slowly: 7, then 9 at C… until
the direct route at 8 takes over. With a ceiling at 16, a
true cut takes **minutes** to be said.

**Definition 8 (split horizon).** <Def id="horizon-partage">**split
horizon**</Def> forbids announcing a route **on the
link it was learned from**; the "poisoned reverse"
variant announces it on that link anyway, but at **infinite cost** — an
explicit denial rather than a silence.

**Why it cures Example 3:** C would never have said "A at 5" *to
B*, since its route goes through B — it would have told it "A: ∞". B has
no mirage left: it switches directly to whatever remains (nothing, then
the route at 8 once C discovers and announces it).

**Remark (blind spot).** Split horizon kills **two**-router
loops; loops of three or more can survive (A believes via B,
B via C, C via A — nobody talks to its source). It is a mitigation,
not a theorem — the true way out is to change algorithm family.

## 4. Link state — the full map, then Dijkstra

Problem: all the previous miseries stem from one cause: the
router reasons on **summaries** (distances) without ever seeing the
map. The second family takes the opposite tack: give everyone the entire
map.

**Definition 9 (link-state routing).** In
<Def id="etat-de-liens">**link-state routing**</Def>, each router:
(1) discovers its neighbours (HELLO packets), (2) builds an **LSP** (Link
State Packet: its identity, its neighbours, the costs), (3) **floods it to
the whole network** — periodically and at every change, (4) rebuilds
the complete map from all the LSPs received, (5) locally computes
its shortest paths on it.

**Definition 10 (flooding).** The <Def id="inondation">**flooding**</Def>
mechanism broadcasts the LSPs while knowing no routes: each router re-emits
the received packet **on all its lines except the incoming one**, with LSP
numbering to discard duplicates and stale copies — otherwise the broadcast
would run away.

**Definition 11 (Dijkstra's algorithm).** <Def id="algorithme-de-dijkstra">**Dijkstra's
algorithm**</Def> computes the shortest paths from a source on
the map. Each node carries a **(cost, via)** label, at first
*provisional*; the smallest provisional label becomes *permanent* at
each round.

**Method (running Dijkstra — the recipe from the exam-prep sheet).**
1. Label the source (0, −), permanent; it is the active node.
2. For each neighbour of the active node: if "active node's cost + link"
   beats its label, relabel it (cost, active).
3. Among **all** the provisional labels in the graph, the smallest
   becomes permanent: new active node.
4. Start again at move 2 until exhaustion.
5. The table is read by **walking back up the vias**: the next hop toward $d$
   is the first node after the source on the chain of vias.

**Example 4 (the preparation past paper).** From D, on the seven-router
graph: first labels A(5, D), B(2, D), F(3, D); B becomes
permanent and relabels C(3, B), E(4, B); then C, which improves A(4, C);
then A, E, F, G(5, C). D's final table: A via B cost 4, B via B 2,
C via B 3, E via B 4, **F via F 3**, G via B 5 — five routes out of six
go through B.

**Remark (the duel of the two families).** The course's comparison:

| | Distance vectors | Link state |
| --- | --- | --- |
| each router sees | summarised distances | the complete map |
| talks to | its neighbours | the whole network (flooding) |
| computation | distributed additions | local Dijkstra |
| memory, CPU | low | high |
| convergence | slow (bounces possible) | fast, no count to infinity |
| incarnation | RIP | OSPF (areas, backbone 0, HELLO 10 s) |

## 5. Autonomous systems — the Internet as a hierarchy

Problem: neither RIP nor OSPF would hold at the scale of the world — and
nobody wants to let a competitor compute their internal routes.

**Definition 12 (autonomous system).** An <Def id="systeme-autonome">**autonomous
system (AS)**</Def> is a domain under a single administrative authority
(an operator, a campus…). **Inside**, an IGP (RIP, OSPF) optimises
freely; **between** ASes, an EGP — BGP — exchanges aggregated routes
according to policies (commercial ones, not only metrics). ASes are
classed as *stub* (a single exit), *multihomed* (several exits, no
transit) and *transit* (the operators, who relay other people's traffic).

**Remark.** It is the <Terme id="agregation">route aggregation</Terme> of
lesson 08 that makes BGP possible: you never announce machines, always
summarised prefixes.

## Worked example — the 2024-25 partiel, line by line

Let us solve the mission. Links R–A, R–B, R–C: cost 2 each.

**Step 1 — process DV₁, received from B: [(A, 4), (B, 7), (C, 4)].**
Candidates: $4+2 = 6$, $7+2 = 9$, $4+2 = 6$.
Network_A: $6 < 8$ → **(B, 6)**. Network_B: $9 > 6$… but the current next
hop is **B itself** → move 3: **(B, 9)**. Network_C:
$6 > 5$ → unchanged. Network_D: not announced → unchanged.

*Why?* Method of section 2: two classic improvements and —
the mark-scheme point — an **accepted degradation** because it comes from
the route's source.

**Step 2 — process DV₂, received from A: [(B, 2), (C, 3), (E, 7)].**
Candidates: $4$, $5$, $9$.
Network_B: $4 < 9$ → **(A, 4)**. Network_C: $5 = 5$ and the announcer is
already the next hop → **(A, 5)** (confirmed). Network_E: unknown →
**created (A, 9)**. Final table: A (B, 6); B (A, 4); C (A, 5);
D (C, 2); E (A, 9).

*Why?* Moves 2 and 4: an improvement, a confirmation, a
discovery — R's table now holds five networks.

**Step 3 — the three "split horizon with poisoned reverse" announcements.** On
each link, R announces everything — but **poisons** (∞) the routes learned
over that link:

| To A (routes via A → ∞) | To B (via B → ∞) | To C (via C → ∞) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A: 6 | A: **∞** | A: 6 |
| B: **∞** | B: 4 | B: 4 |
| C: **∞** | C: 5 | C: 5 |
| D: 2 | D: 2 | D: **∞** |
| E: **∞** | E: 9 | E: 9 |

*Why?* Definition 8: to each neighbour, R explicitly denies the
routes that neighbour is the source of — three different vectors, one per
link, exactly the 1.5-mark answer on the script.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**In Step 1, why not keep Network_B at 6 "while waiting for better"?**

Because the value 6 no longer exists anywhere: it came from B, which
now announces 7. Keeping it means routing on a memory — and
building loops as soon as reality diverges. The rule is brutal
but sound: your source speaks, you pass it on (Method, move 3); if a
better path exists elsewhere, its announcement will prove it — which is
exactly what DV₂ does three lines later (B falls back to 4 via A).

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Refusing a worse announcement coming from your next hop.** The rule
(Method §2, move 3) requires adopting it: it is what takes
Network_B from 6 to 9 in the mission. Forgetting it leaves the table
frozen on a ghost value — and costs the mark-scheme line.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting to add the link cost.** The candidate is always
"announced **+ link**": the mission's vectors give 4, 7, 4 — the
candidates are 6, 9, 6. Write the addition in the margin, systematically.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confusing split horizon with total silence.** Without the poison, you
**omit** the route on its link of origin; with poisoned reverse, you
announce it **at infinity** (Definition 8). The partiel asked for the
poisoned version: ∞ written in black and white, not empty cells.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Making permanent the last node touched, not the smallest.** Move 3
of Dijkstra picks the smallest provisional label **in the entire
graph** — not in the active node's neighbourhood. It is the mistake that
swaps F and C in Example 4.

</Pitfall>

## Summary — what you can do now

<KeyResults title="The two engines of routing, and their exam moves">

| If the paper asks… | You do… | Tool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "where does this packet go?" | AND with the mask row by row, longest prefix, else default | Method §1 |
| "table after receiving the vector" | candidate = announced + link; better → adopt; current source → adopt even if worse | Method §2 |
| "what happens after the failure?" | bounce: the dead route revives via a third party, +cost at each exchange | Definition 7 |
| "split horizon with poisoned reverse" | one vector PER link, routes learned from that link announced at ∞ | Definition 8 |
| "run Dijkstra" | (cost, via) labels; smallest provisional → permanent; table by walking back up the vias | Method §4 |
| "RIP or OSPF?" | vectors (30 s, 16 = ∞) versus link state (LSP, areas, backbone 0) | Remark §4 |

**The reflex that saves you:** on every table row, write the calculation
("$4 + 2 = 6 < 8$") — the mark scheme pays for the reasoning, not the
value.

</KeyResults>
