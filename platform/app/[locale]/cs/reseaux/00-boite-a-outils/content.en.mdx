## 1. Bits, bytes — and the false friend k

**Reflex: always check the unit before you compute.** Half of all
partiel (midterm) errors are unit errors, not reasoning errors.

**Definition 1 (bit).** The <Def id="bit">**bit**</Def> (binary digit) is the
smallest unit of information: a 0 or a 1. **Data rates** are measured in
bits per second (bit/s).

**Definition 2 (byte).** A <Def id="octet">**byte (octet)**</Def> is a group of
**8 bits**. **Sizes** (messages, headers, files) are counted in
bytes. In French: *octet* — hence the "o" and "B" you will see side by side.

**The module's rule:** in networking, prefixes are **decimal** —

$$
1 \text{ kbit/s} = 10^3 \text{ bit/s}, \qquad 1 \text{ Mbit/s} = 10^6, \qquad 1 \text{ Gbit/s} = 10^9.
$$

Never $2^{10} = 1024$: that value (Ki, Mi) belongs to memory, not
to links. A problem statement that says "a 30 KB file" means
$30 \times 10^3$ bytes $= 240\,000$ bits — the ×8 conversion is the most
forgotten step in exam scripts.

**Example 1.** "A 10 KB file on a 10 Mbit/s link":
$10 \times 10^3 \times 8 = 8 \times 10^4$ bits, divided by $10^7$ bit/s
→ $8$ ms. Both conversions (×8, powers of 10) happen **before** the
division.

## 2. Binary — reading and writing

**Reflex: an address, a mask, an offset — everything can be checked in base 2.**

**Definition 3 (binary notation).** In <Def id="notation-binaire">**binary
notation**</Def> a number is written in base 2: each position is worth a
power of 2, from $2^7 = 128$ down to $2^0 = 1$ for one byte.

**Method (decimal → binary, on one byte).** Subtract the powers of 2
going down: $174 = 128 + 32 + 8 + 4 + 2$, so

```
174 = 10101110
      128 64 32 16 8 4 2 1
      1   0  1  0  1 1 1 0
```

**Method (binary → decimal).** Add up the positions holding a 1:
`01101110` $= 64+32+8+4+2 = 110$.

**Example 2.** The mask patterns to recognise at a glance (a
run of 1s then a run of 0s — never a 0 in the middle of the 1s):

| binary | decimal | binary | decimal |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `10000000` | 128 | `11111000` | 248 |
| `11000000` | 192 | `11111100` | 252 |
| `11100000` | 224 | `11111110` | 254 |
| `11110000` | 240 | `11111111` | 255 |

**Remark.** `251 = 11111011` has a 0 in the middle: it is **not** a possible
mask pattern — it is even a classic QCM trap.

## 3. Hexadecimal — the language of frames

**Reflex: 1 hex digit = 4 bits; 2 digits = 1 byte.**

**Definition 4 (hexadecimal notation).** In
<Def id="notation-hexadecimale">**hexadecimal notation**</Def> one writes in
base 16, with the digits 0–9 then A = 10, B = 11, C = 12, D = 13, E = 14,
F = 15. It appears wherever raw bytes are displayed: hardware
addresses, frame contents, IPv6 addresses.

**Method (hex → binary).** Translate each digit into 4 bits,
independently: `2B` → `0010 1011`. In the other direction, group by 4
starting from the right.

**Example 3 (the one from TD 5).** The address "`2B 28 F3 80`" reads byte by
byte: `2B` $= 2 \times 16 + 11 = 43$, `28` $= 40$, `F3` $= 243$,
`80` $= 128$ — that is `43.40.243.128`. Three lines of computation, one mark
earned.

### Mini-exercises

1. `81 68 FE 06` in dotted decimal?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $81_{16}=129$, $68_{16}=104$, $\mathrm{FE}_{16}=254$, $06_{16}=6$:
   `129.104.254.6` — the address hidden in the ARP frame of TD 5.

   </Collapsible>

2. How many bytes does `AA AA AA AA AA AA AA AB` represent?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   8 groups of 2 digits = 8 bytes. (That is the preamble of an Ethernet
   frame followed by its start delimiter.)

   </Collapsible>

## 4. Powers of 2 and the logarithm — "how many bits?"

**Reflex: "at least N values" is paid for in bits: the smallest $b$ such
that $2^b \ge N$.**

The table to carry in your head:

| $2^1$ | $2^2$ | $2^3$ | $2^4$ | $2^5$ | $2^6$ | $2^7$ | $2^8$ | $2^{10}$ | $2^{16}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 | 64 | 128 | 256 | 1024 | 65 536 |

**Method (the smallest b such that 2ᵇ ≥ N).** Trap N between two
powers: $32 < 62 \le 64$ gives $b = 6$. This is also
$b = \lceil \log_2 N \rceil$ — and one day the same computation will tell
you in how many round trips TCP doubles its window up to a target.

**Example 4.** 62 subnets → 6 bits; 29 → 5 bits; 12 hosts →
4 bits ($2^3 = 8 < 12$, careful); 2000 → 11 bits ($2^{10} = 1024 < 2000 \le 2048$).

### Mini-exercise

1. How many bits to number at least 40 subnets? 200 hosts?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $32 < 40 \le 64$ → 6 bits; $128 < 200 \le 256$ → 8 bits.

   </Collapsible>

## 5. Orders of magnitude for time — and reading a timing diagram

**Reflex: milliseconds for links, microseconds for bits,
seconds for humans.**

$$
1 \text{ s} = 10^3 \text{ ms} = 10^6 \text{ µs} = 10^9 \text{ ns}.
$$

Three numbers to know: light (and electricity in a cable)
covers roughly **200 000 to 300 000 km/s** — that is **5 µs per kilometre** in
coaxial cable; a geostationary satellite imposes **~250 ms** of round
trip; a 1000-byte frame at 10 Mbit/s occupies the cable for **0.8 ms**.

**Reading a timing diagram.** Exercises draw time along an axis (downwards
or to the right) with one lifeline per machine. Three shapes keep
coming back:

- a **rectangle** on a machine's line = it is transmitting for that duration
  (transmission time);
- a **slope** between two lines = the signal is travelling (propagation
  delay);
- an **empty gap** = someone is waiting (acknowledgement, queue, token…).

Being able to name those three pieces is already being able to solve half of
the delay exercises — lesson 02 turns this into a complete method.

### Mini-exercise

1. A 6600 km link, signal at 300 000 km/s: propagation delay?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $6600 / 300\,000 = 0{,}022$ s $= 22$ ms. (That is the link from the 2023
   partiel — you will see it again.)

   </Collapsible>
