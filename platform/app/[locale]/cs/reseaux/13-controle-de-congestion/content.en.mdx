# When the network saturates — slow start, AIMD and losses

<LessonMeta id="cs-net-c13" />

<Toc depth={2} />

<Accroche>
Nobody sends an invoice when a router overflows: TCP must guess the network's capacity by testing it — gently on the way up, brutally on the way down.
</Accroche>

<Mission fil="The exercise from the course — how long to fill a gigabit link?">
**Situation.** A TCP connection starts on a path at **1 Gbit/s** with a
round trip of **20 ms**, in segments of **1000 bytes**. We aim for a
cruising window equal to **80%** of the volume that fills the pipe.
**Question.** Starting from a single segment in flight, how many round
trips — and how many milliseconds — does TCP's start-up need to reach
this window? **The obstacle.** Unlike lesson 12, nobody announces the
limit here: the network does not publish its capacity, it makes you
discover it by dropping packets when you overdo it — so you need a
**probing strategy**, and to know how to time it. **The contract.** This
chapter gives you 4 tools: the congestion window, slow start and its
threshold, AIMD avoidance, and the fast recoveries (Tahoe versus Reno).
By the end, we put numbers on the start-up: 11 round trips,
0.22 seconds.
</Mission>

*Already comfortable with this? [Go straight to TD 6 (tutorial sheet)](../td-6/),
exercises 9 and 10.*

## The battle plan

To probe without drowning, we need 4 tools:

1. **the congestion window** — the limit the sender imposes on itself, next to the receiver's;
2. **slow start** — doubling cautiously up to the threshold;
3. **congestion avoidance** — additive growth, multiplicative decrease: AIMD;
4. **the fast recoveries** — reading duplicated acknowledgements to repair without smashing everything.

## 1. Congestion — the third party in the conversation

Problem: lesson 12 protects the receiver (rwnd). But between the two,
there is the **network**: routers whose <Terme id="file-d-attente">queue</Terme>s
are finite. Too much traffic → full queues → dropped packets →
retransmissions → **even more** traffic: the vicious circle.

**Definition 1 (congestion).** <Def id="congestion">**Congestion**</Def>
is the saturation of the network's queues: delays explode first, then
losses come one after another. In 1986, the young Internet lived through
it at scale — links collapsed to a tiny fraction of their useful
throughput — and Van Jacobson's answer (1988) became the heart of TCP.

**Definition 2 (congestion window).** The
<Def id="fenetre-de-congestion">**congestion window (cwnd)**</Def>
(counted in MSS) is the limit the **sender** imposes on itself from its
estimate of the network's state. The window actually used is

$$
\text{wnd} = \min(\text{rwnd}, \text{cwnd}) :
$$

the more cautious of the two parties always wins.

**Remark (the signal).** The network does not speak: TCP infers. A loss
(timer or duplicated acknowledgements) means "too much", smooth
acknowledgements mean "still some margin". Everything that follows is a
policy for reading this binary signal.

### Mini-exercise

1. cwnd = 40 MSS, rwnd = 12 000 bytes, MSS = 1000. How many segments in flight?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $\min(12, 40) = 12$ segments (Definition 2): the receiver is the
   bound, congestion is not even the limiting factor — always check
   both.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Slow start — doubling up to the threshold

Problem: at the first segment of a connection, the sender knows
**nothing**: the pipe may accept 2 segments, or maybe 2000. Sending the
full rwnd window at once risks an instant traffic jam; sending one
segment per RTT wastes minutes.

**Definition 3 (slow start).** The <Def id="demarrage-lent">**slow
start**</Def> begins at cwnd = 1 MSS and adds **1 MSS per
acknowledgement received**. Since every fully acknowledged window
returns as many ACKs as it contained segments, cwnd **doubles every
round trip**: 1, 2, 4, 8… "Slow" only describes the starting point —
the growth is exponential.

**Definition 4 (ss_thresh threshold).** The <Def id="seuil-de-demarrage">**ss_thresh
threshold**</Def> marks the frontier of aggressive probing: as long as
cwnd < ss_thresh, slow start; beyond, we switch to avoidance
(section 3). At each loss detection, the threshold is reset to
**half the in-flight window** (at least 2 MSS) — the memory of the
last capacity observed.

**Proposition 1 (the cost of the start-up).** Starting from 1 MSS,
reaching a window of $W$ segments takes

$$
\lceil \log_2 W \rceil \text{ aller-retours}.
$$

**Why?** After $k$ round trips, cwnd $= 2^k$; we need the first
$k$ such that $2^k \ge W$ — that is the definition of the base-2
logarithm, rounded up (the toolbox's <Terme id="notation-binaire">binary
notation</Terme> move). $\blacksquare$

**Example 1.** Reaching 16 MSS: 1 → 2 → 4 → 8 → 16, i.e.
$\log_2 16 = 4$ round trips after the first send.

### Mini-exercise

1. How many slow-start round trips to reach 100 MSS?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $2^6 = 64 < 100 \le 128 = 2^7$ → **7** round trips (Proposition 1).

   </Collapsible>

## 3. Congestion avoidance — AIMD, the sawtooth

Problem: doubling quickly finds the danger zone — then it becomes the
murder weapon: doubling a window that is already nearly right means
throwing hundreds of segments at once. Near the limit, you must advance
with measured steps.

**Definition 5 (congestion avoidance).** Above the threshold,
<Def id="evitement-de-congestion">**congestion avoidance**</Def> takes
over: cwnd grows by $1/\text{cwnd}$ MSS per acknowledgement — roughly
**+1 MSS per round trip**. Linear growth: still probing, but drop by
drop.

**Definition 6 (AIMD).** <Def id="aimd">**AIMD**</Def> (Additive
Increase, Multiplicative Decrease) is this complete policy: climb by 1
per RTT, **divide by 2** at every loss. On a graph of cwnd over time, it
draws TCP's characteristic sawtooth — and it is what makes several
connections converge towards a fair share of the link.

**Example 2 (the timeout case).** Loss detected by **timeout** (total
silence — the gravest signal): ss_thresh ← max(in-flight window / 2,
2 MSS), then **cwnd ← 1 MSS** and back to slow start. The sawtooth falls
to the floor, the threshold's memory avoids redoing the whole climb in
blind exponential.

**Remark (why divide by 2, and not subtract).** An additive decrease
(−1) would respond too weakly to a signal announcing a **collective**
overflow; the multiplicative decrease frees room in proportion to what
was being occupied. The asymmetry (+1 / ÷2) is the price of the whole
Internet's stability.

### Mini-exercise

1. cwnd = 24 MSS (avoidance), a timeout occurs. What are ss_thresh and cwnd just after, then cwnd three RTTs later?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   ss_thresh = 12, cwnd = 1 (Example 2); then 2, 4, 8 in slow start —
   still under the threshold, the doubling continues towards 12 before
   switching to +1.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. The fast recoveries — Tahoe versus Reno

Problem: the timer is a slow judge (lesson 12: RTO well beyond the RTT).
Yet the receiver is already sending a faster clue: **duplicated**
<Terme id="acquittement-cumulatif">cumulative acknowledgement</Terme>s —
"I am still missing the same byte" — for every segment that arrives
above the gap.

**Definition 7 (fast retransmit).** The
<Def id="retransmission-rapide">**fast retransmit**</Def> declares the
loss at **3 duplicated acknowledgements** (four identical ACKs in all):
the missing segment is retransmitted **without waiting for the timer**.
Three, so as not to confuse a mere reordering (one or two duplicates are
enough to produce it) with a real loss.

**Definition 8 (fast recovery).** The
<Def id="recuperation-rapide">**fast recovery**</Def> (TCP **Reno**)
draws the consequence: duplicated ACKs prove that segments are **still
getting through** — the network has not collapsed. Instead of falling
back to 1: ss_thresh ← cwnd/2, then cwnd restarts **from the threshold**
in avoidance. TCP **Tahoe**, the ancestor, treated the 3 duplicates like
a timeout: back to 1 and slow start.

**Example 3.** cwnd = 16 MSS, 3 duplicated ACKs: Tahoe → cwnd = 1
(climb back: 1, 2, 4, 8 then +1); Reno → cwnd = 8 directly, then 9,
10… On a path with regular isolated losses, the throughput gap is
considerable — that is the whole point of the duel of variants.

**Remark (the course's panorama).** The lineage continues: NewReno
(better on multiple losses), SACK (selective acknowledgements — the
missing vocabulary of lesson 11), Vegas (reads the **delays** rather
than the losses), CUBIC (the current standard on servers), ECN (the
router marks instead of dropping). The principles of this chapter remain
the foundation of them all.

## Worked example — the course's gigabit, timed

Let us unroll the mission: 1 Gbit/s, RTT = 20 ms, MSS = 1000 bytes,
target = 80% of the pipe.

**Step 1 — the volume that fills the pipe.**

$$
\text{BDP} = 10^9 \times 0{,}02 = 2 \times 10^7 \text{ bits} = 2{,}5 \times 10^6 \text{ octets}.
$$

*Why?* It is the <Terme id="produit-delai-bande-passante">bandwidth-delay
product</Terme> of lesson 12 — congestion is reasoned about in the same
units as the flow.

**Step 2 — the target in segments.** 80% of the BDP:
$0{,}8 \times 2{,}5 \times 10^6 = 2 \times 10^6$ bytes, i.e.

$$
W = \frac{2 \times 10^6}{1000} = 2000 \text{ segments}.
$$

*Why?* cwnd is counted in MSS (Definition 2): convert before counting
the doublings, otherwise the logarithm is off by a factor of 1000.

**Step 3 — counting the doublings.** $2^{10} = 1024 < 2000 \le 2048 = 2^{11}$:

$$
\lceil \log_2 2000 \rceil = 11 \text{ aller-retours}.
$$

*Why?* Proposition 1 — and it is the toolbox's "how many bits for N?"
move, recycled as is.

**Step 4 — the stopwatch.** $11 \times 20$ ms $= 0{,}22$ s.

*Why?* Each doubling costs exactly one RTT (Definition 3):
the "slow" start fills a gigabit in a fifth of a second —
the exponential wears its name badly.

**Step 5 — and then?** At 2000 segments, if the threshold is still
above, the doubling would continue — dangerous if the real capacity is
close; it is the threshold's job (Definition 4) to stop the exponential
at the last safe estimate, and avoidance's job (+1 per RTT,
Definition 5) to look for the rest drop by drop.

*Why?* The mission asked for the start-up's stopwatch; the exam, for
its part, often asks for the sequel — know how to tell both regimes
around the threshold.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**Does the result of 11 RTTs depend on the link's rate?**

Only indirectly: the rate fixes the **target** (Step 1's BDP), but the
probing rhythm, for its part, depends only on the RTT — one doubling per
round trip, whatever the link. That is why paths with large RTTs
(satellites) suffer twice over: a bigger target **and** slower
doublings.

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**"Slow start is slow."** It **starts** low but doubles every RTT
(Definition 3): 2000 segments in 11 round trips. The slowness is in the
name, not in the growth — and the exam loves making you time the
exponential.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Doubling per ACK instead of doubling per RTT.** The fine-grained rule
is "+1 MSS per ACK"; it is the **whole acknowledged window** that
produces the doubling per round trip. In an exam computation, we count
in RTTs — but know how to justify where the doubling comes from.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting min(rwnd, cwnd).** A cramped receiver can throttle the
connection well before congestion does (mini-exercise §1): always
compare the two windows before concluding "it's the network".

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confusing the two loss detections.** Timeout = total silence →
cwnd = 1 and slow start (both variants). 3 duplicated ACKs = the path
is still alive → Reno restarts from the threshold (Definition 8), only
Tahoe falls back to 1. The marking scheme always distinguishes the two
cases.

</Pitfall>

## Summary — what you can do now

| Event | TCP's reaction | Tool |
| --- | --- | --- |

| "how many RTTs to W?" | ⌈log₂ W⌉ — convert W into MSS first | Proposition 1 |
| cwnd ≥ ss_thresh | +1 MSS per RTT (avoidance, additive growth) | Definition 5 |

| 3 duplicated ACKs | immediate retransmission; Reno restarts from the threshold, Tahoe from 1 | Definitions 7–8 |
