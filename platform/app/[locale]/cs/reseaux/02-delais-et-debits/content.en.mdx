# How long does a file take? Delays, rates and timing diagrams

<LessonMeta id="cs-net-c02" />

<Toc depth={2} />

<Accroche>
A transfer has only two enemies: pushing the bits into the cable, and waiting for them to cross it — the whole art is never confusing the two.
</Accroche>

<Mission fil="The 2023 partiel (midterm), exercise 1 — 6 marks of timing diagram">
**Situation.** A sends B a **30 KB** file, cut into two pieces
p1 = **10 KB** and p2 = **20 KB**, shipped one after the other. Between them, a
device R that waits until it has received everything before re-emitting; the
A–R leg carries **10 Mbit/s**, the R–B leg **5 Mbit/s**, and each leg
is **6600 km** long (signal at 300 000 km/s). **Question.** What gap separates
the arrival of the last bits of p1 and of p2 at B? What is the total
transfer time, and what rate did the file actually get?
**The obstacle.** Four durations of different natures get mixed together —
pushing the bits, letting them travel, waiting at R — and adding them up
haphazardly gives the wrong answer every time. **The contract.** This chapter
gives you 4 tools: the bit rate, the two elementary times, the timing
diagram, and the effective throughput. By the end, we solve the exercise to
the millisecond.
</Mission>

## The battle plan

To run the stopwatch properly, we need 4 tools:

1. **the bit rate** — what a link is worth, in bits per second;
2. **the two elementary times** — transmission (pushing) and propagation (travelling), never to be confused;
3. **the timing diagram** — the figure that assembles these times across a store-and-forward node, queue included;
4. **the effective throughput** — what the transfer really got, end to end.

## 1. The bit rate — what a link is worth

Problem: "this link is fast" cannot be computed. We need a quantity.

**Definition 1 (bit rate).** The <Def id="debit-binaire">**bit
rate**</Def> $D$ of a link is the number of bits it transmits per
second, in bit/s — with **decimal** prefixes: 1 Mbit/s $= 10^6$
bit/s, never $2^{20}$.

**Example 1.** The mission's links: $D_1 = 10^7$ bit/s (A–R) and
$D_2 = 5 \times 10^6$ bit/s (R–B). The file: 30 KB
$= 30 \times 10^3 \times 8 = 2{,}4 \times 10^5$ bits — the
<Terme id="octet">byte (octet)</Terme> → <Terme id="bit">bit</Terme> conversion
is done right at the start, once and for all.

**Remark.** The rate is a property of the **link**, not of the transfer: the
mission will show it — the file will get neither 10 nor 5 Mbit/s.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $1/10^7 = 10^{-7}$ s $= 0{,}1$ µs. The rate is the inverse of one bit's
   duration.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Transmission and propagation — pushing is not travelling

Problem: sending a packet mixes two physically independent
phenomena — the link moves bits through at its own pace, and each bit takes
time to travel down the cable. Confusing them is mistake number 1 of this
chapter.

**Definition 2 (transmission time).** The
<Def id="temps-de-transmission">**transmission time**</Def> of a unit
of $L$ bits on a link of rate $D$ is

$$
t_t = \frac{L}{D}.
$$

It is the time to **push** all the bits into the cable. It does **not**
depend on the length of the cable.

**Example 2 (the one from the course).** 10 000 bits at 10 Mbit/s:
$t_t = 10^4/10^7 = 1$ ms — whether the cable is 3 metres or 3000 km long.

**Definition 3 (propagation delay).** The
<Def id="temps-de-propagation">**propagation delay**</Def> of a link of
length $d$ where the signal travels at speed $v$ is

$$
t_p = \frac{d}{v}.
$$

It is one bit's **travel** time. It does **not** depend on the rate.

**Example 3.** The course's orders of magnitude: about **5 µs/km** in
coaxial cable ($v = 200\,000$ km/s); a geostationary satellite imposes
**~250 ms** round trip. For the mission: $t_p = 6600/300\,000 = 22$ ms
per leg.

**Definition 4 (transfer time).** The
<Def id="temps-de-transfert">**transfer time**</Def> of a unit over
**one** link is the time between its first bit sent and its last bit
received:

$$
t_r = t_t + t_p.
$$

**Remark (the right exam reflex).** When a duration surprises you,
ask yourself which of the two terms dominates: on a LAN, $t_p$ is
negligible and $t_t$ rules; by satellite, it is the other way round. Problem
statements play on this contrast constantly — and some QCM (multiple-choice)
questions slip in a useless propagation speed just to tempt you.

## 3. The timing diagram — assembling the times across R

Problem: with an intermediate node, the durations do not add up in
one single formula — they **interlock**, and some overlap. We
need a figure that makes the interlocking computable.

**Method (building a timing diagram).** Three lifelines (A, R, B), time
running downwards; then, for each packet, four instants in this
order:
1. **start of emission** at A: as soon as the A–R link is free;
2. **complete arrival at R**: end of emission $+\;t_p$ of leg 1 —
   this is the <Terme id="store-and-forward">store-and-forward</Terme> assumption:
   R can do nothing before the **last** bit;
3. **start of re-emission** by R: as soon as the packet is complete **and**
   the R–B link is free;
4. **complete arrival at B**: end of re-emission $+\;t_p$ of leg 2.

Problem: step 3 hides a possible wait — the output link may be
busy with the previous packet.

**Definition 5 (queue).** The <Def id="file-d-attente">**queue**</Def>
of a node is the memory where units ready to leave wait their turn,
served in **FIFO** order (first come, first served). The time
spent queueing is the journey's fourth delay — the one that will explode
with congestion (lesson 13).

**Example 4 (the exercise from the 2024-25 partiel).** Three packets reach
a router over three different links and all leave over one link at
5 Mbit/s: the first to arrive leaves at once, the other two **pile up**
and their departures follow one another in arrival order. The whole
difficulty of the exercise lies in step 3 of the Method.

**Proposition 1 (pipeline effect, identical links and packets).** For a
message cut into $n$ equal packets of transmission time $t_t$ each,
sent across $k$ store-and-forward nodes (hence $k+1$ identical links,
propagation neglected):

$$
T = (k + n)\, t_t .
$$

**Why?** The first packet takes $(k+1)\,t_t$ to reach B (one
transmission per link). After that, the steady state is established: all the
links work in parallel, and B receives one complete packet **every**
$t_t$ — and $n-1$ remain. Total: $(k+1)\,t_t + (n-1)\,t_t = (k+n)\,t_t$.
$\blacksquare$

**Remark (blind spot).** The formula demands **identical** links and
neglected headers. Different links: the **slowest** link sets the
pace (that is the mission's case — hence the full timing diagram rather
than the formula). Headers counted: each packet carries $h$ extra bits,
and cutting too fine costs — exercise cs-net-td4-01 derives the
optimal size $p = \sqrt{xh/k}$.

### Mini-exercise

1. A 6000-bit message, 2 intermediate nodes, identical 1 Mbit/s links, in 3 packets: total time?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $t_t = 2000/10^6 = 2$ ms per packet; $T = (k+n)\,t_t = (2+3) \times 2 = 10$ ms
   — against $(2+1) \times 6 = 18$ ms as a single block. Proposition 1
   saves 8 ms.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. The effective throughput — what the transfer really got

Problem: the file crossed a 10 Mbit/s link then a 5 Mbit/s one — but
neither of those two numbers describes what the user experienced.

**Definition 6 (effective throughput).** The <Def id="debit-effectif">**effective
throughput**</Def> of a transfer is

$$
D_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\text{quantité utile transférée}}{\text{temps total du transfert}} .
$$

It is the result of an **end-to-end measurement**, never a property of the
links — and above all **never** the average of their rates.

**Example 5.** If 240 000 bits take 100 ms in total, the effective throughput
is $2{,}4 \times 10^5 / 0{,}1 = 2{,}4$ Mbit/s: less than the slower of the
two links, because of store-and-forward and the propagations.

**Remark (blind spot).** The effective throughput depends on the cut: more
packets → more overlap → shorter total time → higher effective
throughput, up to the slow link's limit (and at the cost of headers). The
"throughput" is therefore not a constant of the path.

## Worked example — the 2023 partiel, to the millisecond

Let us solve the mission. Data: p1 $= 8 \times 10^4$ bits, p2 $= 1{,}6 \times 10^5$
bits, $D_1 = 10^7$, $D_2 = 5 \times 10^6$ bit/s,
$t_p = 22$ ms per leg.

**Step 1 — the four transmission times.**

$$
t_t^{A\to R}(p_1) = 8\ \text{ms}, \quad t_t^{A\to R}(p_2) = 16\ \text{ms}, \quad t_t^{R\to B}(p_1) = 16\ \text{ms}, \quad t_t^{R\to B}(p_2) = 32\ \text{ms}.
$$

*Why?* Definition 2, four times over: each packet meets each
link, and the R–B link, twice as slow, doubles each duration.

**Step 2 — p1's journey.** Emission 0 → 8 ms; complete at R at
$8 + 22 = 30$ ms; R re-emits it from 30 to 46 ms; complete at B at
$46 + 22 = \mathbf{68}$ ms.

*Why?* The Method's four instants, in order — store-and-forward
forbids R from starting before 30 ms.

**Step 3 — p2's journey, queue included.** A emits it from 8 to 24 ms
(the A–R link frees up at 8 ms); complete at R at $24 + 22 = 46$ ms; the
R–B link frees up at exactly 46 ms — no wait here — R emits it from 46
to 78 ms; complete at B at $78 + 22 = \mathbf{100}$ ms.

*Why?* Step 3 of the Method: the departure from R is
$\max(\text{arrivée}, \text{lien libre}) = \max(46, 46)$. The requested gap
between the last bits is $100 - 68 = \mathbf{32}$ ms.

**Step 4 — total time and effective throughput.** The transfer finishes at
**100 ms**. Effective throughput:

$$
D_{\text{eff}} = \frac{2{,}4 \times 10^5}{0{,}100} = 2{,}4\ \text{Mbit/s}.
$$

*Why?* Definition 6: useful bits over total time. Not 10, not 5, not
their average 7.5 — a real exam script lost the mark exactly there.

**Step 5 — what if we cut finer?** In $n$ equal packets, the start
stays dominated by the slow link: the time tends toward
$t_p + t_t^{A\to R}(\text{1 paquet}) + \text{(toutes les transmissions R–B)} + t_p \approx 44 + 48 = 92$ ms, that is $\approx 2{,}6$ Mbit/s at best.

*Why?* The Remark after Proposition 1: the slowest link (48 ms of
incompressible transmission) rules; cutting only claws back the
time lost to non-overlap — and the headers would eventually take that
gain back.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**Why does p2 not wait in R's queue, when R was busy
re-emitting p1?**

Because the two durations cancel out exactly here: p2 finishes arriving
at 46 ms, the precise instant when R finishes emitting p1. Change a single
datum — a shorter p2, or a slower R–B link — and a wait appears:
step 3 of the Method arbitrates, not a general rule.

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Averaging the rates.** "(10 + 5)/2 = 7.5 Mbit/s": written on a real
exam script, paid zero. Effective throughput is **always** computed by
dividing the useful bits by the measured total time (Definition 6).

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the ×8.** A 30 KB file is 240 000 bits. The whole
chain of computation is wrong if that first conversion is missing — do it
before anything else, units written out.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Counting one propagation per packet and summing them.** Propagations
**overlap** across packets: on the timing diagram, each packet undergoes its
own 22 + 22 ms, but while one packet travels, the other is being emitted.
Only the timing diagram (Method) adds things up correctly — not an
improvised formula.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confusing store-and-forward with queueing.** The first is an
**obligation** (wait for the last bit, Definition in chapter 01); the
second is a **contingency** (the output link happened to be busy,
Definition 5). Step 3 of the Method combines them: departure =
max(complete, link free).

</Pitfall>

## Summary — what you can do now

<KeyResults title="The stopwatch in four formulas and one drawing">

| If the statement asks for… | You write… | Tool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| time to push L bits | t_t = L/D (after the ×8!) | Definition 2 |
| time to cross d km | t_p = d/v (≈ 5 µs/km in cable) | Definition 3 |
| a transfer through nodes | the timing diagram: emission → +t_p → max(complete, link free) → +t_p | Method |
| n packets, identical links | T = (k+n)·t_t | Proposition 1 |
| "effective throughput?" | useful bits / total time — never an average of links | Definition 6 |

**The reflex that saves you:** before any computation, three conversions set
down in writing — bytes ×8, prefixes as 10ⁿ, kilometres and km/s kept
consistent.

</KeyResults>
