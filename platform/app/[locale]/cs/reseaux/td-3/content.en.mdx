# TD 3 (tutorial sheet) — Local area networks: CSMA/CD and token ring

<ProvenanceBadge provenance="cours" />

Two worlds of medium access: disciplined randomness (CSMA/CD) and taking
turns to speak (the token). Every exercise can be solved with the formulas
of [lesson 07](../07-reseaux-locaux/) — and the delays of

<ExerciseView id="cs-net-td3-01">
<Statement>

A <Terme id="csma-cd">CSMA/CD</Terme> bus network: maximum length
**2.5 km**, rate **10 Mbit/s**, propagation speed **200 000 km/s**.

1. Compute the end-to-end propagation delay, then the duration of the
   “collision window”.
2. What minimum frame size does this network impose?
3. The same cable moves to 100 Mbit/s. New minimum size? What
   solutions did the standards adopt?

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>t_p = d/v, then think of the worst case: the collision is born at the far end, and the news of it must travel back.</Hint>
<Hint>L ≥ 2·t_p·D — Proposition 1 of lesson 07.</Hint>
<Hint>At ×10 the rate, the same time window… but ten times as many bits inside it.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

t_p = 12.5 µs; window = 25 µs; L ≥ 250 bits ≈ 32 bytes; at
100 Mbit/s: 2500 bits ≈ 313 bytes — the standards reduced the network
diameter and kept 64 bytes.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1.** $t_p = 2{,}5 / 200\,000 = 12{,}5$ µs. The collision window is
the round trip: $2\,t_p = 25$ µs — the minimum time during which a
sender must keep listening to its own transmission (lesson 07,
Proposition 1, the worst case at the far end of the cable).

**2.**

$$
L \ge 2\,t_p \cdot D = 25 \times 10^{-6} \times 10^7 = 250 \text{ bits} \approx 32 \text{ octets}.
$$

Any shorter frame could die in a collision **invisible** to its sender.
(The Ethernet standard, sized for longer repeated topologies — a
51.2 µs slot — imposes 512 bits = 64 bytes.)

**3.** At $D = 10^8$ bit/s: $L \ge 25 \times 10^{-6} \times 10^8 = 2500$
bits ≈ **313 bytes** — unacceptable (the smallest acknowledgement would
weigh a third of a kilobyte). The fast standards therefore **reduced the
permitted diameter** of the collision domain (at 100 Mbit/s), then
artificially extended short frames (carrier extension in gigabit), then
**abandoned the shared medium**: in switched full duplex, no more
collisions at all — CSMA/CD dies out along with the shared cable.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

Forgetting the factor of 2 (one way is not enough: the news of the
collision must come back); converting 2.5 km with 300 000 km/s (the
statement gives the speed in the cable); answering in bytes without going
through bits.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="cs-net-td3-02">
<Statement>

Two stations X and Y come into <Terme id="collision">collision</Terme>
while trying to transmit. Binary exponential
<Terme id="backoff">backoff</Terme> applies, with a time slot of 51.2 µs.

1. After this first collision, from which waiting times does each station
   draw? What is the probability of another collision?
2. X and Y collide again. Give the possible draws of the second
   attempt and the new collision probability.
3. At the n-th consecutive collision, what does this probability become?
   What happens at the 10th and 16th failures?

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>After the n-th collision, each station draws uniformly from `{0, …, 2ⁿ − 1}` slots.</Hint>
<Hint>The retry collides if both draw the SAME value.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

1st: a draw from `{0, 1}`, P(collision) = 1/2. 2nd: `{0,…,3}`, P = 1/4.
n-th: P = 2⁻ⁿ; the range caps at 2¹⁰ from the 10th failure on, and the
station gives up at the 16th.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1.** A range of $2^1 = 2$ values: 0 or 1 slot (0 or 51.2 µs). The
two stations collide again if they draw the same value:
$P = 2/4 = 1/2$.

**2.** A range of $2^2 = 4$: draws from $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ slots
(up to 153.6 µs). The same value for both: $P = 4/16 = 1/4$.

**3.** At the n-th collision, each draws from $2^n$ values:
$P = 2^n / 2^{2n} = 2^{-n}$ — the probability of a relapse **melts away
exponentially**; that is the meaning of the “exponential” in the backoff
(lesson 07, Definition 6). The range is capped at $2^{10} = 1024$
slots from the 10th collision on (the maximum wait grows no further),
and after **16** consecutive failures the card **gives up** on the frame
and reports an error upwards — a network that gets to that point is
saturated, and insisting would fix nothing.

</Solution>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="cs-net-td3-03">
<Statement>

A slotted CSMA bus has $N$ stations; in each slot, each station
attempts to transmit with probability $p$, independently of the
others.

1. Express the probability $A$ that a given slot is a **success**
   (exactly one station transmits).
2. For $N = 10$, compare $A$ for $p = 0{,}05$, $p = 0{,}1$ and
   $p = 0{,}3$.
3. Which $p$ maximises $A$, and what is $A$ then worth as $N$ becomes large?

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>Success = one chosen station transmits AND the N − 1 others stay silent: how many ways are there to choose the station?</Hint>
<Hint>Differentiate A(p) = N·p·(1 − p)^(N−1), or reason it out: on average, you want ONE attempt per slot.</Hint>
<Hint>(1 − 1/N)^(N−1) tends to a famous constant.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

A = N·p·(1 − p)^(N−1); for N = 10: A ≈ 0.315 / 0.387 / 0.121; maximum
at p = 1/N, with A → 1/e ≈ 37 % as N grows.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1.** A slot is a success if **exactly one** of the $N$ stations
transmits: $N$ choices of the lucky one, which transmits (probability
$p$) while the $N-1$ others stay silent:

$$
A = N\,p\,(1-p)^{N-1}.
$$

**2.** For $N = 10$: $p = 0{,}05$ → $A = 10 \times 0{,}05 \times 0{,}95^9 \approx 0{,}315$;
$p = 0{,}1$ → $10 \times 0{,}1 \times 0{,}9^9 \approx 0{,}387$;
$p = 0{,}3$ → $10 \times 0{,}3 \times 0{,}7^9 \approx 0{,}121$.
Too timid, and empty slots go to waste; too aggressive, and they are
wasted on collisions.

**3.** $A'(p) = N(1-p)^{N-2}\,[1 - Np] = 0$ gives $p^\star = 1/N$ — on
average **one** attempt per slot, exactly the intuition. Then

$$
A^\star = \left(1 - \frac{1}{N}\right)^{N-1} \xrightarrow[N \to \infty]{} \frac{1}{e} \approx 37\ \%.
$$

A slotted random-access scheme therefore never exceeds ~37 % of useful
utilisation under heavy load — the figure that motivates the token ring
of the next exercise, and the eventual abandonment of the shared medium.

</Solution>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="cs-net-td3-04">
<Statement>

A <Terme id="anneau-a-jeton">token ring</Terme> connects **20 stations**
on a **4 km** loop (propagation 5 µs/km) at **4 Mbit/s**; each
station additionally introduces a 1-bit traversal delay; the maximum
token holding time (THT) is **10 ms** per station.

1. Compute the latency of the idle loop (propagation + traversals).
2. Give the token's rotation time when idle, then its bound at full
   load.
3. A station has just transmitted. How long may it have to wait, at
   worst, before it regains the right to transmit?

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>Latency = d/v + n × (duration of one bit at the network's rate).</Hint>
<Hint>Full load: each station holds the token for its full THT — Proposition 2 of lesson 07.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

Latency ≈ 25 µs (20 µs of propagation + 20 bits at 0.25 µs); idle
rotation ≈ 25 µs; loaded bound = 20 × 10 ms + 25 µs ≈ 200 ms — which is
also a station's maximum wait.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1.** Propagation: $4 \times 5 = 20$ µs. Traversals: 20 stations × 1
bit; at 4 Mbit/s, one bit lasts $1/(4 \times 10^6) = 0{,}25$ µs → 5 µs.
**Loop latency** $\tau = 20 + 5 = 25$ µs.

**2.** When idle, the token goes round in $\tau = 25$ µs — nobody
holds it. At full load, each station keeps it for its entire THT:

$$
T_{\text{rotation}} \le n \cdot \text{THT} + \tau = 20 \times 10\ \text{ms} + 25\ \text{µs} \approx 200\ \text{ms}.
$$

(Lesson 07, Proposition 2 — the bound is **certain**, which is its whole
point.)

**3.** The worst-case wait for a station that has just released the token
is precisely one full loaded rotation: **≈ 200 ms**. That is long — but
it is **guaranteed**: no backoff lottery, no starvation possible. The
token/CSMA choice boils down to this sentence: a certain bound versus
excellence at light load (Pitfalls of lesson 07).

</Solution>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="cs-net-td3-05">
<Statement>

On the ring of the previous exercise, three stations S1, S5 and S12 each
have one frame taking **2 ms** to transmit, ready at time t = 0; the
token (of negligible duration) leaves S1 at t = 0 and takes 1.25 µs
between two neighbouring stations (the latency spread uniformly). The
other stations have nothing to transmit.

1. Establish the timeline: who transmits when, and when does the token
   return to S1?
2. What is the network's utilisation rate during this cycle?
3. What would the timeline become if S12 had TWO 2 ms frames and a
   THT of 3 ms?

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>The token visits the stations in order; each station that has a frame holds it for the time it takes to transmit it.</Hint>
<Hint>Latency between stations: 25 µs / 20 = 1.25 µs per hop.</Hint>
<Hint>THT = 3 ms: how many 2 ms frames fit in one holding?</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

S1 transmits 0→2 ms; S5 around 2.005→4.005 ms; S12 around 4.014→6.014 ms;
back to S1 at ≈ 6.024 ms. Utilisation ≈ 6/6.024 ≈ 99.6 %. With THT = 3 ms,
S12 sends only ONE frame per holding: the second waits for the next
round.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Timeline.** Latency per hop: 1.25 µs.

| Event | Time |
| --- | --- |
| S1 holds the token, transmits its frame | 0 → 2 ms |
| token S1 → S5 (4 hops, 5 µs) | arrives at 2.005 ms |
| S5 transmits | 2.005 → 4.005 ms |
| token S5 → S12 (7 hops, 8.75 µs) | arrives at ≈ 4.014 ms |
| S12 transmits | 4.014 → 6.014 ms |
| token S12 → S1 (9 hops, 11.25 µs) | returns at ≈ 6.025 ms |

**2.** Useful time: $3 \times 2 = 6$ ms out of a cycle of ≈ 6.025 ms →
**≈ 99.6 %** utilisation. Under heavy load, the ring brushes against
100 % — right where slotted CSMA capped out around 37 % (exercise 3):
this is where the two philosophies cross.

**3.** THT = 3 ms allows only **one** 2 ms frame per holding (two would
require 4). S12 sends its first frame, releases the token, and its second
frame waits **one full round** — here an almost idle round (latencies
only), i.e. ≈ 25 µs later, but in the general worst case up to
$n \cdot \text{THT} + \tau$ (exercise 4). The THT is the ring's fairness
tool: nobody monopolises, everybody waits a bounded time.

</Solution>
</ExerciseView>
