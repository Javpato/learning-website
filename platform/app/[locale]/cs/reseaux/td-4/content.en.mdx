<ExerciseView id="cs-net-td4-01">
<Statement>

A message of $x$ bits crosses $k$ <Terme id="store-and-forward">store-and-forward</Terme>
nodes (hence $k+1$ identical links of rate $d$). We cut it into packets of
$p$ data bits, each topped with a header of $h$ bits; we assume
$x \gg p$, so that the number of packets is $n = x/p$, and we neglect
propagation delays.

1. Express the total transfer time $T(p)$ of the message.
2. Show that the packet size minimising $T$ is
   $p^\star = \sqrt{xh/k}$.
3. Application: $x = 10^6$ bits, $h = 90$ bits, $k = 10$ nodes,
   $d = 10$ Mbit/s. Compute $p^\star$, the number of packets and the
   optimal time.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>Time for the first packet to reach the far end + one extra packet per t_t slot afterwards: that is the pipeline formula of lesson 02, with t_t = (p + h)/d.</Hint>
<Hint>Write T as a polynomial in p divided by p, then set the derivative to zero.</Hint>
<Hint>xh/k = 10⁶ × 90 / 10 = 9 × 10⁶: an obvious square root.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

T(p) = (k + x/p)(p + h)/d = [kp² + (kh + x)p + xh]/(dp);
p★ = √(xh/k) = 3000 bits; n ≈ 334 packets; T(p★) ≈ 106.2 ms.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1.** Each packet carries $p + h$ bits: transmission time per
link $t_t = (p+h)/d$. By the pipeline effect (lesson 02, Proposition 1,
adapted to $k$ nodes = $k+1$ links):

$$
T = (k + n)\,t_t = \left(k + \frac{x}{p}\right)\frac{p + h}{d}
  = \frac{kp^2 + (kh + x)p + xh}{dp}.
$$

**2.** Let us differentiate with respect to $p$:

$$
\frac{dT}{dp} = \frac{[2kp + (kh+x)]\,dp - [kp^2 + (kh+x)p + xh]\,d}{d^2p^2}
             = \frac{kp^2 - xh}{dp^2}.
$$

It vanishes for $kp^2 = xh$, that is

$$
p^\star = \sqrt{\frac{xh}{k}}
$$

(a minimum: the derivative is negative before, positive after). How to
read it: **small** packets multiply the headers ($n \cdot h$ grows), and
**large** packets kill the pipeline ($k$ full transmissions to pay in
series) — the optimum balances the two wastes exactly.

**3.** $p^\star = \sqrt{10^6 \times 90 / 10} = \sqrt{9 \times 10^6} = 3000$
bits, i.e. $n = 10^6/3000 \approx 334$ packets, and

$$
T(p^\star) = \left(10 + 334\right)\frac{3090}{10^7} \approx 106{,}3 \text{ ms}
$$

— against $(10+1) \times (10^6 + 90)/10^7 \approx 1{,}1$ s as a single
block: the optimal cut divides the time by ten.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

Forgetting the header in t_t (it is what creates the optimum: without h,
smaller would always be better); differentiating without putting T in
rational form; concluding “p★” without checking that it is a minimum.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="cs-net-td4-02">
<Statement>

Six routers are connected as follows (symmetric costs): A–B: 10, A–E: 5,
B–E: 2, B–C: 2, B–D: 3, C–E: 4, C–F: 1, C–D: 3, D–F: 1.

1. Recall in three lines the label principle of
   <Terme id="algorithme-de-dijkstra">Dijkstra's algorithm</Terme>
   (provisional / permanent).
2. Unroll the algorithm from **A**, giving the state of the labels at
   each round.
3. Give the shortest paths obtained and A's
   <Terme id="table-de-routage">routing table</Terme>
   (destination, next hop, cost).

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>At each round: the smallest provisional label in the graph becomes permanent, then its neighbours are re-examined.</Hint>
<Hint>First round from A: only B and E receive a label.</Hint>
<Hint>For the table: walk each destination's “via” entries back until you touch A.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

Order of the permanent labels: A, E(5), B(7,E), C(9,E), D(10,B), F(10,C).
Paths AE, AEB, AEC, AEBD, AECF — A's table: everything goes through E.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1.** Each node carries (cost, via), at first **provisional**; the
smallest provisional label in the whole graph becomes **permanent** (never
modified again) and its node, now active, offers each neighbour
“my cost + the link” — which replaces any larger label
(lesson 10, Method §4).

**2. The unrolling.**

| Round | Permanent | B | C | D | E | F |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | **A** | (10, A) | ∞ | ∞ | (5, A) | ∞ |
| 1 | **E** (5) | (7, E) | (9, E) | ∞ | — | ∞ |
| 2 | **B** (7) | — | (9, E)* | (10, B) | — | ∞ |
| 3 | **C** (9) | — | — | (10, B)* | — | (10, C) |
| 4 | **D** (10) | — | — | — | — | (10, C)* |
| 5 | **F** (10) | — | — | — | — | — |

(*) ties: at round 2, the path AEBC is also worth $7+2 = 9$ — we keep
the existing label (9, E); at round 3, AECD is worth $9+3 = 12 > 10$; at
round 4, ADF is worth $10+1 = 11 > 10$. When two provisional labels are
tied (D and F at 10), the promotion order is free — the final result does
not change.

**3. Paths and table.** Walking the via entries back: E ← A; B ← E ← A;
C ← E ← A; D ← B ← E ← A; F ← C ← E ← A. Hence the paths **AE, AEB,
AEC, AEBD, AECF**, and A's table:

| Destination | Next hop | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| B | E | 7 |
| C | E | 9 |
| D | E | 10 |
| E | E | 5 |
| F | E | 10 |

Everything leaves towards E: the “next hop” column keeps only the
**first step** of the calculation — that is the whole philosophy of
forwarding (lesson 10, Definition 1).

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

Promoting the last relabelled node instead of the smallest provisional
label in the graph; forgetting to compare before relabelling (a label
never gets worse); giving the full path in the table instead of the next
hop.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="cs-net-td4-03">
<Statement>

Four routers exchange vectors under <Terme id="vecteur-de-distances">distance-vector
routing</Terme> on the following network (symmetric costs): A–B: 2,
B–C: 2, A–C: 3, B–D: 3, C–D: 3.

1. Give each router's initial vector (its direct neighbours).
2. Each router sends its initial vector to its neighbours, which
   update. Give the tables after this first round of exchanges.
3. Is a second round enough for convergence? Give each router's final
   table (distance and next hop).

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>Initial vector = direct links only; A does not know D at the start.</Hint>
<Hint>Rule: candidate = announced distance + link cost; adopt it if strictly better.</Hint>
<Hint>The only interesting non-direct journey is A↔D — two paths of cost 5 exist.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

After one round: A learns D at 5 (via B), D learns A at 5 (via B); every
other pair was already optimal directly. Tables stable from the second
round on: A–B 2, A–C 3, A–D 5 via B, B–C 2, B–D 3, C–D 3.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Initial vectors.** A: (B: 2, C: 3); B: (A: 2, C: 2,
D: 3); C: (A: 3, B: 2, D: 3); D: (B: 3, C: 3).

**2. First round.** Each router processes its neighbours' vectors
(lesson 10, Method §2):

- **A** receives from B: D at $3+2 = 5$ → **new entry (D, 5, via B)**;
  C at $2+2 = 4 > 3$ direct → unchanged. From C: D at $3+3 = 6 > 5$; B at
  $2+3 = 5 > 2$ → unchanged.
- **B**: everything is already direct and optimal (A 2, C 2, D 3); via C, A
  would be worth $3+2 = 5 > 2$.
- **C**: likewise (A 3, B 2, D 3); via B, A would be worth $2+2 = 4 > 3$.
- **D** receives from B: A at $2+3 = 5$ → **new entry (A, 5, via B)**;
  from C: A at $3+3 = 6 > 5$ → unchanged.

**3. Convergence.** At the second round, the vectors include the new
entries (A announces D: 5, D announces A: 5), but no candidate beats
the values in place: **no more changes — the protocol has
converged.** Final tables:

| | to A | to B | to C | to D |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | — | 2 (B) | 3 (C) | 5 (B) |
| **B** | 2 (A) | — | 2 (C) | 3 (D) |
| **C** | 3 (A) | 2 (B) | — | 3 (D) |
| **D** | 5 (B) | 3 (B) | 3 (C) | — |

Exam remark: A↔D admits two paths of cost 5 (through B or through C) —
the first one learned is kept, and the paper accepts either as long as
the calculation is shown.

</Solution>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="cs-net-td4-04">
<Statement>

Three routers: A–B: 3, B–C: 2, A–C: 8, converged tables (C reaches A
in 5 via B). The **A–B link breaks** just after B has sent its periodic
vector to C.

1. Establish the timeline of B's and C's tables over the following
   exchanges (T1: B's vector leaves for C; T2: B detects the break; T3: C
   processes the received vector; T4: C sends its vector to B). What do we observe?
2. How does this “bounce” evolve over the following exchanges, and where
   does it stop?
3. Replay the timeline with <Terme id="horizon-partage">split
   horizon</Terme> (with poisoned reverse). What exactly does it change?

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>The vector that left at T1 still contains “A: 3” — it is already stale when C reads it.</Hint>
<Hint>At T4, B no longer has a route to A… and C announces one to it at 5.</Hint>
<Hint>With poisoned reverse: what does the “A” entry contain in the vector C sends TO B?</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

T3: C believes it reaches A at 5 via B (stale information); T4: B adopts
A at 7 via C — packets for A bounce between B and C. The cost swells
exchange after exchange until the direct A–C route at 8 becomes the best
again. With poisoned reverse, C announces “A: ∞” to B: no bounce,
direct convergence onto the link at 8.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. The timeline of the bounce** (lesson 10, Definition 7 — it is the
scenario of the official solution):

| Time | Event | B's table (to A) | C's table (to A) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T1 | B sends its vector (A: 3, C: 2) to C | (direct, 3) | (via B, 5) |
| T2 | B detects the A–B break | **(∞)** | (via B, 5) |
| T3 | C processes the T1 vector: A = min(8, 2+3) = 5 | (∞) | (via B, 5) — confirmed on **dead** information |
| T4 | C sends (A: 5, …) to B; B: A = 5+2 = 7 | **(via C, 7)** | (via B, 5) |

What we observe: the tables **lie in a loop** — B sends the packets for
A towards C, C sends them back towards B (the solution's “bounce
effect”); neither of the two knows it, because a vector does not say
**which way** the route goes.

**2. The escalation.** Next exchange: B announces (A: 7) to C → C
recomputes A = min(8, 7+2 = 9) = **8 via the direct link** — the bounce
stops here because a real route at 8 existed. Without it (no A–C link),
the costs would have swollen 2 by 2 **up to the ceiling** (16 in RIP):
minutes of looping — count to infinity properly speaking.

**3. With split horizon (poisoned reverse).** Already at T1, C's vector
towards B would have contained “**A: ∞**” (a route learned from B →
poisoned on that link, lesson 10 Definition 8); symmetrically, C would
never have received from B a route it had supplied to it. At T4, B reads
“A: ∞”: no mirage, it stays without a route until C discovers the direct
link (A = 8) and announces it — clean convergence in two exchanges, zero
bounce. A limit worth knowing (Remark in lesson 10): the poisoned reverse
kills loops between **two** routers; loops among three can survive it.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

Making C react as early as T2 (it knows nothing: information travels in
vectors, not by magic); forgetting to add the link cost when adopting
(7 = 5 + 2); writing “split horizon = send nothing” (the poisoned
reverse sends ∞ — an explicit denial, not silence).

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>
