# Sharing a cable — CSMA/CD, Ethernet and the token ring

<LessonMeta id="cs-net-c07" />

<Toc depth={2} />

<Accroche>
On a shared cable, the real protocol is not "how to speak" but "how to stay
silent at the right moment" — and physics even imposes a minimum size on what
you say.
</Accroche>

<Mission fil="Exercise 1 of TD 3 (tutorial sheet) — where Ethernet's 64 bytes come from">
**Situation.** A local network on a bus: a **2.5 km** cable shared by all
the stations, rate **10 Mbit/s**, signal at **200,000 km/s**. Each station
transmits whenever it likes — and two simultaneous transmissions garble
each other. **Question.** What minimum size must frames be given so that a
transmitting station is **certain** to notice, before it finishes, that its
frame has been garbled — and why did the Ethernet standard carve
"64 bytes minimum" in stone? **The obstacle.** The garbling may be born at
the far end of the cable: by the time the news travels back, the sender may
already have finished and hung up — convinced that everything went fine.
**The contract.** This chapter gives you 4 tools: the multiple-access
problem, the CSMA/CD method with its random backoff, the Ethernet frame
field by field, and the token ring. At the end, we compute the TD's minimum
size, to the byte.
</Mission>

*Already comfortable with this? [Go straight to TD 3](../td-3/).*

## The battle plan

To tame the shared cable, we need 4 tools:

1. **the multiple-access problem** — why a broadcast medium demands arbitration;
2. **CSMA/CD** — listen before, detect during, back off at random afterwards;
3. **the Ethernet frame** — the real format, from preamble to FCS, and its 64-byte minimum;
4. **the token ring** — deterministic arbitration, and its computable rotation time.

## 1. Multiple access — one channel, many candidates

Problem: in a broadcast <Terme id="reseau-informatique">computer
network</Terme>, everyone hears everything — that is its strength (one
transmission reaches every destination) and its curse (two simultaneous
transmissions destroy each other). Who has the right to speak, and when?

**Definition 1 (local area network).** A <Def id="reseau-local">**local area
network (LAN)**</Def> is a network at the scale of a building or a campus,
historically built on a medium **shared** among the stations (coaxial bus,
ring, radio waves today).

**Definition 2 (multiple access).** The problem of <Def id="acces-multiple">**multiple
access**</Def> is that of arbitrating a shared medium: deciding, with no
central conductor, which station transmits at each instant. Two
philosophies face off: controlled randomness (CSMA/CD) and taking turns
(the token ring).

Problem: before arbitrating, we must know **whom** each frame is talking
to — on a medium where everyone receives everything.

**Definition 3 (MAC address).** The <Def id="adresse-mac">**MAC address**</Def>
is the **6-byte** identifier burned into every network card, unique in the
world (3 manufacturer bytes + 3 serial bytes). Every station reads all the
frames but **keeps** only those carrying its address — or the broadcast
address `FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF`, which everyone keeps.

**Remark.** The MAC address identifies without locating — it is a serial
number, not a set of directions. The comparison with the <Terme id="adresse-ip">IP
address</Terme>, which is structured into network + host, is a TD 5
question.

### Mini-exercise

1. Why can the worldwide Internet not be routed with MAC addresses alone?

   is. A routing table would have to list every machine in the world one
   by one — that is the opening argument of lesson 08.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. CSMA/CD — listen, detect, back off

Problem: the simplest scheme would be to transmit whenever you like; the
collisions would be permanent. Listening first helps — but is not enough,
because of propagation: a station may be listening to a cable that is
**still silent** while a distant transmission is already on its way.

**Definition 4 (collision).** A <Def id="collision">**collision**</Def>
is the superposition of two transmissions on the medium: the signals add
up, both frames are unreadable and will have to be resent.

**Definition 5 (CSMA/CD).** <Def id="csma-cd">**CSMA/CD**</Def> (Carrier
Sense Multiple Access / Collision Detection) combines three gestures:
**listen before** transmitting (never cut into an ongoing transmission),
**listen while** you transmit (compare what you hear with what you send
— any difference betrays a collision), and, on collision,
**reinforce** the garbling (a jam sequence, so that everyone sees it)
then retry later.

**Proposition 1 (minimum frame size).** On a medium with maximum
propagation delay $t_p$ (end to end) and rate $D$, collision detection is
guaranteed only if every frame lasts at least
$2\,t_p$, that is, if its length satisfies

$$
L \;\ge\; 2\,t_p \cdot D \;=\; \frac{2\,d\,D}{v}.
$$

**Why?** The worst case: A transmits; its signal takes $t_p$ to reach
the far end, where B — who heard silence — has just started to
transmit. The collision is born over there, and its garbling takes another
$t_p$ to travel back to A. If A finished transmitting before $2\,t_p$, it
is no longer listening "during": its frame is dead without A knowing, and
nobody will retransmit it. Transmitting for at least $2\,t_p$ means staying
on listen long enough for every worst case. $\blacksquare$

Problem: after a collision, if the two stations retry immediately, they
collide again identically. They must be desynchronised — by
randomness.

**Definition 6 (binary exponential backoff).** The <Def id="backoff">**backoff**</Def>
makes each station wait a **random** number of time slots
(one slot = $2\,t_p$, the time of one detection): after the $n$-th
collision of the same frame, the draw is made among
$\{0, 1, \ldots, 2^n - 1\}$ slots (capped at $2^{10}$; give up after
16 attempts). Each failure **doubles the range**, not the wait:
randomness gets more and more room to separate the rivals.

**Example 1 (the TD scenario).** Two stations collide.
1st attempt: each draws from $\{0, 1\}$ — one chance in two of
colliding again. 2nd: a draw from $\{0,\ldots,3\}$ — one chance in four.
The probability of a prolonged deadlock melts away exponentially — but is
never zero: CSMA/CD **guarantees** no bound on the access delay.

## 3. The Ethernet frame — the format that won

Problem: we now have to pin down the exact format — because each field of
the frame answers a constraint we have just met.

**Definition 7 (Ethernet frame).** The <Def id="trame-ethernet">**Ethernet
frame**</Def> is made of:

| Field | Size (bytes) | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preamble | 7 | `10101010`×7: synchronise the receivers' clocks |
| SFD | 1 | `10101011`: "the frame starts at the next bit" |
| DA | 6 | destination MAC address (1st bit: individual/group) |
| SA | 6 | source MAC address |
| Length | 2 | size of the data field |
| Data | 0 to 1500 | the payload (the IP datagram, typically) |
| Padding | 0 to 46 | tops things up if the data are too short |
| FCS | 4 | the 32-bit <Terme id="crc">CRC</Terme>, computed from DA to Padding |

The **64-byte minimum** is counted from DA to FCS inclusive
($6+6+2+46+4 = 64$): the preamble and SFD, pure attention-grabbing signals,
are not part of it. Hence the **Padding** field: a 10-byte payload travels
with 36 bytes of filler.

**Remark.** 1500 bytes of maximum payload: that is Ethernet's
<Terme id="mtu">MTU</Terme>, which lesson 09 will meet again while
fragmenting datagrams. And the network's rate also bounds the **maximum**
size — so that no station monopolises the bus.

### Mini-exercise

1. An application sends 1 useful byte over Ethernet. What actually goes out on the cable?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   64 bytes from DA to FCS (including 45 of padding around the byte +
   upper-layer headers), preceded by the 8 bytes of preamble + SFD — that
   is 72 bytes "on the air" for 1 useful one. The minimum of Proposition 1
   is paid in full on small messages.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. The token ring — arbitration without randomness

Problem: CSMA/CD is simple and excellent under light load, but its access
delay is **unbounded** — unacceptable for industrial real-time use. The
opposite solution: organise turn-taking.

**Definition 8 (token ring).** In a <Def id="anneau-a-jeton">**token
ring**</Def>, the stations form a loop in which each bit travels from
neighbour to neighbour; a special frame — the **token** — circulates, and
only the station holding it transmits (for at most THT, the token-holding time).
It then releases the token to its neighbour. No collisions, ever.

**Proposition 2 (rotation time).** With $n$ stations each carrying
at most THT of traffic, and a loop latency $\tau$ (propagation around
the ring + station-crossing delays), the token comes back within at most

$$
T_{\text{rotation}} \;\le\; n \cdot \text{THT} + \tau .
$$

**Why?** The worst lap: every station holds the token for its maximum
(n·THT), and the token itself travels the ring once ($\tau$). It is
a **certain bound** — the exact opposite of the random backoff.
$\blacksquare$

**Remark (blind spot).** Bounded does not mean short: when idle, the
token goes around in $\tau$; under load, every station waits its full turn.
And the ring pays for its robustness in management: a lost token, a failed
station — TD 3 turns these into timed scenarios.

## Worked example — the 64 bytes, starting from the cable

Let us unroll the mission: a 2.5 km bus, 10 Mbit/s, $v = 200\,000$ km/s.

**Step 1 — the end-to-end propagation delay.**

$$
t_p = \frac{d}{v} = \frac{2{,}5}{200\,000} = 1{,}25 \times 10^{-5} \text{ s} = 12{,}5 \text{ µs}.
$$

*Why?* This is the <Terme id="temps-de-propagation">propagation
delay</Terme> brick from lesson 02 — the worst case of Proposition 1 plays
out between the two ends of the cable.

**Step 2 — the detection window.** The round trip is worth
$2\,t_p = 25$ µs: that is the length of time during which the sender must
still be transmitting.

*Why?* Proof of Proposition 1: collision at the far end ($t_p$),
garbling on the way back ($t_p$) — before $2\,t_p$, no silence is
proof.

**Step 3 — the minimum size on THIS network.**

$$
L \ge 2\,t_p \cdot D = 25 \times 10^{-6} \times 10^7 = 250 \text{ bits} \approx 32 \text{ octets}.
$$

*Why?* Proposition 1 turns a duration into bits via the rate: at
10 Mbit/s, "transmit for 25 µs" means "transmit 250 bits".

**Step 4 — why the standard says 64.** Ethernet must work on
topologies worse than our bare bus: up to ~5 km with repeaters (which
add their own delays), giving a standardised slot of **51.2 µs** — and
$51{,}2\ \text{µs} \times 10^7 = 512$ bits $= 64$ bytes.

*Why?* The standard takes the worst case of **all** compliant
installations, not of ours: the minimum is a worldwide contract, hence
Definition 7's Padding field for short messages.

**Step 5 — the contrapositive that kills.** If a station transmitted 100 bits
(10 µs) and then fell silent, a collision born 11 µs of cumulative
propagation away would never reach it during its transmission: frame lost,
**and nobody knows** — no CRC (the station is no longer listening), no
timer (the Ethernet link layer has none): the loss would only surface in
the upper layers, milliseconds later.

*Why?* That is the real stake of the minimum: without it, CSMA/CD loses its
**detection**, hence its fast retransmission — the whole point of the CD.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**If the same cable is moved from 10 Mbit/s to 1 Gbit/s, what happens to
the minimum size?**

It is multiplied by 100: $L \ge 2\,t_p \cdot D$ grows with the rate
(Proposition 1) — 25 µs at 1 Gbit/s make 25,000 bits ≈ 3 KB per minimum
frame! That is why gigabit Ethernet had to change the rules (carrier
extension, then abandoning the shared medium in favour of switches): the
formula governs the history of the hardware.

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Counting the preamble in the 64 bytes.** The minimum is measured from
**DA to FCS** (Definition 7): $6+6+2+46+4 = 64$. The preamble and SFD are
the signal's hook, not the frame.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confusing collision detection with the CRC.** A collision is detected
**during transmission**, by comparing what is sent with what is heard
(Definition 5) — not at reception by the FCS. The CRC catches noise; the
CD catches rivals — two mechanisms, two moments.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**"Backoff doubles the wait."** No: it doubles the **range** of the
draw (Definition 6) — the drawn wait can even be zero. It is the
probability of another clash that is halved, not the time that is
doubled.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believing the ring is "faster".** The ring offers a **bounded** delay
(Proposition 2), not a short one: when idle, CSMA/CD starts on the spot
while the token keeps you waiting. Light load → randomness; real-time
constraint → token. That is the summary sentence expected in the TD.

</Pitfall>

<MissionSolved>
The mission now has its numbers: on the TD's bus, $t_p = 12{,}5$ µs (Step 1),
detection window $2\,t_p = 25$ µs (Proposition 1), hence a frame of at
least $25\ \text{µs} \times 10\ \text{Mbit/s} = $ **250 bits ≈ 32 bytes**
on this cable (Step 3). The Ethernet standard, for its part, covers the
worst case of every installation (a 51.2 µs slot): $512$ bits $=$
**64 bytes from DA to FCS** — and it is the Padding field (Definition 7)
that inflates short messages up to the contract. Below it, a distant
collision would become invisible (Step 5): the minimum is not a
standards-body whim, it is the condition of existence of the "CD" in
CSMA/CD.
</MissionSolved>
