# Diagonalizability

<Toc />

<p className="accroche">To diagonalize is to find directions along which the map merely multiplies.</p>

## Eigen-directions

A nonzero vector $v$ is an eigenvector of $u$ when $u(v)=\lambda v$. The line $\mathbb Rv$ stays invariant: only its scale, and possibly its orientation, changes.

## Criterion

An endomorphism of an $n$-dimensional space $E$ is diagonalizable if and only if $E$ has a basis of eigenvectors. Equivalently,

$$
E=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\operatorname{Sp}(u)}\ker(u-\lambda I).
$$

In particular, a characteristic polynomial that splits into $n$ distinct roots guarantees diagonalizability. This condition is sufficient, not necessary.

## Example — an involution

If $u^2=I$, then $X^2-1=(X-1)(X+1)$ annihilates $u$. Its roots are simple, so $u$ is diagonalizable and

$$
E=\ker(u-I)\oplus\ker(u+I).
$$

Transposition $M\mapsto M^\mathsf T$ is an involution: symmetric matrices form the eigenspace for $1$, while skew-symmetric matrices form the eigenspace for $-1$.

## Why the criterion works

If an annihilating polynomial splits into distinct linear factors, the kernel lemma gives a direct sum of the kernels $\ker(u-\lambda I)$. Each is an eigenspace, and together they fill $E$.

## Common pitfalls

- A repeated eigenvalue does not prevent diagonalizability.
- Compare geometric and algebraic multiplicities.
- A triangular matrix is not automatically diagonalizable.

## Quick test

The matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ has only one eigen-direction. Its eigenspace has dimension $1<2$, so it is not diagonalizable.
