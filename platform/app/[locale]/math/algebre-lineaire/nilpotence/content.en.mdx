# Nilpotency on $\mathbb R_n[X]$

<Toc />

<p className="accroche">Nilpotent means that, after enough iterations, every vector ends at zero.</p>

## Intuition

A nilpotent map destroys information in stages. Differentiation lowers the degree at each step:

$$
\mathbb R_n[X]\longrightarrow\mathbb R_{n-1}[X]\longrightarrow\cdots
\longrightarrow\mathbb R_0[X]\longrightarrow\{0\}.
$$

## Formal definition

An endomorphism $u\colon E\to E$ is **nilpotent** if $u^p=0$ for some $p\geq1$. The least such integer is its **index of nilpotency**.

In dimension $d$, every nilpotent endomorphism satisfies $u^d=0$. Its only possible eigenvalue is $0$.

## Worked example

On $\mathbb R_n[X]$, let $D(P)=P'$. For $0\leq k\leq n$,

$$
D^k(X^n)=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}X^{n-k}.
$$

Hence $D^{n+1}=0$, while $D^n(X^n)=n!\neq0$: the index is exactly $n+1$.

## Proof — spectrum and diagonalizability

If $u(x)=\lambda x$ with $x\neq0$, then $0=u^p(x)=\lambda^p x$, so $\lambda=0$. If a nilpotent map were diagonalizable, its diagonal matrix would contain only zeros, making it the zero map.

Therefore **a nonzero nilpotent map is never diagonalizable**.

## Common pitfalls

- Zero trace is not enough for nilpotency.
- A sum of nilpotent maps need not be nilpotent without an extra condition such as commutation.
- To prove the exact index, show $u^{p-1}\neq0$ as well as $u^p=0$.

## Mini exercises

<details>
<summary>What is the index of $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$?</summary>

Its square is zero but the matrix is not: the index is $2$.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why is $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$ not nilpotent?</summary>

Its eigenvalues are $1$ and $-1$, and every power remains invertible.
</details>
