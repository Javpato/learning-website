# Differentiability

<Accroche>« To be differentiable is, very closely, to *be* a linear map. »</Accroche>

## Intuition

When you zoom in on a smooth curve, it eventually looks like its **tangent line**. When
you zoom in on a smooth surface, it eventually looks like a **plane**. Differentiability
is exactly this idea, lifted to the level of vector spaces: near a point $a$, a function
$f$ behaves like an **affine** map

$$h \longmapsto f(a) + L(h),$$

where $L$ is a *linear* map. To differentiate is to find the best local linear
approximation — and to certify that it is *good*, in the sense that the error decreases
faster than $\lVert h \rVert$.

## Formal definition

Let $E$ and $F$ be two normed vector spaces, $U \subset E$ an open set and
$a \in U$. We say that $f \colon U \to F$ is **differentiable at $a$** if there exists a
continuous linear map $df_a \in \mathcal{L}(E, F)$ such that

$$f(a + h) = f(a) + df_a(h) + o\!\left(\lVert h \rVert\right) \quad (h \to 0),$$

that is,

$$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\bigl\lVert f(a + h) - f(a) - df_a(h) \bigr\rVert_F}{\lVert h \rVert_E} = 0.$$

The map $df_a$ is called the **differential** of $f$ at $a$. We will see that it is
**unique**: we may therefore speak of *the* differential, without ambiguity.

## Worked example

Take $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ and $a = (1, 1)$. For $h = (h_1, h_2)$:

$$f(a + h) = (1 + h_1)^2 + (1 + h_2)^2 = \underbrace{2}_{f(a)} + \underbrace{(2h_1 + 2h_2)}_{\text{linear}} + \underbrace{(h_1^2 + h_2^2)}_{= \,\lVert h \rVert^2}.$$

The differential and the remainder can be read off directly:

$$df_a(h_1, h_2) = 2h_1 + 2h_2, \qquad r(h) = \lVert h \rVert^2.$$

And the key estimate is immediate:

$$\frac{\lVert r(h) \rVert}{\lVert h \rVert} = \frac{\lVert h \rVert^2}{\lVert h \rVert} = \lVert h \rVert \xrightarrow[h \to 0]{} 0.$$

The remainder tends to $0$ *faster* than $\lVert h \rVert$: $f$ is indeed differentiable at
$a$, with differential $h \mapsto 2h_1 + 2h_2$.

## Proof

The definition gives more than intuition: it has rigorous consequences. Here are three,
with their proofs.

<Collapsible title="1. Uniqueness of the differential">

Suppose that $L_1$ and $L_2$ both work. Then, for every $h$,

$$L_1(h) - L_2(h) = \bigl[f(a + h) - f(a) - L_2(h)\bigr] - \bigl[f(a + h) - f(a) - L_1(h)\bigr] = o(\lVert h \rVert).$$

Set $M = L_1 - L_2$, linear. Fix $u \neq 0$ and take $h = t u$ with $t \to 0^+$.
By linearity, $M(tu) = t\, M(u)$, so

$$\frac{\lVert M(tu) \rVert}{\lVert tu \rVert} = \frac{\lVert M(u) \rVert}{\lVert u \rVert} = o(1) \to 0.$$

The left-hand side is constant in $t$; it is therefore $0$, hence $M(u) = 0$. This for
every $u$, so $M = 0$ and $L_1 = L_2$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

<Collapsible title="2. Differentiable ⟹ continuous at a">

For $h \to 0$,

$$\lVert f(a + h) - f(a) \rVert \le \lVert df_a(h) \rVert + \lVert r(h) \rVert \le \lVert df_a \rVert_{\mathrm{op}}\, \lVert h \rVert + o(\lVert h \rVert) \xrightarrow[h \to 0]{} 0,$$

where $\lVert df_a \rVert_{\mathrm{op}}$ is the operator norm of the differential (finite because $df_a$ is
continuous). So $f(a + h) \to f(a)$: $f$ is continuous at $a$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="3. Link with partial derivatives (case E = ℝⁿ)">

If $f \colon \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is differentiable at $a$, then taking
$h = t\, e_i$ (the $i$-th basis vector) we obtain

$$\frac{f(a + t e_i) - f(a)}{t} = \frac{df_a(t e_i) + o(t)}{t} = df_a(e_i) + o(1) \xrightarrow[t \to 0]{} df_a(e_i).$$

So the partial derivative $\partial f / \partial x_i(a)$ exists and equals $df_a(e_i)$. By
linearity, for $h = (h_1, \dots, h_n)$,

$$df_a(h) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(a)\, h_i = \nabla f(a) \cdot h.$$

The differential can thus be read off the **gradient** (or the Jacobian matrix for
$f \colon \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$). $\qquad\blacksquare$

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**The existence of partial derivatives does not entail differentiability.** Consider

$$g(x, y) = \frac{x^2 y}{x^4 + y^2} \ \text{ if } (x,y) \neq (0,0), \qquad g(0,0) = 0.$$

Both partial derivatives at $0$ exist and equal $0$ (since $g(x, 0) = g(0, y) = 0$).
Better still: *all* directional derivatives exist at $0$. Yet $g$ is not even
**continuous** at $0$: along the parabola $y = x^2$,

$$g(x, x^2) = \frac{x^2 \cdot x^2}{x^4 + x^4} = \frac{1}{2} \not\to 0 = g(0,0).$$

By result 2, a differentiable function is continuous; $g$ is not, so
$g$ is **not differentiable** at $0$ — despite the existence of all its directional
derivatives.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**The differential is not a number, it is a linear map.** Writing
"$df_a = 2h_1 + 2h_2$" is a shortcut; rigorously, $df_a$ is the map
$h \mapsto 2h_1 + 2h_2$, an element of $\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^2, \mathbb{R})$.

</Pitfall>

## Mini-exercises

<Collapsible title="Compute the differential of f(x, y) = xy at (a, b).">

$f(a + h_1,\, b + h_2) = (a + h_1)(b + h_2) = ab + (b\,h_1 + a\,h_2) + h_1 h_2$. The
linear part is $df_{(a,b)}(h_1, h_2) = b\,h_1 + a\,h_2$, and the remainder $h_1 h_2$ satisfies
$\lvert h_1 h_2 \rvert \le \lVert h \rVert^2 = o(\lVert h \rVert)$. So
$\nabla f(a,b) = (b, a)$.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Why does « C¹ ⟹ differentiable »?">

If the partial derivatives of $f$ exist **and are continuous** on a neighbourhood of $a$
(the $C^1$ condition), then $f$ is differentiable at $a$, and $df_a(h) = \nabla f(a) \cdot h$.
The proof applies the mean value theorem coordinate by coordinate, the continuity of the
partials controlling the remainder uniformly. This is the practical criterion:
compute the partials, check that they are continuous, and differentiability
is secured — without going back through the limit in the definition.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Build a continuous function, with zero partial derivatives at 0, but not differentiable.">

Take the pitfall's idea again but ensuring continuity:
$\varphi(x, y) = \dfrac{x^3}{x^2 + y^2}$ (and $\varphi(0,0) = 0$). It is continuous at $0$
(since $\lvert \varphi \rvert \le \lvert x \rvert$), its directional derivatives exist,
but $h \mapsto$ (directional derivative) is **not linear**: no linear
map can be the differential, so $\varphi$ is not differentiable at $0$.

</Collapsible>
