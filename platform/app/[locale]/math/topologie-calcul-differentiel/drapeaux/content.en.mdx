# Flags

<Accroche>« A flag is a tower of nested subspaces, each strictly larger than the previous one. »</Accroche>

## Intuition

Building a basis means stacking directions one at a time: first a line, then thickening it into a
plane, then into the whole space. At each step there is a subspace, and they are **nested**: a line in
a plane in space. This tower is a **flag**. When each step adds exactly one dimension, the flag is
**complete** — and it encodes precisely what it means to "put a matrix in triangular form".

## Formal definition

Let $E$ be a vector space of dimension $n$. A **flag** of $E$ is a **strictly increasing** sequence of
subspaces, none reduced to $\{0\}$,

$$D_1 \subsetneq D_2 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq D_p \subseteq E.$$

The flag is **complete** (or full) when $\dim D_i = i$ for all $i$ and $D_n = E$; one then has the
maximal tower

$$\{0\} \subsetneq D_1 \subsetneq D_2 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq D_n = E, \qquad \dim D_i = i.$$

A basis $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ is **adapted** to the flag if $D_i = \operatorname{span}(e_1, \dots, e_i)$
for all $i$.

## Worked example

In $\mathbb{R}^3$, $\ \{0\} \subsetneq \operatorname{span}(e_1) \subsetneq \operatorname{span}(e_1, e_2) \subsetneq \mathbb{R}^3\ $
is a complete flag, with adapted basis $(e_1, e_2, e_3)$. An endomorphism $u$ **stabilises** this flag
($u(D_i) \subseteq D_i$ for all $i$) if and only if its matrix in $(e_1, e_2, e_3)$ is

$$\begin{pmatrix} * & * & * \\ 0 & * & * \\ 0 & 0 & * \end{pmatrix} \quad\text{(upper triangular).}$$

## Proof

<Collapsible title="Stabilising a complete flag ⟺ being upper triangular" open={true}>

Let $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$ be an adapted basis, $D_i = \operatorname{span}(e_1, \dots, e_i)$.

*($\Rightarrow$)* If $u(D_i) \subseteq D_i$ for all $i$, then in particular
$u(e_i) \in D_i = \operatorname{span}(e_1, \dots, e_i)$: the $i$-th column of the matrix of $u$ has
entries only in rows $1, \dots, i$, hence **zeros below the diagonal**. The matrix is upper triangular.

*($\Leftarrow$)* Conversely, if the matrix is upper triangular, then
$u(e_j) \in \operatorname{span}(e_1, \dots, e_j) = D_j \subseteq D_i$ for $j \le i$; since $D_i$ is
spanned by $e_1, \dots, e_i$, we have $u(D_i) \subseteq D_i$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Consequence: triangularisable ⟺ stabilises a complete flag">

To say that $u$ is **triangularisable** (similar to a triangular matrix) is exactly to say that there
is a basis in which its matrix is upper triangular — hence, by the above, that there exists a **complete
flag stabilised** by $u$. Over $\mathbb{C}$ (or as soon as the characteristic polynomial splits),
**every** endomorphism is triangularisable: it always stabilises a complete flag. This is the basis of
reduction theory (Jordan, Dunford). $\qquad\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**The inclusions must be strict.** $\{0\} \subseteq D \subseteq D \subseteq E$ is not a flag: repeating
a subspace does not "climb" the tower. And "complete" requires $\dim D_i = i$ **exactly** — an
increasing sequence that skips a dimension is not a complete flag.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**A flag is not unique.** $\mathbb{R}^2$ has infinitely many complete flags: one per chosen line $D_1$.
Choosing a flag means choosing a "preferred direction" — hence the infinitely many triangularising bases
for a single endomorphism.

</Pitfall>

## Mini-exercises

<Collapsible title="1. Build a complete flag from a basis.">

Given a basis $(e_1, \dots, e_n)$, set $D_i = \operatorname{span}(e_1, \dots, e_i)$. The inclusions are
strict (each $e_{i+1}\notin D_i$ since the family is free) and $\dim D_i = i$: it is a complete flag,
and the basis is an adapted basis.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="2. Two distinct complete flags of ℝ².">

$\{0\} \subset \operatorname{span}(e_1) \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ and
$\{0\} \subset \operatorname{span}(e_1 + e_2) \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ differ by their line $D_1$. Every
line of the plane gives a distinct complete flag.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="3. Why 'upper triangular' and not 'lower'?">

It is a convention tied to the order of the adapted basis: with
$D_i = \operatorname{span}(e_1,\dots,e_i)$, stability gives zeros **below** the diagonal. Reversing the
basis order ($e_n, \dots, e_1$) swaps "upper" and "lower": the two notions are equivalent, up to the
choice of basis.

</Collapsible>
