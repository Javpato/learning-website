# Controlling a sum of functions everywhere at once

<LessonMeta id="math-an-c06" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

Adding infinitely many curves becomes safe when each curve is assigned a numerical bound and the sum of these bounds remains finite.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="The spectrum of a hot body reconstructed mode by mode">

**Situation:** the reduced spectrum is approximated by $S_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{-n x}/n^2$, where $x$ is the inverse of the reduced temperature. At $x=0{,}05$, the first $20$ modes give $S_{20}(0{,}05)\approx1{,}438$.
**Question:** can the omitted modes change this number by more than $0{,}02$? And does a small variation in $x$ around $0{,}05$ indeed produce a small variation in the complete spectrum?
**The obstacle:** each omitted mode is tiny, but there are infinitely many of them; controlling them one by one does not control their cumulative effect.
**The contract:** this chapter gives you 3 tools (reading partial sums, summing numerical bounds, distinguishing three levels of guarantee). By the end, we will answer both questions with numbers to support the conclusions.

</Mission>

## The battle plan

To make the spectrum safe, we need three tools.

1. **Turn an infinite sum of curves into a sequence of finite curves**: know which object must have a limit.
2. **Replace each curve with a summable number**: obtain a guarantee that holds for every $x$ at once.
3. **Read implications without reversing them**: recognise what is stronger, disprove the converses and restrict the domain when necessary.

## 1. From terms to partial sums

**Tool 1 — look at the right sequence of functions.** An infinite sum is never handled directly: we begin by keeping only finitely many terms.

Problem: writing $\sum f_n(x)$ does not yet tell us which function this expression represents.
We therefore need an object built from finite sums whose limit we can study.

**Definition 1 (series of functions).** Let $(f_n)_{n\ge1}$ be a family of functions defined on the same domain $D$. A <Def id="serie-de-fonctions">**series of functions**</Def> (a sum in which each term is a curve) is the object denoted by $\sum_{n\ge1}f_n$. Its $N$-th <Terme id="somme-partielle">partial sum</Terme> is the function

$$S_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_n(x).$$

If the sequence $(S_N)$ has a limit $S$, then $S$ is called the sum function of the series.

**Example (physics) 1.** For the hot-body spectrum, the term of index $n$ and the partial sum are

$$f_n(x)=\frac{e^{-n x}}{n^2},\qquad S_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{e^{-n x}}{n^2},\qquad x\in[0,+\infty).$$

At $x=0{,}05$, each $S_N(0{,}05)$ is a measurable number; as $x$ varies, $S_N$ is a continuous curve obtained by superimposing $N$ modes.

**Remark (laboratory notation).** In a TP (lab session), you would instead write the intensity as $I_N(T)$ in terms of the temperature $T$, with $x=1/T$. Same object, different outfit: here $x$ simplifies the exponential decay of each mode.

Problem: the sequence $(S_N)$ is a sequence of functions; the previous chapter showed that “having a limit” can then mean two things.
We therefore need to specify whether we fix $x$ or require the same control over all of $D$.

**Reminder 1 (two readings of the sum).** The series has <Terme id="convergence-simple">pointwise convergence</Terme> to $S$ on $D$ if, for every fixed $x$, the <Terme id="serie-numerique">numerical series</Terme> $\sum f_n(x)$ has sum $S(x)$. It has <Terme id="convergence-uniforme">uniform convergence</Terme> if

$$\|S_N-S\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in D}|S_N(x)-S(x)|\longrightarrow0,$$

where $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ is the <Terme id="norme-uniforme">uniform norm</Terme> from the previous chapter.

| Question asked | Object whose limit is taken | May the index depend on $x$? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What does a detector placed at a fixed $x$ see? | $S_N(x)$, a sequence of numbers | yes |
| Is the entire curve close to $S$? | $\|S_N-S\|_\infty$, a single number | no |

**Example 2.** On $D=[0,1]$, let $f_n(x)=x^n-x^{n+1}$. The sum telescopes:

$$S_N(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}(x^n-x^{n+1})=1-x^{N+1}.$$

For $x<1$, $S_N(x)\to1$; for $x=1$, $S_N(1)=0$. The sum is therefore $1$ on $[0,1)$ and $0$ at $1$. We will soon make use of the jump at $1$.

**Remark.** Letting the general term $f_n(x)$ tend to $0$ is never enough: it is only a necessary condition. The series is governed by the sequence $(S_N)$, not by the sequence $(f_n)$ alone.

## 2. Summing bounds: the practitioner's tool

**Tool 2 — replace functions with numbers.** We seek a condition strong enough to control all tails of the series at once, at every point in the domain.

Problem: calculating $\|S_N-S\|_\infty$ exactly requires knowing $S$, precisely the function we are looking for.
We therefore need a control that requires only the individual terms $f_n$.

**Definition 2 (normal convergence).** The series $\sum f_n$ has <Def id="convergence-normale">**normal convergence**</Def> on $D$ (the maximum sizes of the terms are themselves summable) if the numerical series

$$\sum_{n\ge1}\|f_n\|_\infty$$

converges, with $\|f_n\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in D}|f_n(x)|$.

**Example (physics) 3.** On $D=[0,+\infty)$, $0<e^{-n x}\le1$, with equality at $x=0$. Thus, for the spectrum,

$$\|f_n\|_\infty=\sup_{x\ge0}\frac{e^{-n x}}{n^2}=\frac1{n^2}.$$

The series $\sum 1/n^2$ is a <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> with exponent $2$, so it converges. The spectrum series converges normally on all of $[0,+\infty)$.

**Remark.** The absolute value is essential. Cancellation between signs can make $\sum f_n(x)$ converge, but normal convergence measures the total cost without any cancellation.

**Theorem 1 (from normal to uniform).** Every series of functions that converges normally on $D$ converges uniformly on $D$, and therefore pointwise on $D$.

<Collapsible title="Proof — controlling all tails at once">

Suppose that $\sum\|f_n\|_\infty$ converges. For $p>q$, the triangle inequality gives, for every $x\in D$,

$$
\left|\sum_{n=q+1}^{p}f_n(x)\right|
\le\sum_{n=q+1}^{p}|f_n(x)|
\le\sum_{n=q+1}^{p}\|f_n\|_\infty.
$$

The right-hand side is the tail of a convergent numerical series: it tends to $0$ as $q$ tends to infinity, independently of $x$ and of $p>q$. For each $x$, the partial sums therefore form a Cauchy sequence of real numbers and have a limit $S(x)$.

Then, letting $p$ tend to infinity in the inequality, we obtain

$$|S(x)-S_q(x)|\le\sum_{n=q+1}^{+\infty}\|f_n\|_\infty.$$

The bound does not depend on $x$ and tends to $0$: the convergence is uniform. It is therefore pointwise as well, since fixing one point is less demanding than controlling all points at once. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Theorem 1 cannot be read backwards: if $\sum\|f_n\|_\infty$ diverges, it tells us nothing. The series of functions may still converge uniformly thanks to cancellation between terms; the alternating series in section 3 will show this.

Problem: the exact supremum of each $f_n$ may be tedious to calculate, while a simple bound would suffice.
We therefore need a test that accepts bounds $M_n$ slightly larger than the exact norms.

**Theorem 2 (majorant test).** The <Def id="critere-de-weierstrass">**Weierstrass M-test**</Def> says that if numbers $M_n\ge0$ satisfy

$$\forall x\in D,\quad |f_n(x)|\le M_n,\qquad\text{et}\qquad\sum_{n\ge1}M_n<+\infty,$$

then $\sum f_n$ converges normally, hence uniformly and pointwise on $D$.

**Justification.** We have $\|f_n\|_\infty\le M_n$; the comparison test for positive series makes $\sum\|f_n\|_\infty$ convergent, and Theorem 1 then gives the result. $\blacksquare$

**Remark (inconclusive case).** A non-summable majorant does not prove that the series of functions diverges. It only says that **this choice** of majorants does not allow you to apply the test. Look for a sharper bound, restrict the domain, or exploit the signs of the terms.

**Method 1 (applying Weierstrass).** On the requested domain:

1. isolate the term $f_n(x)$;
2. take its absolute value;
3. eliminate $x$ with a supremum or a bound $|f_n(x)|\le M_n$;
4. name the reference numerical series $\sum M_n$;
5. write the chain “Weierstrass, hence normal, hence uniform, hence pointwise”.

**Example (physics) 4.** For the spectrum, choosing $M_n=1/n^2$ gives more than a verdict: it bounds the <Terme id="reste-d-une-serie">remainder</Terme> after $N$ modes,

$$\sup_{x\ge0}|S(x)-S_N(x)|\le\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2}\le\int_N^{+\infty}\frac{dt}{t^2}=\frac1N.$$

With $N=20$, the error is therefore at most $0{,}05$ everywhere. At the specific point $x=0{,}05$, the exponential will give a sharper bound in the Solved Mission.

**Proposition 1 (continuity of the sum).** If every $f_n$ is continuous on $D$ and if $\sum f_n$ converges uniformly on $D$, then its sum $S$ is continuous on $D$.

<Collapsible title="Proof — splitting epsilon into three parts">

Fix $x_0\in D$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Uniform convergence provides $N$ such that $\|S-S_N\|_\infty<\varepsilon/3$. The finite sum $S_N$ is continuous at $x_0$; in a neighbourhood of $x_0$, we therefore have $|S_N(x)-S_N(x_0)|<\varepsilon/3$. Thus,

$$|S(x)-S(x_0)|\le |S(x)-S_N(x)|+|S_N(x)-S_N(x_0)|+|S_N(x_0)-S(x_0)|<\varepsilon.$$

The same index $N$ controls both ends because the convergence is uniform. Therefore $S$ is continuous at $x_0$. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## 3. Positioning the implications and their two false converses

**Tool 3 — know what a proof allows.** A strong condition can be very useful without being necessary; you must keep the arrows pointing in the right direction.

**Proposition 2 (chain of the three types of convergence).** For a series of functions on a domain $D$,

$$\boxed{\text{convergence normale}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\text{convergence uniforme}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\boxed{\text{convergence simple}}.$$

The first arrow is Theorem 1. The second follows directly from the fact that a bound valid for every $x$ remains valid when a single $x$ is fixed.

| Arrow | Verdict | Counterexample to the converse |
| --- | --- | --- |
| normal $\Rightarrow$ uniform | always true | $\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^n/(n+x^2)$ on $\mathbb R$ |
| uniform $\Rightarrow$ pointwise | always true | $\sum_{n\ge0}(x^n-x^{n+1})$ on $[0,1]$ |

**Counterexample 1 (pointwise without uniform).** The statement “pointwise convergence $\Rightarrow$ uniform convergence” is false. Consider

$$\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(x^n-x^{n+1})\quad\text{sur }[0,1].$$

The pointwise-convergence hypothesis is indeed satisfied because Example 2 gives a limit at every point: $S(x)=1$ for $x<1$ and $S(1)=0$. Yet the conclusion of uniform convergence fails because every $S_N$ is continuous while $S$ is not; Proposition 1 would rule out this jump if the convergence were uniform.

We can also exhibit the witness from the previous chapter: with $x_N=2^{-1/(N+1)}$,

$$|S_N(x_N)-S(x_N)|=x_N^{N+1}=\frac12.$$

The maximum gap therefore does not tend to $0$.

**Counterexample 2 (uniform without normal).** The statement “uniform convergence $\Rightarrow$ normal convergence” is false. Consider

$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n+x^2}\quad\text{sur }\mathbb R.$$

The uniform-convergence hypothesis is indeed satisfied because the <Terme id="critere-de-leibniz">Leibniz test</Terme>, applied for every $x$, gives a remainder satisfying

$$
\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}|S(x)-S_N(x)|
\le\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}\frac1{N+1+x^2}
=\frac1{N+1}
\longrightarrow0.
$$

Yet the conclusion of normal convergence fails because

$$\left\|\frac{(-1)^n}{n+x^2}\right\|_\infty=\frac1n$$

and the <Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme> $\sum1/n$ diverges. Alternation saves uniform convergence, but the norm of each term precisely erases that alternation. This is the mechanism behind exercise math-an-td4-02.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Failing to prove normal convergence therefore does not settle uniform convergence. After a failed Weierstrass test, ask yourself whether the signs alternate or whether the terms cancel before drawing a conclusion.

**Method 2 (restricting the domain).** When the majorant depends dangerously on a boundary:

1. identify the value of $x$ where the supremum is too large;
2. work on a domain that stays a distance $a>0$ from that boundary;
3. recalculate $\|f_n\|_\infty$ on this domain;
4. apply Weierstrass with a majorant that depends on $a$, but no longer on $x$;
5. state “normally on each restricted domain”, without unjustifiably joining the domains back together.

**Example (physics) 5.** Consider a less strongly damped version of the same spectrum,

$$\widetilde S(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-n x}}n.$$

On $[a,+\infty)$ with $a>0$,

$$\sup_{x\ge a}\frac{e^{-n x}}n=\frac{e^{-n a}}n\le e^{-n a}.$$

The geometric series $\sum e^{-n a}$ converges: Weierstrass gives normal convergence on every $[a,+\infty)$. On $(0,+\infty)$, however, the supremum is still $1/n$; normal convergence fails. At $x=0$, the series becomes harmonic and diverges. The boundary must therefore be treated separately.

## Worked example — reaching the right verdict with only what is necessary

**Example 6 — a damped exponential on a half-line.** Study

$$F(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-n x}}{n^2}\quad\text{sur }[0,+\infty).$$

**Step 1 — isolate the term and the domain.** Let $f_n(x)=e^{-n x}/n^2$ for $x\ge0$.

*Why?* Method 1 begins by fixing the domain: the supremum of $e^{-n x}$ would not be the same if values $x<0$ were allowed.

**Step 2 — eliminate the variable.** Since $e^{-n x}\le1$ on the domain,

$$|f_n(x)|\le\frac1{n^2}=M_n.$$

*Why?* Theorem 2 requires a number $M_n$ independent of $x$ to control every point with the same index.

**Step 3 — name the reference series.** The series $\sum M_n=\sum1/n^2$ is a Riemann series with exponent $2$, so it converges.

*Why?* A bound is useful only if the series of majorants is recognised and declared convergent; this is Weierstrass's numerical hypothesis.

**Step 4 — write all useful arrows.** By Theorem 2, $\sum f_n$ converges normally, hence uniformly, hence pointwise on $[0,+\infty)$. Since every $f_n$ is continuous, Proposition 1 also gives the continuity of $F$.

*Why?* Naming each arrow separates the test used from its consequences and prevents us from turning Weierstrass into an equivalence.

**Step 5 — quantify the remainder.** For every $N\ge1$,

$$\|F-S_N\|_\infty\le\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2}\le\frac1N.$$

*Why?* The qualitative verdict does not answer a request for precision; the proof of Theorem 1 directly provides a uniform bound on the remainder. This reasoning prepares you for exercise math-an-td4-01.

**Example 7 — your turn, with less help.** On $\mathbb R$, study the series

$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n+x^2}$$

from the pointwise, uniform and then normal perspectives.

**Your turn:** first find a uniform bound on the alternating remainder, then calculate the norm of the general term.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

**Step 1 — check convergence at every point.** For fixed $x$, $1/(n+x^2)$ decreases to $0$; the Leibniz test therefore gives pointwise convergence.

*Why?* Before discussing uniform error, we need to know that a sum function $S(x)$ exists at every point.

**Step 2 — make the remainder bound independent of $x$.** The Leibniz test gives

$$|S(x)-S_N(x)|\le\frac1{N+1+x^2}\le\frac1{N+1}.$$

*Why?* The last majorant no longer contains $x$ and tends to $0$: this is exactly the uniform guarantee recalled in section 1.

**Step 3 — test the stronger level.** The norm of the term is $1/n$, so the series of norms diverges.

*Why?* Definition 2 requires the norms to be summable; the harmonic series shows that this stronger condition fails despite uniformity.

The series therefore converges uniformly, but not normally. It is the canonical counterexample to the converse of the first arrow.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**Why does Weierstrass work term by term when uniform convergence concerns the entire remainder?**

Because the triangle inequality assembles the controls: $|\sum_{n>N}f_n(x)|\le\sum_{n>N}|f_n(x)|\le\sum_{n>N}M_n$. The numerical series of the $M_n$ thus turns infinitely many local bounds on the terms into a single global bound on the entire tail. This strategy is powerful, but it loses sign cancellation — hence its failure on the alternating series.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**“The term $f_n(x)$ tends to zero, so the series converges.”** No: this test is necessary but never sufficient. You must study the partial sums $S_N$, or apply a test concerning the whole series.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Choose one constant majorant for every index.** Writing $|f_n(x)|\le1$ gives $M_n=1$, but $\sum1$ diverges. Weierstrass requires a summable sequence of majorants, not merely bounded terms.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Read Weierstrass as an equivalence.** If the majorant you found is not summable, the test is silent. The alternating series $\sum(-1)^n/(n+x^2)$ converges uniformly even though the series of its norms diverges.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Join domains together without a common index.** “Normally on $[a,+\infty)$ for every $a>0$” does not mean “normally on $(0,+\infty)$”. The majorant and the index may deteriorate as $a$ approaches the boundary.

</Pitfall>

**1. Construct the spectrum and control the entire domain.** The terms $f_n(x)=e^{-n x}/n^2$ are defined on $[0,+\infty)$ and Example (physics) 3 gives $\|f_n\|_\infty=1/n^2$. Since $\sum1/n^2$ converges, Theorem 2 gives normal convergence, and then Theorem 1 gives uniform and pointwise convergence of $S_N$ to $S$.

**2. Bound the omitted modes at the requested point.** At $x=0{,}05$ and for $n\ge21$, we have $1/n^2\le1/21^2$. Since the terms are positive,

$$
0\le S(0{,}05)-S_{20}(0{,}05)
\le\frac1{21^2}\sum_{n=21}^{+\infty}e^{-0{,}05n}
=\frac{e^{-1{,}05}}{21^2(1-e^{-0{,}05})}
\approx0{,}0163.
$$

Thus the omitted modes change $1{,}438$ by less than $0{,}02$; more precisely, the complete spectrum is guaranteed to lie between $1{,}438$ and $1{,}455$ with the displayed rounding.

**3. Complete the continuity checklist at $x_0=0{,}05$.**

- **Hypothesis on the terms:** every $f_n(x)=e^{-n x}/n^2$ is continuous on $[0,+\infty)$.
- **Hypothesis on the approximations:** every $S_N$, a finite sum of continuous functions, is continuous.
- **Global control:** Weierstrass is satisfied with $M_n=1/n^2$, so $S_N\to S$ uniformly.
- **Named theorem, then conclusion:** Proposition 1 on the uniform limit of continuous functions applies; $S$ is continuous on $[0,+\infty)$, in particular at $x_0=0{,}05$.

Both decisions in the mission are therefore settled: twenty modes guarantee an error below $0{,}02$ here, and the complete spectrum varies continuously around $x=0{,}05$.

## Lesson summary

You now have the three abilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="Key points">

- **Read the series:** study the partial sums $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_n$, not just the general term $f_n$.
- **Prove normal convergence:** calculate or bound $\|f_n\|_\infty$, then recognise the numerical series $\sum\|f_n\|_\infty$.
- **Apply Weierstrass:** find $|f_n(x)|\le M_n$ with $M_n$ independent of $x$ and $\sum M_n$ convergent.
- **Quantify:** the numerical tail $\sum_{n>N}M_n$ uniformly bounds the remainder $|S-S_N|$.
- **Keep the arrows pointing the right way:** normal $\Rightarrow$ uniform $\Rightarrow$ pointwise, never the reverse without additional proof.
- **Know the two counterexamples:** the telescoping series $\sum(x^n-x^{n+1})$ is pointwise but not uniform; the alternating series $\sum(-1)^n/(n+x^2)$ is uniform but not normal.
- **Localise:** if the supremum misbehaves at the boundary, start again on a domain that stays a positive distance from that boundary, without joining the domains back together.

</KeyResults>
