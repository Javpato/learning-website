<Toc depth={2} />

<GuidingQuestion>

We approximate an infinite sum of functions by its first $N$ terms. **How can we guarantee that one same index $N$ makes the error small at every point of the domain, and how can that guarantee be turned into a usable numerical bound?**

</GuidingQuestion>

## 1. Seeing a series as a sequence of partial sums

Lesson 05 fixed the vocabulary: a sequence of functions is read either point by point, or with one same index valid everywhere. A series of functions creates no new object of convergence; it supplies one particular sequence of functions, that of its partial sums.

Problem: the symbol $\sum f_n$ already suggests an infinite sum, whereas no sum function has yet been constructed. We must start again from finite objects.

**Definition 1 (series of functions).** Let $(f_n)_{n\ge1}$ be a family of functions from $D$ into $\mathbb R$. The <Def id="serie-de-fonctions">**series of functions**</Def> $\sum_{n\ge1}f_n$ is studied through the sequence of its <Terme id="somme-partielle">partial sums</Terme>

$$S_N:D\to\mathbb R,\qquad S_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_n(x).$$

It converges pointwise on $D$ when the sequence $(S_N)$ converges <Terme id="convergence-simple">pointwise</Terme> in the sense of Definition 1 of lesson 05. Its limit $S$ is then called the **sum function**:

$$S(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}f_n(x)\qquad\text{for each }x\in D.$$

When $S$ exists, the <Terme id="reste-d-une-serie">remainder</Terme> after index $N$ is the function

$$R_N=S-S_N,\qquad R_N(x)=\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}f_n(x).$$

That last expression describes a limit of finite sums; it does not grant the right to manipulate an infinite sum spontaneously.

**Example 2.** On $D=[0,+\infty)$, consider

$$f_n(x)=\frac{e^{-nx}}{n^2},\qquad S_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{e^{-nx}}{n^2}.$$

With $x$ fixed, $\sum e^{-nx}/n^2$ is a <Terme id="serie-convergente">convergent series</Terme>, since $0\le e^{-nx}/n^2\le1/n^2$ and the <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> of exponent $2$ converges. This proves the pointwise convergence of the $S_N$ to a function $S$. It does not yet supply an index common to all $x$.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** The condition $f_n(x)\to0$ for each $x$ is necessary, but it does not suffice to make the numerical series $\sum f_n(x)$ converge. The decisive object remains $(S_N)$, exactly as the decisive object of a numerical series is its sequence of partial sums.

### Mini-exercises

1. For $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1)$, which sequence of functions must be studied?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   One must study $S_N(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}x^n$. The fact that $f_n(x)\to0$ with $x$ fixed is only a necessary condition; the convergence of the series concerns $(S_N)$.

   </Collapsible>

2. If $S_N\to S$ pointwise, what is $R_N(x)$ at a fixed point $x$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   We have $R_N(x)=S(x)-S_N(x)$, so $R_N(x)\to0$. The index obtained may still depend on $x$: this is only pointwise information.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Measuring the remainder uniformly

Problem: pointwise convergence allows a different index at each point. To approximate the whole sum function at once, we must take up the notion of lesson 05 with the particular sequence $(S_N)$.

The series $\sum f_n$ converges <Terme id="convergence-uniforme">uniformly</Terme> to $S$ on $D$ when its partial sums $S_N$ converge uniformly to $S$ on $D$.

**Theorem 3 (uniform criterion through the remainder).** Suppose $\sum f_n$ converges pointwise to $S$ on $D$. It converges uniformly on $D$ if and only if $R_N$ is bounded from some index on and

$$\sup_{x\in D}|R_N(x)|\xrightarrow[N\to+\infty]{}0.$$

<Proof title="the remainder is exactly the gap between the partial sums and the sum">

**Idea.** There are not two criteria to compare: by definition, the remainder $R_N$ is precisely the gap $S-S_N$. Theorem 1 of lesson 05 then translates uniform convergence into convergence of the uniform norm.

Suppose first that $S_N\to S$ uniformly. By the <Terme id="norme-uniforme">uniform norm</Terme> criterion of lesson 05,

$$\|S_N-S\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in D}|S_N(x)-S(x)|\longrightarrow0.$$

Now $R_N=S-S_N$, so $|R_N(x)|=|S_N(x)-S(x)|$ at every point. Hence

$$\sup_{x\in D}|R_N(x)|=\|S_N-S\|_\infty\longrightarrow0.$$

Conversely, suppose that supremum is finite from some index on and tends to $0$. For every $x\in D$,

$$|S_N(x)-S(x)|=|R_N(x)|\le\sup_{y\in D}|R_N(y)|.$$

The right-hand side no longer depends on $x$ and tends to $0$. The same index therefore works for the whole domain: $S_N\to S$ uniformly.

**What the proof teaches.** For a series, the uniform gap is not a new quantity to invent: it is the largest size of the remainder, and any uniform estimate of the remainder is already a proof of uniform convergence. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Theorem 3 is an equivalence, but it assumes $S$ is known in order to form $R_N=S-S_N$. When the sum is unknown, the criterion is unusable as it stands; that means neither convergence nor divergence.

**Theorem 4 (uniform Cauchy criterion for a series).** By Theorem 3 of lesson 05, the series $\sum f_n$ converges uniformly on $D$ if and only if

$$\forall\varepsilon>0, \exists N, \forall p>q\ge N, \forall x\in D,\qquad \left|\sum_{n=q+1}^{p}f_n(x)\right|\le\varepsilon.$$

<Proof title="apply Cauchy to the partial sums">

**Idea.** The criterion of lesson 05 applies to the sequence $(S_N)$. It suffices to recognise that the difference of two partial sums is a finite tail of the series.

For $p>q$, the finite identity

$$S_p(x)-S_q(x)=\sum_{n=q+1}^{p}f_n(x)$$

transforms the uniform Cauchy criterion of lesson 05 word for word into the statement above. In the difficult direction, the completeness of $\mathbb R$ first constructs $S(x)$ at each point; the common index of the criterion then makes that convergence uniform. No passage to an infinite sum is needed before its existence has been obtained.

**What the proof teaches.** The Cauchy criterion is the right tool when $S$ is unknown: it compares only two finite approximations and demands that all their tails be small with one common index. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If a crude bound on a finite tail does not tend to $0$, the criterion has not failed: only that bound has. On the other hand, exhibiting tails that stay large for arbitrarily high indices does disprove uniform convergence, without necessarily settling pointwise convergence.

### Mini-exercises

1. We know that $|R_N(x)|\le 4/N$ for every $x\in D$. Which convergence do we obtain?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The supremum of the remainder is at most $4/N$, which tends to $0$. Theorem 3 gives the uniform convergence of the series on $D$.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why can Theorem 4 be applied before $S$ is known?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   It contains only the terms $f_n$ and finite tails $\sum_{n=q+1}^{p}f_n$. The sum function is constructed by the proof, thanks to the completeness of $\mathbb R$ recalled in lesson 05.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. Summing the largest sizes of the terms

Theorem 4 demands control of every finite tail. The triangle inequality suggests a robust mechanism: replace each function by its largest size, then study a positive numerical series.

Problem: a different bound at each point gives no common index. We must measure each term over the whole domain before summing those measurements.

**Definition 5 (normal convergence).** The series $\sum f_n$ converges <Def id="convergence-normale">**normally**</Def> on $D$ when the numerical series of the uniform norms converges:

$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\|f_n\|_\infty<+\infty,\qquad \|f_n\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in D}|f_n(x)|.$$

Normal convergence therefore requires the summability of the largest sizes **term by term**. It deliberately ignores cancellations of sign.

**Proposition 6 (normal convergence implies uniform convergence).** If $\sum f_n$ converges normally on $D$, then it converges uniformly on $D$.

<Proof title="dominate the remainder by a numerical tail">

**Idea.** A convergent numerical series has arbitrarily small tails. The triangle inequality carries that smallness over to every tail of functions, uniformly in $x$.

Suppose $\sum\|f_n\|_\infty$ convergent. For $p>q$ and every $x\in D$,

$$\left|\sum_{n=q+1}^{p}f_n(x)\right| \le\sum_{n=q+1}^{p}|f_n(x)| \le\sum_{n=q+1}^{p}\|f_n\|_\infty.$$

The numerical series on the right converges. By its Cauchy criterion, for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $N$ such that its tail is below $\varepsilon$ as soon as $p>q\ge N$. That index depends neither on $p$, nor on $q$, nor on $x$. Theorem 4 therefore gives the uniform convergence of $\sum f_n$ to a function $S$.

We may then let $p$ tend to $+\infty$ in the weak inequality, with $q$ and $x$ fixed:

$$|R_q(x)|\le\sum_{n=q+1}^{+\infty}\|f_n\|_\infty.$$

Taking the supremum in $x$,

$$\|R_q\|_\infty\le\sum_{n=q+1}^{+\infty}\|f_n\|_\infty\longrightarrow0.$$

**What the proof teaches.** Normal convergence is effective because the tail of a convergent numerical series becomes an envelope for the whole remainder; that envelope no longer contains $x$ and supplies the common index we were after. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Theorem 7 (Weierstrass criterion).** The <Def id="critere-de-weierstrass">**Weierstrass criterion**</Def> states that if there exist numbers $M_n\ge0$ such that

$$\forall x\in D,\qquad |f_n(x)|\le M_n,\qquad \text{and}\qquad \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}M_n<+\infty,$$

then $\sum f_n$ converges normally, hence uniformly, on $D$.

<Proof title="from summable bounds to summable norms">

**Idea.** The bound $M_n$ holds for every $x$; it therefore also bounds the supremum of the values of $|f_n|$.

For each $n$, the hypothesis gives $\|f_n\|_\infty\le M_n$. The comparison criterion for positive series implies the convergence of $\sum\|f_n\|_\infty$. Definition 5 gives normal convergence, and Proposition 6 then gives uniform convergence.

**What the proof teaches.** Weierstrass allows a simple envelope rather than the exact computation of each norm, but that envelope must be independent of $x$ and must form a convergent numerical series. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Finding $|f_n(x)|\le M_n$ with $\sum M_n$ divergent proves no divergence. The bound may be too crude, or the signs may produce cancellations that absolute values erase.

### Mini-exercises

1. On $[0,+\infty)$, which bound suits $f_n(x)=e^{-nx}/n^3$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   We have $|f_n(x)|\le1/n^3$. The Riemann series of exponent $3$ converges; Theorem 7 gives normal, then uniform, convergence.

   </Collapsible>

2. Is a bound $|f_n(x)|\le1/n$ enough to apply Weierstrass?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No: the <Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme> diverges. The criterion is inconclusive with that bound; the series of functions may still converge uniformly.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. Keeping the implications the right way round

We now have three levels of control. Comparing them avoids two symmetric errors: demanding too strong a hypothesis when uniformity suffices, or concluding uniformity from purely pointwise information.

**Proposition 8 (chain of convergences).** For every series of functions on $D$,

$$\boxed{\text{normal convergence}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\text{uniform convergence}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\text{pointwise convergence}}.$$

<Proof title="assembling the two arrows">

**Idea.** The first arrow is a tail estimate; the second is a weakening of the control demanded.

Proposition 6 proves the first implication. For the second, if $S_N\to S$ uniformly, the index supplied for all $x$ works in particular for each fixed $x$. This is Proposition 1 of lesson 05 applied to the partial sums.

**What the proof teaches.** Each arrow forgets information: uniformity may come from cancellations invisible to the norms of the terms, and pointwise convergence may use indices that vary with the point. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Counterexample 9 (the implication "uniform implies normal" fails).** On $\mathbb R$, consider

$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n+x^2}.$$

For each $x$, the sequence $a_n(x)=1/(n+x^2)$ decreases to $0$. The <Terme id="critere-de-leibniz">Leibniz criterion</Terme> gives the existence of the sum $S(x)$ and the estimate by the first omitted term:

$$|R_N(x)|\le\frac1{N+1+x^2}\le\frac1{N+1}.$$

Theorem 3 therefore gives uniform convergence on $\mathbb R$. Yet

$$\left\|\frac{(-1)^n}{n+x^2}\right\|_\infty=\frac1n,$$

and $\sum1/n$ diverges. The series is not normally convergent. The alternation controls the remainder, whereas the norm of each term erases that cancellation.

**Counterexample 10 (the implication "pointwise implies uniform" fails).** Take again the example $x^N$ of lesson 05 in telescoping form:

$$\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(x^n-x^{n+1})\qquad\text{on }[0,1].$$

We have the finite identity

$$S_N(x)=1-x^{N+1}.$$

For $x<1$, $S_N(x)\to1$; at $x=1$, $S_N(1)=0$. The convergence is therefore pointwise to the function equal to $1$ on $[0,1)$ and $0$ at $1$. But, for $x<1$,

$$|R_N(x)|=x^{N+1},\qquad \sup_{x\in[0,1]}|R_N(x)|=1.$$

The supremum is not attained, but it does not tend to $0$. One may also take again the <Terme id="suite-temoin">witness sequence</Terme> $x_N=2^{-1/(N+1)}$ of lesson 05: $|R_N(x_N)|=1/2$. Theorem 3 rules out uniform convergence.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Failing to obtain normal convergence says nothing in itself about uniformity; failing to obtain uniformity says nothing in itself about pointwise convergence. Counterexamples 9 and 10 name exactly the two converses that fail.

### Mini-exercises

1. A series converges uniformly and the series of its norms diverges. Is there a contradiction?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No. Proposition 8 does not assert "uniform implies normal". Counterexample 9 is precisely uniform without being normal.

   </Collapsible>

2. In Counterexample 10, why is the point $x=1$ not enough as a witness?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   At $x=1$ the gap is always $0$. The defect approaches $1$ without reaching it; a moving point $x_N$ is therefore needed, as in the method of lesson 05.

   </Collapsible>

## 5. Recovering normality by staying away from a boundary

On an open or unbounded domain, the supremum explores points arbitrarily close to a dangerous boundary. Normality often appears on every domain that stays at a positive distance from that boundary, without holding on the whole domain.

**Method 11 (restrict before bounding).**

1. locate the boundary where $|f_n(x)|$ is largest;
2. fix a distance $a>0$ from that boundary;
3. compute the supremum on the restricted domain;
4. compare the norms obtained with a named numerical series;
5. announce the exact domain, without merging conclusions that have different indices.

**Example 12.** Consider first

$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n}\qquad\text{on }(-1,1).$$

Fix $0<a<1$. On the segment $[-a,a]$,

$$\left\|\frac{x^n}{n}\right\|_{\infty,[-a,a]}=\frac{a^n}{n}\le a^n.$$

The <Terme id="serie-geometrique">geometric series</Terme> $\sum a^n$ converges, so Theorem 7 gives normal convergence on each $[-a,a]$. On the whole of $(-1,1)$, however,

$$\sup_{|x|<1}\frac{|x|^n}{n}=\frac1n,$$

and the series of norms diverges. The series is therefore not normal on $(-1,1)$.

The same mechanism holds on a half-line. For

$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-nx}}n\qquad\text{on }(0,+\infty),$$

we obtain, on each $[a,+\infty)$ with $a>0$,

$$\left\|\frac{e^{-nx}}n\right\|_{\infty,[a,+\infty)}=\frac{e^{-na}}n\le e^{-na}.$$

The series $\sum e^{-na}$ is geometric with ratio $e^{-a}<1$: the convergence is normal on each $[a,+\infty)$. On $(0,+\infty)$ the supremum equals $1/n$, so normality fails.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** "Normal on $[-a,a]$ for every $a<1$" does not mean "normal on $(-1,1)$", and "normal on $[a,+\infty)$ for every $a>0$" does not mean "normal on $(0,+\infty)$". The bound and the index depend on $a$ and deteriorate as $a$ approaches the boundary.

### Mini-exercises

1. On which segment can you bound $|x|^n/n$ by $(3/4)^n$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   On $[-3/4,3/4]$, since $|x|^n/n\le|x|^n\le(3/4)^n$. The associated geometric series converges, so the series of functions converges normally there.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why does the bound $e^{-na}$ prove nothing when $a=0$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   It becomes $1$, and the series $\sum1$ diverges. This corresponds to the boundary where the exponential damping disappears.

   </Collapsible>

## 6. Turning a proof into a numerical guarantee

A qualitative convergence says that an index exists. To compute an approximation, we must extract from the proof a formula that lets us choose that index before computing.

**Proposition 13 (uniform bound on the remainder).** Under the hypotheses of Theorem 7, for every $N$,

$$\sup_{x\in D}|R_N(x)|\le\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}M_n.$$

<Proof title="keep the tail instead of discarding it">

**Idea.** The proof of Proposition 6 already contains the estimate; it suffices to keep its value instead of retaining only that it tends to $0$.

For $p>N$ and every $x\in D$,

$$|S_p(x)-S_N(x)|\le\sum_{n=N+1}^{p}|f_n(x)|\le\sum_{n=N+1}^{p}M_n.$$

Letting $p$ tend to $+\infty$, both sides converge and the weak inequality gives

$$|R_N(x)|\le\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}M_n.$$

The right-hand side does not depend on $x$; it therefore bounds the supremum of the left-hand side.

**What the proof teaches.** The same computation proves uniformity and certifies the error: an explicit numerical tail becomes a guaranteed tolerance for the whole sum function. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Method 14 (choosing a computational index).**

1. fix the tolerance $\varepsilon$ and the domain on which it must hold;
2. obtain a summable bound $M_n$;
3. bound or compute the tail $\sum_{n>N}M_n$;
4. solve the inequality $\sum_{n>N}M_n\le\varepsilon$;
5. announce the chosen index and the guarantee, with its domain.

For Example 2, $M_n=1/n^2$. The series-integral comparison gives

$$\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2}\le\int_N^{+\infty}\frac{dt}{t^2}=\frac1N.$$

So $N=100$ guarantees $\sup_{x\ge0}|R_N(x)|\le0{,}01$.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** An upper bound larger than the tolerance does not prove that the actual error is too large. It only says that this estimate does not certify it; one may increase $N$ or look for a finer tail on a smaller domain.

### Mini-exercises

1. With the bound $\|R_N\|_\infty\le1/N$, which index guarantees an error of at most $0{,}02$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   It suffices to take $N\ge50$. At index $50$ the bound equals exactly $0{,}02$.

   </Collapsible>

2. If an estimate gives $\|R_{20}\|_\infty\le0{,}05$, can we assert that the error exceeds $0{,}02$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No. A bound of $0{,}05$ allows a much smaller actual error. It simply does not yet certify the tolerance $0{,}02$.

   </Collapsible>

## Worked example — certifying a global and a local approximation

Let us study

$$S(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-nx}}{n^2}\qquad\text{on }[0,+\infty),$$

then compare a guarantee valid everywhere with an estimate at the point $x=0{,}05$.

**Step 1 — identify the finite approximations.** Set $f_n(x)=e^{-nx}/n^2$ and $S_N(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}f_n(x)$.

*Why?* Definition 1 requires treating the series as the sequence $(S_N)$; it also fixes the remainder we shall try to bound.

**Step 2 — build a uniform bound.** For $x\ge0$ we have $0<e^{-nx}\le1$, so

$$|f_n(x)|\le\frac1{n^2}=M_n.$$

*Why?* Theorem 7 demands an envelope independent of $x$, and $\sum1/n^2$ is a convergent Riemann series.

**Step 3 — conclude at the right level.** The Weierstrass criterion gives normal convergence; Proposition 8 then gives uniform, and then pointwise, convergence on $[0,+\infty)$.

*Why?* Writing each arrow separates the hypothesis actually verified from its consequences, and prevents reversing the criterion.

**Step 4 — choose an index for the whole domain.** Proposition 13 and the integral comparison give

$$\sup_{x\ge0}|R_N(x)|\le\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2}\le\frac1N.$$

For a global error of at most $0{,}02$, index $N=50$ suffices.

*Why?* A uniform demand must be met with a bound that remains true at the worst point of the domain, here $x=0$.

**Step 5 — refine at the requested point.** At $x=0{,}05$ and for $n\ge21$ we have $1/n^2\le1/21^2$. By positivity,

$$0\le R_{20}(0{,}05) \le\frac1{21^2}\sum_{n=21}^{+\infty}e^{-0{,}05n} =\frac{e^{-1{,}05}}{21^2(1-e^{-0{,}05})} \approx0{,}0163.$$

*Why?* A guarantee at a single point can exploit the factor $e^{-0{,}05n}$ that the global supremum loses; twenty terms suffice here for the tolerance $0{,}02$, without sufficing to certify it everywhere.

<Collapsible title="Your turn — answer">

On $[0{,}2,+\infty)$, show that

$$\sup_{x\ge0{,}2}|R_N(x)| \le\frac{e^{-0{,}2(N+1)}}{(N+1)^2(1-e^{-0{,}2})}.$$

For $n\ge N+1$ and $x\ge0{,}2$ we have $e^{-nx}/n^2\le e^{-0{,}2n}/(N+1)^2$. The sum of the bounds is geometric, whence the formula. It illustrates Method 11: removing the neighbourhood of $0$ sharply accelerates the decay of the remainder.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Confusing the term with the partial sum.** Showing $f_n(x)\to0$ does not make $\sum f_n(x)$ converge. The series is the sequence $(S_N)$ of Definition 1.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Using the remainder before having a sum.** The expression $R_N=S-S_N$ presupposes that $S$ exists. When it is unknown, start with the finite tails of Theorem 4.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Reading Weierstrass as an equivalence.** A non-summable bound leaves Theorem 7 silent. Counterexample 9 converges uniformly without converging normally.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the domain in a norm.** The norm of $e^{-nx}/n$ equals $e^{-na}/n$ on $[a,+\infty)$ but $1/n$ on $(0,+\infty)$. The normality verdict therefore depends on the domain.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Turning a bound into an exact value.** If $\|R_N\|_\infty\le0{,}05$, the error may be $0{,}01$. A bound that is too large does not refute the requested precision; it simply fails to certify it.

</Pitfall>

    <QExplain>The supremum controls every point. That bound certifies the uniform error, without necessarily proving normal convergence.</QExplain>

  </QItem>
</Quiz>

<GuidingAnswer>

To guarantee that one same index works everywhere, you must control the sequence of partial sums in the uniform norm. If the sum $S$ is known, Theorem 3 reduces the question exactly to $\sup_D|R_N|\to0$. If it is not, Theorem 4 demands that all the finite tails be small with one common index.

The most robust route is to bound $|f_n(x)|$ by a summable number $M_n$. Theorem 7 then gives normal convergence, Proposition 8 gives uniform and then pointwise convergence, and Proposition 13 keeps the tail $\sum_{n>N}M_n$ as a numerical bound on the error. That route is sufficient, not necessary: alternation may give uniformity without normality, while a merely pointwise convergence may leave the worst gap untouched.

</GuidingAnswer>

## Summary — choosing the right level of control

<KeyResults title="The decision sheet">

| Situation | Tool | Conclusion | Inconclusive case |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $S$ known, $\sup_D\lvert R_N\rvert \to0$ | Theorem 3 | uniform convergence | without $S$, forming $R_N$ is impossible |
| $S$ unknown, finite tails uniformly small | Theorem 4 | uniform convergence | a crude bound that does not tend to zero settles nothing |
| $\sum\lVert f_n\rVert _\infty<+\infty$ | Definition 5 and Proposition 6 | normal, then uniform, convergence | divergence of the norms does not exclude uniformity |
| $\lvert f_n(x)\rvert \le M_n$ and $\sum M_n<+\infty$ | Theorem 7 | normal, uniform, pointwise | a non-summable bound settles nothing |
| domain away from a boundary | Method 11 | often normal on each restricted domain | do not merge the indices when the boundary returns |
| explicit numerical tail | Proposition 13 | uniform bound on the error | a bound that is too large may simply be imprecise |

**Counterexamples worth being able to reconstruct.** Counterexample 9 shows that "uniform implies normal" is false: Leibniz gives $\|R_N\|_\infty\le1/(N+1)$, but the norms form the harmonic series. Counterexample 10 shows that "pointwise implies uniform" is false: the telescoping series coming from $x^N$ keeps a supremum of the remainder equal to $1$.

**Proof ideas to take away.** A series of functions is a sequence of partial sums; the difference $S_p-S_q$ is a finite tail; the triangle inequality dominates it by a numerical tail; letting $p$ tend to infinity preserves that bound for the remainder; and solving an inequality on that tail finally turns the proof into a computational guarantee.

</KeyResults>
