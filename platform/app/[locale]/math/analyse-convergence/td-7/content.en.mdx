<ExerciseView id="math-an-td7-01">
<Statement>

Let $D$ denote the triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, and $(0,1)$.

1. Describe $D$ using vertical slices.
2. Calculate the <Terme id="integrale-double">double integral</Terme>

$$
\iint_D x y\,dx\,dy
$$

using <Terme id="theoreme-de-fubini">Fubini's theorem</Terme>.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>Draw the three sides: two lie on the axes and the third has equation $x+y=1$.</Hint>
<Hint>For fixed $x$, the vertical slice starts at the horizontal axis and ends at the line $y=1-x$.</Hint>
<Hint>After integrating with respect to $y$, expand $x(1-x)^2$ before integrating with respect to $x$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

$D=\{(x,y):x\ge0,\ y\ge0,\ x+y\le1\}$ and $\displaystyle\iint_Dxy\,dx\,dy=1/24$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Domain.** Before writing the bounds, let us describe the region. The
triangle is bounded by the two axes and the line with equation $x+y=1$. It is
therefore the part of the first quadrant lying below this line:

$$
D=\bigl\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2:x\ge0,\ y\ge0,\ x+y\le1\bigr\}.
$$

A vertical slice is non-empty when $0\le x\le1$; for this fixed $x$, it runs
from $y=0$ to $y=1-x$.

**2. Fubini checklist.**

1. The domain $D$ is the compact triangle described above.
2. The function $(x,y)\mapsto xy$ is continuous on $D$, so it is integrable.
3. Fubini's theorem allows the double integral to be calculated through two
   successive integrations.
4. Using the vertical slices obtained in question 1, we may therefore write

$$
\iint_Dxy\,dx\,dy
=\int_0^1\left(\int_0^{1-x}xy\,dy\right)dx.
$$

**3. Calculation.** The inner integration gives

$$
\int_0^{1-x}xy\,dy
=x\left[\frac{y^2}{2}\right]_0^{1-x}
=\frac{x(1-x)^2}{2}.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\iint_Dxy\,dx\,dy
=\frac12\int_0^1\left(x-2x^2+x^3\right)dx
=\frac12\left(\frac12-\frac23+\frac14\right)
=\frac1{24}.
$$

**Conclusion.** The integrand is positive on $D$, and the result is indeed
positive: the requested integral is $1/24$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Writing the bounds without identifying the triangle.** The sloping line is $x+y=1$, so the upper edge of the slice is $1-x$, not $x$.
- **Letting $y$ vary from $0$ to $1$ for every $x$.** Those bounds describe the unit square and add the region above the diagonal.
- **Forgetting that $x$ is constant in the inner integral.** When integrating with respect to $y$, the result is $xy^2/2$, not $x^2y/2$.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td7-02">
<Statement>

Consider

$$
I=\int_0^1\left(\int_y^1 e^{x^2}\,dx\right)dy.
$$

1. Interpret $I$ as a <Terme id="integrale-double">double integral</Terme> and describe its domain.
2. Reverse the order of integration using <Terme id="theoreme-de-fubini">Fubini's theorem</Terme>.
3. Hence find the exact value of $I$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>The given bounds mean that the point $(x,y)$ satisfies $y\le x$ in the unit square.</Hint>
<Hint>After drawing the triangle, fix $x$: the vertical slice starts at $y=0$ and reaches the diagonal $y=x$.</Hint>
<Hint>In the new order, integrating with respect to $y$ produces the factor $x$ that was missing in order to recognise the derivative of $e^{x^2}$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

The domain is $D=\{(x,y):0\le y\le x\le1\}$; after reversing the order, $\displaystyle I=\int_0^1\int_0^x e^{x^2}\,dy\,dx=(e-1)/2$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Domain.** Before choosing any new bounds, let us read the region being
covered. The point $(x,y)$ remains in the unit square, to the right of the
line $x=y$; this is the triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, and $(1,1)$.
Thus

$$
D=\bigl\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2:0\le y\le x\le1\bigr\}.
$$

The initial description sweeps through this triangle using horizontal
slices: for fixed $y$, $x$ runs from the diagonal to the right-hand side. To
reverse the order, we sweep through it using vertical slices: for fixed $x$,
$y$ runs from the lower side to the diagonal.

**2. Fubini checklist.**

1. The domain $D$ is the compact triangle described above.
2. The function $(x,y)\mapsto e^{x^2}$ is continuous on $D$, so it is integrable.
3. Fubini's theorem permits both orders of integration and states that they
   give the same double integral.
4. The vertical description of the domain therefore leads to

$$
I=\int_0^1\left(\int_0^x e^{x^2}\,dy\right)dx.
$$

**3. Calculation.** Since the integrand does not depend on $y$,

$$
\int_0^x e^{x^2}\,dy=x e^{x^2}.
$$

We then recognise the derivative of $e^{x^2}$ up to a factor of $2$:

$$
I=\int_0^1x e^{x^2}\,dx
=\frac12\left[e^{x^2}\right]_0^1
=\frac{e-1}{2}.
$$

**Conclusion.** The initial order leads to a non-elementary antiderivative
of $e^{x^2}$; describing the same domain in the other direction makes the
calculation immediate. We obtain $I=(e-1)/2$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Looking for an antiderivative of $e^{x^2}$ in the given order.** It is not elementary; the methodological clue is precisely the bound $x=y$, which allows the slices to be reversed.
- **Writing $0\le x\le y\le1$.** This inequality describes the other half of the square; the initial bounds instead impose $y\le x$.
- **Reversing the differentials without reconsidering the domain.** Going from $dx\,dy$ to $dy\,dx$ requires both pairs of bounds to be recalculated from the triangle.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td7-03">
<Statement>

Let

$$
D=\bigl\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2:x\ge0,\ y\ge0,\ x^2+y^2\le1\bigr\},
$$

be the quarter of the unit disc in the first quadrant. Calculate the
<Terme id="integrale-double">double integral</Terme>

$$
\iint_D\left(x^2+y^2\right)\,dx\,dy
$$

using the polar <Terme id="changement-de-variables">change of variables</Terme>.
Specify the parameter domain, its image, and the absolute value of the
<Terme id="jacobien">Jacobian</Terme>.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>In the first quadrant, the angle sweeps through a quarter turn and the radius starts at the centre and reaches the unit circle.</Hint>
<Hint>With $x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$, the expression $x^2+y^2$ becomes $r^2$.</Hint>
<Hint>Calculate the absolute value of the determinant of the transformation: it adds a factor $r$ to the integrand.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

For $Q=[0,1]\times[0,\pi/2]$ and $\Phi(r,\theta)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)$, we have $\Phi(Q)=D$ and $|\det D\Phi|=r$; the integral is $\pi/8$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Domain.** Before writing the polar bounds, let us describe the region.
The domain $D$ is the part of the unit disc in the first quadrant: the radius
starts at the origin and ends at the unit circle, while the angle sweeps
through the sector between the two positive half-axes.

**2. Parameter domain and image.** Set

$$
\Phi(r,\theta)=\bigl(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta\bigr).
$$

The parameter domain is

$$
Q=\left\{(r,\theta):0\le r\le1,\ 0\le\theta\le\frac\pi2\right\}.
$$

Its image is exactly the quarter-disc: $\Phi(Q)=D$; only the boundary $r=0$
has several values of $\theta$, which has no effect on the integral.

**3. Absolute value of the Jacobian.** The derivative matrix is

$$
D\Phi(r,\theta)=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&r\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its determinant is

$$
\det D\Phi(r,\theta)
=r\cos^2\theta+r\sin^2\theta=r.
$$

Since $r\ge0$ on $Q$, the **absolute value** of the Jacobian is
$|\det D\Phi(r,\theta)|=r$. The change-of-variables theorem therefore gives
$dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta$.

**4. Calculation.** On the image, $x^2+y^2=r^2$. With the image domain and
the Jacobian factor now established,

$$
\iint_D\left(x^2+y^2\right)\,dx\,dy
=\int_0^{\pi/2}\left(\int_0^1 r^2\,r\,dr\right)d\theta
=\int_0^{\pi/2}\left[\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^1d\theta
=\frac\pi8.
$$

**Conclusion.** By the change-of-variables theorem in polar coordinates, the
integral over the quarter-disc is $\pi/8$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Using the whole disc.** The constraints $x\ge0$ and $y\ge0$ restrict the angle to the first quadrant; taking a full turn multiplies the answer by $4$.
- **Replacing $x^2+y^2$ with $r$ instead of $r^2$.** The radius satisfies $r^2=x^2+y^2$; the additional factor $r$ comes separately from the absolute value of the Jacobian.
- **Writing only “the Jacobian is $r$”.** A complete solution gives the domain $Q$, its image $\Phi(Q)=D$, and the absolute value $|\det D\Phi|=r$ before the transformed integral.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td7-04">
<Statement>

Let $a>0$ and $b>0$, and let

$$
E=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2:\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}\le1\right\}.
$$

Calculate the area of the ellipse $E$, written as a
<Terme id="integrale-double">double integral</Terme>, using the
<Terme id="changement-de-variables">change of variables</Terme>
$(u,v)\mapsto(au,bv)$. Specify the initial domain, its image, and the absolute
value of the <Terme id="jacobien">Jacobian</Terme>, then check the case $a=b$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>Dividing the coordinates of a point in the ellipse by $a$ and $b$ turns its inequality into that of the unit disc.</Hint>
<Hint>The parameter domain is the unit disc $B=\{(u,v):u^2+v^2\le1\}$, and the transformation maps it onto the entire ellipse.</Hint>
<Hint>The matrix of $(u,v)\mapsto(au,bv)$ is diagonal; the absolute value of its determinant gives the factor by which the area of the unit disc is multiplied.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

For $B=\{(u,v):u^2+v^2\le1\}$ and $\Phi(u,v)=(au,bv)$, we have $\Phi(B)=E$ and $|\det D\Phi|=ab$; thus $\operatorname{Aire}(E)=\pi ab$, and $a=b$ gives back the area $\pi a^2$ of a disc.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Domain.** Before choosing any bounds or transforming the integral, let
us describe the region. Since $a$ and $b$ are positive, $E$ is the filled
ellipse centred at the origin, with semi-axis $a$ horizontally and $b$
vertically. Its area is written as

$$
\operatorname{Aire}(E)=\iint_E1\,dx\,dy.
$$

**2. Initial domain and image.** Consider the unit disc

$$
B=\bigl\{(u,v)\in\mathbb R^2:u^2+v^2\le1\bigr\}
$$

and the map $\Phi(u,v)=(au,bv)$. If $(u,v)\in B$, then

$$
\frac{(au)^2}{a^2}+\frac{(bv)^2}{b^2}=u^2+v^2\le1,
$$

so $\Phi(u,v)\in E$. Conversely, if $(x,y)\in E$, then
$(u,v)=(x/a,y/b)\in B$ and $\Phi(u,v)=(x,y)$. The image domain is therefore
exactly $\Phi(B)=E$.

**3. Absolute value of the Jacobian.** The derivative matrix is constant:

$$
D\Phi(u,v)=
\begin{pmatrix}
a&0\\
0&b
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det D\Phi(u,v)=ab.
$$

The **absolute value** of the Jacobian is

$$
|\det D\Phi(u,v)|=|ab|=ab,
$$

because $a>0$ and $b>0$.

**4. Calculation.** The change-of-variables theorem, applied from the disc
$B$ to its image $E$, gives

$$
\operatorname{Aire}(E)
=\iint_B 1\cdot|\det D\Phi(u,v)|\,du\,dv
=ab\iint_B1\,du\,dv
=ab\,\pi.
$$

**5. Checking the case $a=b$.** If $a=b$, the inequality defining $E$
becomes $x^2+y^2\le a^2$: $E$ is the disc of radius $a$. The formula obtained
gives $\pi ab=\pi a^2$, exactly its known area.

**Conclusion.** By the change-of-variables theorem, the area of the ellipse
with semi-axes $a$ and $b$ is $\pi ab$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Using the ellipse as the parameter domain.** The transformation starts from the unit disc $B$ and its image is the ellipse $E$; reversing these roles leads to the factor $1/(ab)$.
- **Multiplying by $a+b$.** A horizontal dilation multiplies areas by $a$ and a vertical dilation by $b$: the absolute value of the Jacobian is their product $ab$.
- **Giving $ab$ without an absolute value or a sign assumption.** The theorem uses $|\det D\Phi|$; only here do the assumptions $a>0$ and $b>0$ allow us to write $|ab|=ab$.

</CommonErrors>
