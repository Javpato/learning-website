<GuidingQuestion>

An arbitrary <Terme id="serie-de-fonctions">series of functions</Terme> can converge on a complicated set: isolated points, a union of intervals, almost anything. But as soon as the functions are the monomials $a_nx^n$, the domain of convergence is **always** an interval centred at $0$ — never anything else. **Where does that rigidity come from, and what exactly happens at its endpoints?**

</GuidingQuestion>

## 1. A single bound controls a whole interval

**Definition 1 (power series).** A <Def id="serie-entiere">**power series**</Def> is a series of functions of the form

$$
\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n,
$$

where the $a_n$ are fixed numbers, called the coefficients, and $x$ is the variable.

Problem: for a general series of functions, knowing what happens at one point tells us nothing about the others. Here, a **very weak** piece of information at a single point will control a whole interval. We must understand why.

**Lemma 1 (Abel's lemma).** <Def id="lemme-d-abel">**Abel's lemma**</Def> states the following: if there exists $r>0$ such that the sequence $(a_nr^n)$ is **bounded**, then for every $x$ with $|x|<r$ the series $\sum a_nx^n$ converges **absolutely**.

<Proof title="one bound, plus a geometric factor">

**Idea.** The hypothesis does not even assume convergence at $r$: only that the terms do not blow up. All the work will be done by the ratio $|x|/r$, strictly less than $1$, which manufactures a dominating <Terme id="serie-geometrique">geometric series</Terme>.

Let $M$ be such that $|a_nr^n|\le M$ for every $n$. For $|x|<r$, write the central manoeuvre — making $r^n$ appear artificially:

$$
|a_nx^n|=|a_nr^n|\cdot\left|\frac{x}{r}\right|^{n}\le M\,q^{\,n},
\qquad\text{where }q=\frac{|x|}{r}<1 .
$$

The series $\sum Mq^n$ is geometric with ratio $q\in[0,1)$, hence convergent. The <Terme id="critere-de-comparaison">comparison test</Terme> for <Terme id="serie-a-termes-positifs">series with positive terms</Terme> then gives the convergence of $\sum|a_nx^n|$, that is the <Terme id="convergence-absolue">absolute convergence</Terme> announced.

**What the proof teaches.** This is geometric domination again — the mechanism of <Terme id="regle-de-d-alembert">d'Alembert's rule</Terme> and of <Terme id="regle-de-cauchy">Cauchy's rule</Terme>. What is new is the point of view: we do not study an isolated $x$, we **carry** information from a point $r$ across to all points nearer the origin. It is that transportability, and it alone, that will make the domain symmetric. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** The lemma says nothing **at** $r$. That $(a_nr^n)$ is bounded implies neither convergence nor divergence at $r$: take $a_n=1$ and $r=1$, where the sequence is bounded and the series diverges.

**Useful contrapositive.** If $\sum a_nx_0^{\,n}$ diverges at a point $x_0$, then it diverges at every $x$ with $|x|>|x_0|$ — otherwise the lemma, applied from that $x$, would make it converge at $x_0$.

### Mini-exercises

1. We know that $\sum a_n3^n$ converges. What can be said at $x=2$? And at $x=3$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Convergence at $3$ forces $a_n3^n\to0$, so $(a_n3^n)$ is bounded. Lemma 1 gives absolute convergence for $|x|<3$, in particular at $x=2$. At $x=3$ itself we only know that the series converges, not that it converges absolutely.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why does the lemma ask only for a bounded sequence, and not a convergent one?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because the proof uses only the bound $|a_nr^n|\le M$. Weakening the hypothesis as far as possible makes the lemma applicable more often — and it is this weak version that allows the radius to be defined as a supremum.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. The radius of convergence, and the trichotomy

Problem: Lemma 1 says that the set of "acceptable" $r$ is hereditary downwards. Such a set is an interval, and is therefore described by a single number: its supremum.

**Definition 2 (radius of convergence).** The <Def id="rayon-de-convergence">**radius of convergence**</Def> of $\sum a_nx^n$ is

$$
R=\sup\bigl\{\,r\ge0\ :\ (a_nr^n)\ \text{is bounded}\,\bigr\}\in[0,+\infty] .
$$

**Theorem 1 (trichotomy).** With this $R$:

1. if $|x|<R$, the series converges absolutely;
2. if $|x|>R$, the series diverges **grossly**, that is, its general term does not tend to $0$;
3. if $|x|=R$, no general conclusion is possible.

<Proof title="reading a supremum in both directions">

**Idea.** A supremum is used twice: below it there is always an element of the set, above it there is none. Each of the two uses gives one of the two points.

**Point 1.** Let $|x|<R$. By the characterisation of the supremum there exists $r$ in the set with $|x|<r\le R$; that is, $(a_nr^n)$ is bounded. Lemma 1 applied with that $r$ gives absolute convergence at $x$.

**Point 2.** Let $|x|>R$. If $(a_nx^n)$ were bounded, then $|x|$ would belong to the set, so $|x|\le R$ — a contradiction. The sequence $(a_nx^n)$ is therefore unbounded; in particular it does not tend to $0$, and the necessary condition for convergence of a series is violated.

**Point 3.** There is nothing to prove: it is an absence of conclusion, illustrated below by four series of the same radius with different boundary behaviour.

**What the proof teaches.** Point 2 gives **gross** divergence, far stronger than mere divergence: outside the radius, no compensation by signs is conceivable. That is why the boundary is the only place requiring work. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Definition 3 (open interval of convergence).** The <Def id="intervalle-ouvert-de-convergence">**open interval of convergence**</Def> is $(-R,R)$: the set of points where Theorem 1 guarantees absolute convergence, endpoints excluded.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** At the boundary anything can happen, and the radius does not predict it. Four series of radius $1$:

| Series | At $x=1$ | At $x=-1$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\sum x^n$ | diverges grossly | diverges grossly |
| $\sum x^n/n$ | diverges (<Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic</Terme>) | converges (<Terme id="critere-de-leibniz">Leibniz</Terme>) |
| $\sum x^n/n^2$ | converges absolutely | converges absolutely |
| $\sum x^n/\bigl(n(n+1)\bigr)$ | converges absolutely | converges absolutely |

All four have the same radius and three distinct boundary behaviours. **An endpoint is studied point by point, with the tools of arc 1** — never by extrapolation from the interior.

### Mini-exercises

1. A power series converges at $x=-4$ and diverges at $x=5$. What do we know about $R$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Convergence at $-4$ gives $R\ge4$; divergence at $5$ gives $R\le5$. So $4\le R\le5$, and we cannot do better without more information.

   </Collapsible>

2. Can $R=0$ occur? And $R=+\infty$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Yes to both. For $a_n=n!$, the sequence $(n!\,r^n)$ is bounded for no $r>0$, so $R=0$ and the series converges only at $0$. For $a_n=1/n!$, it is bounded for every $r$, so $R=+\infty$.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. Computing the radius — and recognising when you cannot

Definition 2 is perfect for proving and dreadful for computing. In practice we apply the criteria of arc 1 to the numerical series $\sum|a_nx^n|$, with $x$ fixed, and read off the threshold.

**Proposition 1 (radius by the ratio or by the root).** Assume the $a_n$ are non-zero from some index on.

$$
\text{If }\ \left|\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}\right|\longrightarrow L\in[0,+\infty],\qquad\text{then }R=L .
$$

$$
\text{If }\ |a_n|^{1/n}\longrightarrow \ell\in(0,+\infty),\qquad\text{then }R=\frac1\ell .
$$

<Proof title="apply d'Alembert to the series of moduli, with x fixed">

**Idea.** We do not guess $R$: we ask d'Alembert's rule for which values of $x$ the series of moduli converges, and the threshold it produces **is** the radius.

Fix $x\ne0$ and set $u_n=|a_nx^n|>0$. Then

$$
\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|\,|x|\longrightarrow\frac{|x|}{L}.
$$

D'Alembert's rule gives convergence if $|x|/L<1$, that is $|x|<L$, and divergence if $|x|>L$. These two regions are exactly those of Theorem 1, which characterise $R$; so $R=L$. The argument with the root is identical, via Cauchy's rule and $u_n^{1/n}=|a_n|^{1/n}|x|$.

**What the proof teaches.** The radius is not a new notion: it is the threshold d'Alembert produces when given a series depending on a parameter. The whole of lesson 03 is reinvested here without modification. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Proposition 1 requires the limit to **exist**. It says nothing if the ratios oscillate, and above all it does not apply to the **lacunary** case, where infinitely many coefficients vanish: the ratio $a_{n+1}/a_n$ is not even defined. In that case, go back to the definition and study $\sum|a_nx^n|$ directly.

**Example 1 (lacunary series).** For $\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{x^{2n}}{2^{n}}$, every other coefficient vanishes. Set $y=x^2$: the series becomes $\sum y^n/2^n$, geometric with ratio $y/2$, which converges if and only if $|y|<2$. The original series therefore converges if and only if $x^2<2$, that is $|x|<\sqrt2$: the radius equals $\sqrt2$.

**Example 2 (factorials).** For $\sum\dfrac{(n!)^2}{(2n)!}\,x^n$, expand nothing: simplify the ratio.

$$
\left|\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}\right|=\frac{(n!)^2}{(2n)!}\cdot\frac{(2n+2)!}{\bigl((n+1)!\bigr)^2}=\frac{(2n+1)(2n+2)}{(n+1)^2}\longrightarrow4 ,
$$

so $R=4$.

### Mini-exercises

1. What is the radius of $\sum n^3x^n$, and what happens at the boundary?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The ratio $a_n/a_{n+1}=n^3/(n+1)^3\to1$, so $R=1$. At the boundary $n^3(\pm1)^n$ does not tend to $0$: gross divergence on both sides.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why does Proposition 1 fail for $\sum x^{n^2}$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because almost all the coefficients vanish: the ratio is undefined. With $x$ fixed we study $\sum|x|^{n^2}$ directly, which converges for $|x|<1$ and diverges for $|x|\ge1$; the radius equals $1$.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. Inside, everything becomes comfortable

Problem: Theorem 1 gives convergence **at each point** of $(-R,R)$. That is pointwise information, and lesson 07 showed that it authorises neither continuity, nor integration, nor differentiation. So a uniform control is needed.

**Theorem 2 (normal convergence on every interior segment).** For every $r$ with $0\le r<R$, the series $\sum a_nx^n$ converges <Terme id="convergence-normale">normally</Terme> on $[-r,r]$.

<Proof title="on a segment the sup is computable and the series of sups is already classified">

**Idea.** On $[-r,r]$ each monomial attains its maximum at the endpoints of the segment. The series of norms is then a numerical series evaluated at $r$ — and $r$ lies strictly inside, where we already know everything converges.

On $[-r,r]$, the <Terme id="norme-uniforme">uniform norm</Terme> of $x\mapsto a_nx^n$ equals

$$
\bigl\|a_nx^n\bigr\|_\infty=|a_n|\,r^{\,n}.
$$

Since $r<R$, point 1 of Theorem 1 applied at the point $x=r$ gives the convergence of $\sum|a_n|r^n$. The series of norms therefore converges: that is exactly the definition of normal convergence (lesson 06).

**What the proof teaches.** The **strict** inequality $r<R$ is indispensable: it is what places $r$ in the region of absolute convergence. At $r=R$ the argument collapses, and that is precisely why the boundary escapes this theorem. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Corollary.** On $(-R,R)$ the sum $S(x)=\sum a_nx^n$ is **continuous**: the convergence is normal, hence uniform, on every segment of $(-R,R)$, and Theorem 1 of lesson 07 applies.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** The convergence is in general **not** normal on the whole of $(-R,R)$. For $\sum x^n$ on $(-1,1)$ the uniform norm of $x^n$ equals $1$ at every index. We say "normal on every segment of the open interval", never "normal on the open interval" — the same trap as lesson 05 in different clothes.

**What this prepares.** Theorem 2 places power series exactly within the hypotheses of lesson 07. On every interior segment we shall therefore be able to integrate term by term and — the series of derivatives having the same radius — to differentiate term by term. This is the starting point of lesson 09.

**The boundary, one last time.** That $S$ is continuous on $(-R,R)$ says nothing about its limit at $\pm R$. The bridge between the interior and the boundary exists: it is Abel's radial theorem, which rests on the <Terme id="transformation-d-abel">Abel transformation</Terme> of lesson 04. We name it here without proving it; it is what will make legitimate the Leibniz formula left pending in lesson 07.

### Mini-exercises

1. Is the convergence of $\sum x^n/n$ normal on $(-1,1)$? On $[-0{,}9\,;0{,}9]$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Not on $(-1,1)$: the uniform norm of $x^n/n$ equals $1/n$ there, and $\sum1/n$ diverges. On $[-0{,}9\,;0{,}9]$ yes, since the norm equals $0{,}9^n/n\le0{,}9^n$, the term of a convergent geometric series.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why does the continuity of $S$ not follow directly from Theorem 1?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because Theorem 1 only provides convergence at each point, and a pointwise limit of continuous functions may be discontinuous (lesson 05). We must go through the normal convergence of Theorem 2.

   </Collapsible>

## Worked example — the radius, then each endpoint separately

Let us determine the **exact** domain of convergence of

$$
\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^n}{n\,3^{\,n}}\,x^{n}.
$$

**Step 1 — compute the radius.** The coefficients $a_n=(-1)^n/(n3^n)$ are all non-zero, so

$$
\left|\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}\right|=\frac{(n+1)\,3^{\,n+1}}{n\,3^{\,n}}=3\cdot\frac{n+1}{n}\longrightarrow3 ,
$$

and Proposition 1 gives $R=3$.

*Why?* We apply the criterion to the **coefficients alone**; the factor $x^n$ has already been taken into account in the proof of Proposition 1.

**Step 2 — conclude inside.** For $|x|<3$ the series converges absolutely (Theorem 1), and normally on every $[-r,r]$ with $r<3$ (Theorem 2); the sum is continuous there.

*Why?* The trichotomy settles the whole interior at once: it is the only place where the radius suffices to conclude.

**Step 3 — study $x=3$.** The general term becomes $(-1)^n/n$. The sequence $1/n$ decreases to $0$, so Leibniz's criterion gives convergence; but $\sum1/n$ diverges, so there is no absolute convergence. The series is <Terme id="semi-convergence">conditionally convergent</Terme> at $3$.

*Why?* Theorem 1 says nothing at $|x|=R$: we must become a numerical series again and reopen the arc 1 toolbox.

**Step 4 — study $x=-3$.** The general term becomes

$$
\frac{(-1)^n(-3)^n}{n\,3^{\,n}}=\frac{(-1)^{2n}}{n}=\frac1n .
$$

This is the harmonic series: it **diverges**.

*Why?* The two endpoints have no reason to resemble each other — here the signs cancel on one side and add up on the other. The exact domain is therefore $(-3,3]$: a centred interval, closed on one side only.

<Collapsible title="Your turn — answer">

Determine the exact domain of convergence of $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{x^{n}}{n^{2}}$.

The ratio of the coefficients equals $(n+1)^2/n^2\to1$, so $R=1$. At $x=1$ we obtain $\sum1/n^2$, a <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> with exponent $2>1$: convergent. At $x=-1$ we obtain $\sum(-1)^n/n^2$, which converges absolutely by comparison with the previous one.

The exact domain is therefore the closed segment $[-1,1]$, and on that segment the convergence is even normal, since $\|x^n/n^2\|_\infty=1/n^2$ is summable there. This is the comfortable case where the boundary poses no problem — but it still had to be checked.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Deducing the boundary from the interior.** The radius describes a frontier, not what happens on it. Four series of radius $1$ have different boundaries; each endpoint requires a numerical-series study.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Applying the ratio to a lacunary series.** If infinitely many $a_n$ vanish, $a_{n+1}/a_n$ does not exist. Substitute $y=x^2$, or study $\sum|a_nx^n|$ directly.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Writing "normal on $(-R,R)$".** The convergence is normal on every interior **segment**, not on the open interval in general.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confusing divergence with gross divergence.** Outside the radius the general term does not even tend to $0$: there is no point invoking Leibniz or a cancellation of signs for $|x|>R$.

</Pitfall>

<GuidingAnswer>

The rigidity comes from **Lemma 1**, and from it alone. It carries information from a point $r$ across to all points strictly nearer the origin, because the ratio $|x|/r$ manufactures a geometric factor of ratio less than $1$. The set of acceptable $r$ is therefore hereditary downwards — it is an interval — and such a set is entirely described by a single number, its supremum: the radius.

In other words, the symmetry of the domain is not a property of monomials as functions, but a consequence of the fact that $|a_nx^n|$ depends on $x$ only through $|x|$. That is also why, in a complex variable, the natural statement speaks of a **disc**: the real interval is only its trace, as Painlevé's epigraph suggests.

At the endpoints none of this survives: the geometric factor equals exactly $1$ and dominates nothing. The boundary becomes an ordinary numerical series again, to be handled with arc 1 — Riemann, Leibniz, comparison — and the four series of radius $1$ show that the different outcomes really do occur.

</GuidingAnswer>

## Summary — one number for the interior, one study per endpoint

<KeyResults title="The decision sheet">

| Situation | What we write | Conclusion | Inconclusive case |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $(a_nr^n)$ bounded | Lemma 1 | absolute convergence for $\lvert x\rvert <r$ | nothing at $r$ itself |
| $\lvert x\rvert <R$ | Theorem 1, point 1 | absolute convergence | — |
| $\lvert x\rvert >R$ | Theorem 1, point 2 | gross divergence | — |
| $\lvert x\rvert =R$ | ordinary numerical series | to be decided with arc 1 | the radius predicts nothing |
| ratio of coefficients convergent | Proposition 1 | $R$ equals that limit | lacunary: ratio undefined |
| segment $[-r,r]$ with $r<R$ | Theorem 2 | normal convergence, continuous sum | false on the whole open interval |

**Proofs worth being able to reconstruct.** Making $r^n$ appear so as to create the geometric factor $(|x|/r)^n$ (Lemma 1); reading a supremum in both directions to obtain the trichotomy; computing $\|a_nx^n\|_\infty=|a_n|r^n$ on a segment and recognising an already-classified numerical series.

**The idea to take away.** A power series introduces no new mechanism: it is the geometric domination of arc 1, applied to a family depending on a parameter. What is new is that a single number summarises the whole interior — and that this number says nothing about the boundary.

</KeyResults>
