<ExamProblem title="Exercise 1 — Theory question and differentiation under the integral sign" points={4}>

1. State precisely the theorem on <Terme id="derivation-sous-le-signe-integral">differentiating under the integral sign</Terme> for an integral that may be improper: hypotheses, conclusion and resulting formula.
2. For $x\in\mathbb R$, let

$$
A(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}\cos(xt)}{1+t}\,dt.
$$

   Show that $A$ is of class $C^1$ on $\mathbb R$ and give an integral expression for $A'(x)$. Every hypothesis of the theorem must be verified.

<Collapsible title="Corrigé (solution) and indicative barème (marking scheme)">

**1. Precise statement of the theorem (2 pts).** Let $I$ be an open interval of $\mathbb R$, let $J$ be an interval of integration and let $\varphi:I\times J\to\mathbb R$. Assume that:

1. for every $x\in I$, the function $t\mapsto\varphi(x,t)$ is integrable on $J$;
2. for almost every $t\in J$, the function $x\mapsto\varphi(x,t)$ is differentiable on $I$;
3. for every compact set $K\subset I$, there is a function $g_K$ integrable on $J$ such that, for every $x\in K$ and almost every $t\in J$,

$$
\left|\partial_x\varphi(x,t)\right|\le g_K(t).
$$

Then the function

$$
F(x)=\int_J\varphi(x,t)\,dt
$$

is differentiable on $I$ and

$$
F'(x)=\int_J\partial_x\varphi(x,t)\,dt.
$$

If, in addition, $x\mapsto\partial_x\varphi(x,t)$ is continuous for almost every $t$ — with the same local domination on each compact set — then $F'$ is continuous and $F\in C^1(I)$. Award **1,25 pt** for the three hypotheses, **0,5 pt** for the differentiation formula and **0,25 pt** for the class $C^1$ conclusion.

**2. Application with hypothesis checklist (2 pts).** Let

$$
\varphi(x,t)=\frac{e^{-t}\cos(xt)}{1+t}
\qquad(x\in\mathbb R,\ t\ge0).
$$

1. **Domain and existence.** For every $x\in\mathbb R$, the function $t\mapsto\varphi(x,t)$ is continuous on $[0,+\infty)$ and

$$
|\varphi(x,t)|
\le e^{-t}.
$$

   Since $e^{-t}$ is integrable on $[0,+\infty)$, the integral defining $A(x)$ converges absolutely.
2. **Regularity.** For every $t\ge0$, the function $x\mapsto\varphi(x,t)$ is of class $C^1$ and

$$
\partial_x\varphi(x,t)
=-\frac{t e^{-t}\sin(xt)}{1+t}.
$$

3. **Domination.** For every compact set $K\subset\mathbb R$, every $x\in K$ and every $t\ge0$,

$$
\left|\partial_x\varphi(x,t)\right|
\le\frac{t}{1+t}e^{-t}
\le e^{-t}.
$$

   The dominating function $e^{-t}$ is integrable and does not depend on $x$.
4. **Theorem and conclusion.** The theorem on differentiating under the integral sign applies on every compact subset of $\mathbb R$. The partial derivative depends continuously on $x$; hence $A\in C^1(\mathbb R)$ and

$$
A'(x)
=-\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{t e^{-t}\sin(xt)}{1+t}\,dt.
$$

Award **0,5 pt** for existence, **0,5 pt** for calculating the partial derivative, **0,5 pt** for the integrable dominating function and **0,5 pt** for the conclusion with the formula for $A'$.

<ExamProblem title="Exercise 2 — Lacunary power series" points={5}>

Consider the <Terme id="serie-entiere">power series</Terme>

$$
S(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{2n}}{n3^n}.
$$

1. Explain why this series is lacunary and determine its <Terme id="rayon-de-convergence">radius of convergence</Terme>.
2. Study convergence separately at the two real endpoints of the interval of convergence.
3. For $x$ strictly inside this interval, justify <Terme id="derivation-terme-a-terme">term-by-term differentiation</Terme>, calculate $S'(x)$, then deduce a closed-form expression for $S(x)$.

<Collapsible title="Corrigé and indicative barème">

**1. Gaps and radius of convergence (1,5 pt).** Written in the form $\sum_{k\ge0}a_kx^k$, the series has all its odd-indexed coefficients equal to zero: only the degrees $2,4,6,\ldots$ appear. The ratio test applied without care to the successive coefficients $a_{k+1}/a_k$ is therefore not available.

Let $z=x^2/3$. The series becomes

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{z^n}{n}.
$$

It converges absolutely if $|z|<1$, hence if $|x|<\sqrt3$. If $|x|>\sqrt3$, its general term does not tend to $0$, because

$$
\left|\frac{x^{2n}}{n3^n}\right|
=\frac1n\left(\frac{x^2}{3}\right)^n.
$$

The radius is therefore

$$
R=\sqrt3.
$$

Award **0,5 pt** for identifying the gaps, **0,5 pt** for the condition $|x|<\sqrt3$ and **0,5 pt** for justifying divergence beyond the radius.

**2. Endpoint study (1 pt).** For $x=\sqrt3$ as well as for $x=-\sqrt3$, the power is even and

$$
\frac{x^{2n}}{n3^n}=\frac1n.
$$

The resulting series is the harmonic series, which diverges. The real interval of convergence is therefore $(-\sqrt3,\sqrt3)$, with neither endpoint included. Award **0,5 pt** for each endpoint correctly studied and justified using the reference series.

**3. Differentiation and closed-form expression (2,5 pts).** Within the radius, a power series may be differentiated term by term; the differentiated series has the same radius. Thus, for $|x|<\sqrt3$,

$$
S'(x)
=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{2n x^{2n-1}}{n3^n}
=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{2x^{2n-1}}{3^n}.
$$

For $x\ne0$, this is a geometric series with ratio $x^2/3$:

$$
S'(x)
=\frac2x\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{x^2}{3}\right)^n
=\frac2x\frac{x^2/3}{1-x^2/3}
=\frac{2x}{3-x^2}.
$$

The formula remains true at $x=0$, where both sides are equal to $0$. Since $S(0)=0$, integrate from $0$ to $x$:

$$
S(x)
=\int_0^x\frac{2u}{3-u^2}\,du
=-\ln\left(1-\frac{x^2}{3}\right)
\qquad(|x|<\sqrt3).
$$

Award **0,75 pt** for the theorem and its domain of application, **0,75 pt** for calculating the geometric series, **0,5 pt** for using $S(0)=0$ and **0,5 pt** for the final expression with its domain.

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>

<ExamProblem title="Exercise 3 — Parameter-dependent integral and asymptotic equivalent" points={5}>

For $x>0$, define the <Terme id="integrale-a-parametre">parameter-dependent integral</Terme>

$$
E(x)=\int_x^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t}\,dt.
$$

1. Show that $E(x)$ exists for every $x>0$ and that $E$ is continuous on $(0,+\infty)$.
2. Show that $E$ is differentiable, calculate $E'(x)$ and deduce its monotonicity.
3. Using integration by parts and an explicit bound on the remainder, establish an asymptotic equivalent of $E(x)$ as $x\to+\infty$.

<Collapsible title="Corrigé and indicative barème">

**1. Existence and continuity (1,5 pt).** Fix $x>0$. For $t\ge x$,

$$
0\le\frac{e^{-t}}t\le\frac{e^{-t}}x.
$$

Now

$$
\int_x^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}x\,dt
=\frac{e^{-x}}x<+\infty.
$$

By comparison, $E(x)$ exists. To establish continuity at a point $a>0$, write, for $x>0$,

$$
E(x)=E(a)-\int_a^x\frac{e^{-t}}t\,dt.
$$

The integrand is continuous on $(0,+\infty)$; the last integral tends to $0$ as $x\to a$. Hence $E(x)\to E(a)$ and $E$ is continuous. Award **1 pt** for the integrable comparison and **0,5 pt** for the continuity argument.

**2. Differentiation and monotonicity (1,5 pt).** Applying the fundamental theorem of calculus to the preceding expression gives

$$
E'(x)=-\frac{e^{-x}}x
\qquad(x>0).
$$

Since $e^{-x}/x>0$, we have $E'(x)<0$ for every $x>0$. The function $E$ is therefore strictly decreasing on $(0,+\infty)$. Award **1 pt** for the justified calculation of the derivative and **0,5 pt** for monotonicity.

**3. Asymptotic equivalent by integration by parts (2 pts).** On $[x,b]$, perform integration by parts with

$$
u(t)=\frac1t,
\qquad
dv(t)=e^{-t}\,dt,
\qquad
du(t)=-\frac1{t^2}\,dt,
\qquad
v(t)=-e^{-t}.
$$

Letting $b$ tend to $+\infty$, the term $e^{-b}/b$ tends to $0$, and we obtain

$$
E(x)
=\frac{e^{-x}}x
-\int_x^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t^2}\,dt.
$$

Denote the positive remainder by

$$
R(x)=\int_x^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t^2}\,dt.
$$

For $t\ge x$,

$$
0\le R(x)
\le\frac1{x^2}\int_x^{+\infty}e^{-t}\,dt
=\frac{e^{-x}}{x^2}.
$$

Consequently, when $x>1$,

$$
1-\frac1x
\le\frac{E(x)}{e^{-x}/x}
\le1.
$$

Both bounds tend to $1$; the squeeze theorem finally gives

$$
E(x)\sim\frac{e^{-x}}x
\qquad(x\to+\infty).
$$

Award **0,75 pt** for the integration by parts with the correct sign, **0,75 pt** for bounding the remainder and **0,5 pt** for the asymptotic equivalent concluded by squeezing.

</Collapsible>

<ExamProblem title="Exercise 4 — Two paths or a bound" points={3}>

Define the functions on $\mathbb R^2$

$$
f(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2},&(x,y)\ne(0,0),\\
0,&(x,y)=(0,0),
\end{cases}
$$

and

$$
g(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2},&(x,y)\ne(0,0),\\
0,&(x,y)=(0,0).
\end{cases}
$$

For each one, study the <Terme id="limite">limit</Terme> at $(0,0)$ and decide whether it is <Terme id="continuite">continuous</Terme> at that point. For $f$, use two paths; for $g$, give a bound valid in every direction.

<Collapsible title="Corrigé and indicative barème">

**1. Disproof using two paths for $f$ (1,5 pt).** Along the axis $y=0$, for $x\ne0$,

$$
f(x,0)=0,
$$

so the value tends to $0$ as $x\to0$. In contrast, along the line $y=x$, for $x\ne0$,

$$
f(x,x)=\frac{x^2}{2x^2}=\frac12.
$$

The two paths lead to different values. Therefore, the limit of $f$ at $(0,0)$ does not exist; in particular, $f$ is not continuous at $(0,0)$. Award **0,5 pt** for each calculated path and **0,5 pt** for the logical conclusion.

**2. Global proof using a bound for $g$ (1,5 pt).** For $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$,

$$
|g(x,y)|
=|y|\frac{x^2}{x^2+y^2}
\le|y|
\le\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
$$

The upper bound tends to $0$ as $(x,y)\to(0,0)$. By the squeeze theorem,

$$
\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}g(x,y)=0=g(0,0).
$$

Thus $g$ is continuous at $(0,0)$. Award **1 pt** for the path-independent bound and **0,5 pt** for the conclusion linking the limit and the value of the function. Testing a few paths would not be enough to prove this second conclusion: the global bound is what guarantees it.

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>

<ExamProblem title="Exercise 5 — Double integral over a parallelogram" points={3}>

Let

$$
D=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2\;\middle|\;0\le x+y\le1
\text{ et }0\le x-y\le1\right\}.
$$

Using the <Terme id="changement-de-variables">change of variables</Terme> $u=x+y$, $v=x-y$, calculate the integral

$$
I=\iint_D(x+y)e^{x-y}\,dx\,dy.
$$

Specify the transformed domain, the <Terme id="jacobien">Jacobian</Terme> and the new bounds.

<Collapsible title="Corrigé and indicative barème">

**1. Transformation of the domain (1 pt).** The proposed change directly gives

$$
0\le u\le1,
\qquad
0\le v\le1.
$$

The parallelogram $D$ is therefore transformed into the square $[0,1]^2$. The change is linear and invertible, with

$$
x=\frac{u+v}{2},
\qquad
y=\frac{u-v}{2}.
$$

The point is awarded for identifying the square and the inverse of the change.

**2. Calculation of the Jacobian (1 pt).** Calculate

$$
\left|\det\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|
=\left|\det
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac12&\frac12\\
\frac12&-\frac12
\end{pmatrix}
\right|
=\frac12.
$$

Thus $dx\,dy=\frac12\,du\,dv$. The point is awarded for the determinant taken in absolute value and the correct differential factor.

**3. Integration over the square (1 pt).** The integrand becomes $ue^v$. Fubini's theorem gives

$$
I
=\frac12\int_0^1\int_0^1ue^v\,dv\,du
=\frac12\left(\int_0^1u\,du\right)
\left(\int_0^1e^v\,dv\right)
=\frac{e-1}{4}.
$$

The point is awarded for transforming the integrand, the bounds and the final value.

</Collapsible>
