<ExamProblem title="Problem 1 — True or false: hypotheses and counterexamples" points={5}>

Decide whether each of the following statements is true or false. Every answer
must be justified by a proof or by a counterexample that satisfies the
hypotheses and makes the conclusion fail.

1. If the <Terme id="serie-convergente">series</Terme> $\sum_{n\ge1}u_n$ converges and the sequence $(v_n)$ is bounded, then $\sum_{n\ge1}u_nv_n$ converges.
2. If $a_n>0$ and $\dfrac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\to1$, then $\sum_{n\ge1}a_n$ diverges.
3. If $f_n$ converges <Terme id="convergence-uniforme">uniformly</Terme> to $f$ and each $f_n$ is differentiable, then $f$ is differentiable.

<Collapsible title="Solution and indicative barème (marking scheme)">

**1. False statement (1,5 pt).** Set

$$
u_n=\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt n}
\qquad\text{et}\qquad
v_n=(-1)^n.
$$

The series $\sum u_n$ converges by the <Terme id="critere-de-leibniz">Leibniz test</Terme>: the sequence $1/\sqrt n$ is decreasing and tends to $0$. The sequence $(v_n)$ is indeed bounded since $|v_n|=1$. Yet,

$$
u_nv_n=\frac1{\sqrt n},
$$

and $\sum 1/\sqrt n$ diverges: it is a <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> with exponent $1/2\le1$. Both hypotheses are satisfied, but the conclusion fails. Award **0,5 pt** for the convergence of $\sum u_n$, **0,5 pt** for the boundedness of $(v_n)$ and the product, then **0,5 pt** for the reference series and the conclusion.

**2. False statement (1,5 pt).** Consider the two positive sequences

$$
a_n=\frac1n
\qquad\text{et}\qquad
b_n=\frac1{n^2}.
$$

Their ratios satisfy

$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\frac{n}{n+1}\longrightarrow1,
\qquad
\frac{b_{n+1}}{b_n}=\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^2\longrightarrow1.
$$

Now $\sum a_n$ is the divergent harmonic series, whereas $\sum b_n$ is a convergent Riemann series with exponent $2>1$. The <Terme id="regle-de-d-alembert">ratio test</Terme> is inconclusive when the limit of the ratio is $1$: both behaviours remain possible. A single counterexample, $b_n=1/n^2$, is enough to disprove the statement; the pair shows why the test is silent. Award **0,5 pt** for the ratios, **0,5 pt** for the reference series and **0,5 pt** for the logical conclusion.

**3. False statement (2 pts).** On $\mathbb R$, take

$$
f_n(x)=\sqrt{x^2+\frac1{n^2}}.
$$

Each $f_n$ is differentiable. For every $x\in\mathbb R$,

$$
0\le f_n(x)-|x|
=\frac{1/n^2}{\sqrt{x^2+1/n^2}+|x|}
\le\frac1n,
$$

and equality holds at $x=0$. Thus $\|f_n-|\cdot|\|_\infty=1/n\to0$: the uniform convergence hypothesis is indeed satisfied. Yet the limit $f(x)=|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$, because its left- and right-hand derivatives are $-1$ and $1$, respectively. Award **0,5 pt** for choosing a differentiable family, **1 pt** for the proof of uniform convergence and **0,5 pt** for explicitly showing the failure of differentiability.

<ExamProblem title="Problem 2 — Numerical series and perturbation" points={5}>

1. Determine the convergence behaviour of the series

$$
\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{e^{1/n}-1}{\sqrt n}.
$$

2. Let $u_n=(-1)^n/\sqrt n$. Determine the convergence behaviour of $\sum_{n\ge1}u_n$, then of $\sum_{n\ge1}|u_n|$.
3. Finally, study $\sum_{n\ge2}\dfrac{u_n}{1+u_n}$. This last sum starts at index $2$ because $1+u_1=0$; removing finitely many terms does not change the convergence behaviour of a series.

<Collapsible title="Solution and indicative barème (marking scheme)">

**1. Reduction to a reference series (1,5 pt).** As $n\to+\infty$, $1/n\to0$ and

$$
e^t-1\sim t\qquad(t\to0).
$$

Consequently,

$$
\frac{e^{1/n}-1}{\sqrt n}
\sim\frac{1/n}{\sqrt n}
=\frac1{n^{3/2}}.
$$

The terms are positive. Since the term is an <Terme id="equivalent">asymptotic equivalent</Terme> of that of the Riemann series $\sum 1/n^{3/2}$, which converges because $3/2>1$, the proposed series converges. Award **0,5 pt** for the standard asymptotic equivalent, **0,5 pt** for naming the reference series and **0,5 pt** for the conclusion.

**2. Conditional convergence (1,5 pt).** The sequence $1/\sqrt n$ decreases to $0$; the Leibniz test therefore gives the convergence of

$$
\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt n}.
$$

On the other hand,

$$
\sum_{n\ge1}|u_n|=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{\sqrt n}
$$

diverges as a Riemann series with exponent $1/2\le1$. Thus $\sum u_n$ has <Terme id="semi-convergence">conditional convergence</Terme> and does not converge absolutely. Award **0,75 pt** for each of the two behaviours, with its test.

**3. Decomposition of the perturbation (2 pts).** For $n\ge2$, $|u_n|<1$, so $1+u_n>0$, and the identity

$$
\frac{u_n}{1+u_n}
=u_n-\frac{u_n^2}{1+u_n}
$$

is well defined. The first series on the right-hand side converges by question 2. For the second, its terms are positive and

$$
\frac{u_n^2}{1+u_n}
\sim u_n^2
=\frac1n,
$$

because $u_n\to0$. It therefore diverges by comparison with an asymptotic equivalent, the harmonic series. The partial sums of $\sum u_n$ have a finite limit, whereas those of $\sum u_n^2/(1+u_n)$ tend to $+\infty$; hence

$$
\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{u_n}{1+u_n}
$$

diverges, more precisely to $-\infty$. Award **0,75 pt** for the identity, **0,75 pt** for the asymptotic equivalent and the harmonic reference, then **0,5 pt** for the conclusion about the original series.

<ExamProblem title="Problem 3 — Convergence of a sequence of functions" points={5}>

For $n\ge1$, define on $\mathbb R$

$$
f_n(x)=\sqrt{x^2+\frac1{n^2}}.
$$

1. Determine the <Terme id="convergence-simple">pointwise</Terme> limit of $(f_n)$, then study uniform convergence on $\mathbb R$.
2. Compute $f_n'$ and study its pointwise and then uniform convergence on $\mathbb R$.
3. Let $a>0$. Study the uniform convergence of $(f_n')$ on $(-\infty,-a]\cup[a,+\infty)$.
4. Is the limit function of $(f_n)$ differentiable on $\mathbb R$? Explain what this example prevents us from concluding.

<Collapsible title="Solution and indicative barème (marking scheme)">

**1. Pointwise and uniform limit (1,5 pt).** For every fixed $x$,

$$
f_n(x)\longrightarrow\sqrt{x^2}=|x|.
$$

Multiplying by the conjugate expression,

$$
0\le f_n(x)-|x|
=\frac{1/n^2}{\sqrt{x^2+1/n^2}+|x|}
\le\frac{1/n^2}{1/n}
=\frac1n.
$$

The upper bound does not depend on $x$, and equality is attained at $x=0$. Thus

$$
\|f_n-|\cdot|\|_\infty=\frac1n\longrightarrow0.
$$

By the <Terme id="norme-uniforme">uniform norm</Terme> criterion, $f_n$ converges uniformly to $|\cdot|$ on $\mathbb R$. Award **0,5 pt** for the pointwise limit and **1 pt** for the exact norm calculation.

**2. Convergence of the derivatives on $\mathbb R$ (1,5 pt).** We have

$$
f_n'(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1/n^2}}.
$$

For fixed $x>0$, this quantity tends to $1$; for $x<0$, it tends to $-1$; at $x=0$, it is $0$. The pointwise limit is therefore

$$
s(x)=
\begin{cases}
-1,&x<0,\\
0,&x=0,\\
1,&x>0.
\end{cases}
$$

Each $f_n'$ is continuous, whereas $s$ is discontinuous at $0$. But a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous: the convergence of $(f_n')$ is therefore not uniform on $\mathbb R$. We can also exhibit the <Terme id="suite-temoin">witness sequence</Terme> $x_n=1/n$:

$$
|f_n'(x_n)-s(x_n)|
=1-\frac1{\sqrt2}
\not\longrightarrow0.
$$

Award **0,75 pt** for calculating the pointwise limit and **0,75 pt** for disproving uniform convergence.

**3. Uniform convergence away from the origin (1 pt).** If $|x|\ge a$, then

$$
|f_n'(x)-s(x)|
=1-\frac{|x|}{\sqrt{x^2+1/n^2}}.
$$

This expression decreases as $|x|$ increases. Its supremum on the domain is therefore attained when $|x|=a$ and equals

$$
\sup_{|x|\ge a}|f_n'(x)-s(x)|
=1-\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2+1/n^2}}
\longrightarrow0.
$$

The convergence is uniform on $(-\infty,-a]\cup[a,+\infty)$. The point is awarded for a uniform bound or for this exact supremum calculation.

**4. Differentiability of the limit (1 pt).** The limit $f(x)=|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$: the left- and right-hand difference quotients are $-1$ and $1$. This example shows that uniform convergence of differentiable functions preserves continuity, but not differentiability; the latter requires additional hypotheses on the sequence of derivatives.

<ExamProblem title="Problem 4 — Power series and parameter-dependent integral" points={5}>

Consider the <Terme id="serie-entiere">power series</Terme>

$$
S(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n(n+1)}.
$$

1. Determine its <Terme id="rayon-de-convergence">radius of convergence</Terme>.
2. Find a closed-form expression for $S(x)$ for $x\in(-1,1)$.
3. Determine the limit of $S(x)$ as $x\to0$.

Also consider the <Terme id="integrale-a-parametre">parameter-dependent integral</Terme>

$$
F(x)=\int_0^1\frac{e^{xt}}{1+t}\,dt.
$$

4. Show that $F$ is of class $C^1$ on $\mathbb R$.
5. Show that $F$ is increasing on $\mathbb R$.
6. Give its first-order expansion at $0$.

<Collapsible title="Solution and indicative barème (marking scheme)">

**1. Radius of convergence (0,75 pt).** With $c_n=1/[n(n+1)]$,

$$
\sqrt[n]{|c_n|}
=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\ln n+\ln(n+1)}{n}\right)
\longrightarrow1.
$$

The Cauchy–Hadamard formula gives $1/R=1$, so $R=1$. Full credit requires the formula used and the conclusion.

**2. Closed-form expression (1,25 pt).** For $|x|<1$,

$$
\frac1{n(n+1)}=\frac1n-\frac1{n+1},
$$

and hence

$$
S(x)
=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n}
-\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n+1}.
$$

The standard logarithmic series gives

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n}=-\ln(1-x),
$$

and, for $x\ne0$,

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n+1}
=\frac1x\sum_{m=2}^{+\infty}\frac{x^m}{m}
=\frac{-\ln(1-x)-x}{x}.
$$

Therefore

$$
S(x)=1+\left(\frac1x-1\right)\ln(1-x)
\qquad(|x|<1,\ x\ne0),
$$

and $S(0)=0$. Award **0,5 pt** for the decomposition, **0,5 pt** for correctly using the logarithmic series on its domain and **0,25 pt** for the final formula including the case $x=0$.

**3. Limit at $0$ (0,5 pt).** The <Terme id="formule-de-taylor-young">Taylor-Young formula</Terme>

$$
\ln(1-x)=-x-\frac{x^2}{2}+o(x^2)
$$

gives

$$
S(x)=\frac{x}{2}+o(x).
$$

In particular, $\lim_{x\to0}S(x)=0$, consistently with $S(0)=0$.

**4. Regularity and differentiation under the integral sign (1 pt).** Set $\varphi(x,t)=e^{xt}/(1+t)$. Here is the hypothesis checklist on an arbitrary compact interval $K=[A,B]\subset\mathbb R$:

1. **Domain.** For every $x\in K$, the function $t\mapsto\varphi(x,t)$ is integrable on the interval $[0,1]$.
2. **Regularity.** The functions $\varphi$ and $\partial_x\varphi(x,t)=t e^{xt}/(1+t)$ are continuous on $K\times[0,1]$.
3. **Domination.** If $M=\max(|A|,|B|)$, then, for $(x,t)\in K\times[0,1]$,

$$
|\varphi(x,t)|\le e^M,
\qquad
|\partial_x\varphi(x,t)|\le e^M,
$$

and the constant $e^M$ is integrable on $[0,1]$ and independent of $x$.
4. **Theorem and conclusion.** The theorem on <Terme id="derivation-sous-le-signe-integral">differentiating under the integral sign</Terme> applies: $F$ is of class $C^1$ on $K$ and

$$
F'(x)=\int_0^1\frac{t e^{xt}}{1+t}\,dt.
$$

Since $K$ is arbitrary, $F\in C^1(\mathbb R)$. The point is awarded for a complete hypothesis checklist; writing only the formula for $F'$ does not justify the interchange.

**5. Monotonicity (0,5 pt).** For every $x\in\mathbb R$, the integrand of $F'(x)$ is positive on $]0,1]$. Thus $F'(x)>0$ and $F$ is in fact strictly increasing on $\mathbb R$.

**6. First-order expansion (1 pt).** We calculate

$$
F(0)=\int_0^1\frac{dt}{1+t}=\ln2
$$

and

$$
F'(0)
=\int_0^1\frac{t}{1+t}\,dt
=\int_0^1\left(1-\frac1{1+t}\right)dt
=1-\ln2.
$$

Since $F$ is differentiable at $0$, the Taylor-Young formula of order $1$ gives

$$
F(x)=\ln2+(1-\ln2)x+o(x)
\qquad(x\to0).
$$

Award **0,25 pt** for $F(0)$, **0,5 pt** for $F'(0)$ and **0,25 pt** for the final expansion.
