<ExamProblem title="Exercise 1 — Four series, four decisions" points={8}>

For each of the following series, determine whether it converges or diverges.
Every answer must include the calculation for the chosen test, the name of a
reference series where relevant, and then an explicit conclusion.

1. $\displaystyle ∑_{n=1}^{+\infty}\ln\!\left(1+\frac1n\right)$.
2. $\displaystyle ∑_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{n!}{n^n}$.
3. $\displaystyle ∑_{n=1}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{2n+1}{n+2}\right)^n$.
4. $\displaystyle ∑_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{\sqrt n}$.

<Collapsible title="Corrigé and indicative barème (mark scheme)">

**1. Reduction to a reference series (2 pts).** Let
$a_n=\ln(1+1/n)$. Since $1/n\to0$ and $\ln(1+t)\sim t$ as $t\to0$,

$$
a_n\sim\frac1n.
$$

The terms are positive. The comparison theorem using an
<Terme id="equivalent">asymptotic equivalent</Terme> therefore transfers the
behaviour of the reference series. Now $\sum 1/n$ is the
<Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme>, which diverges. Thus
$\sum a_n$ diverges.

Barème: justified asymptotic equivalent, 1 pt; harmonic reference and
conclusion, 1 pt.

**2. Ratio analysis (2 pts).** Let $b_n=n!/n^n>0$. We calculate

$$
\frac{b_{n+1}}{b_n}
=\frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1)^{n+1}}\,\frac{n^n}{n!}
=\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n
\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}e^{-1}<1.
$$

The <Terme id="regle-de-d-alembert">ratio test</Terme> therefore establishes
the convergence of $\sum b_n$.

Barème: correctly simplified quotient, 1 pt; limit, named test, and
conclusion, 1 pt.

**3. Root analysis (2 pts).** Let
$c_n=((2n+1)/(n+2))^n>0$. Then

$$
\sqrt[n]{c_n}=\frac{2n+1}{n+2}\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}2>1.
$$

The <Terme id="regle-de-cauchy">root test</Terme> establishes the divergence
of $\sum c_n$. We can also observe that $c_n$ does not tend to $0$, which
already contradicts the necessary condition for a series to converge.

Barème: root and limit, 1 pt; named test and conclusion, 1 pt.

**4. Alternation and absolute convergence (2 pts).** This is an
<Terme id="serie-alternee">alternating series</Terme> whose absolute values
$1/\sqrt n$ decrease to $0$. The
<Terme id="critere-de-leibniz">Leibniz test</Terme> therefore proves its
convergence. However, the series of absolute values is the Riemann series
$\sum 1/n^{1/2}$, which diverges because $1/2\le1$. It does not have
<Terme id="convergence-absolue">absolute convergence</Terme>: the series has
<Terme id="semi-convergence">conditional convergence</Terme>.

Barème: the three Leibniz hypotheses and convergence, 1 pt; analysis of the
absolute value, named Riemann series, and conditional convergence, 1 pt.

**What the examiner rewards.** Each tool must appear with its decisive
calculation. Writing only “ratio test”, “root test”, or “alternating” replaces
neither the verification of the hypotheses nor the conclusion.

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>

<ExamProblem title="Exercise 2 — Bound a remainder to set the precision" points={6}>

Let

$$
S=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2},
\qquad
S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac1{n^2},
\qquad
R_N=S-S_N.
$$

1. Justify that $S$ exists and interpret the
   <Terme id="reste-d-une-serie">remainder</Terme> $R_N$.
2. By comparing the sum with an integral, show that, for every $N\ge1$,

   $$
   0<R_N\le\frac1N.
   $$

3. How many terms is it sufficient to calculate to guarantee
   $|S-S_N|\le5\cdot10^{-4}$?

<Collapsible title="Corrigé and indicative barème">

**1. Existence of the remainder (1 pt).** The series
$\sum 1/n^2$ is a <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme>
with exponent $2>1$; it converges. Its sum $S$ therefore exists, and

$$
R_N=S-S_N=\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2}
$$

is exactly the part of the sum that has not yet been calculated after the
first $N$ terms.

**2. Upper bound (3 pts).** The function $f(x)=1/x^2$ is positive and
decreasing on $[1,+\infty[$. For every integer $n\ge N+1$ and every
$x\in[n-1,n]$, we have $f(x)\ge f(n)$, so

$$
\frac1{n^2}\le\int_{n-1}^{n}\frac{dx}{x^2}.
$$

Summing these inequalities and then taking the limit, the
<Terme id="comparaison-serie-integrale">integral test</Terme> gives

$$
0<R_N
=\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2}
\le\int_N^{+\infty}\frac{dx}{x^2}
=\left[-\frac1x\right]_N^{+\infty}
=\frac1N.
$$

Barème: decrease and inequality on each interval, 1,5 pt; summation and
improper integral, 1 pt; final bound, 0,5 pt.

**3. Number of terms (2 pts).** The preceding upper bound guarantees the
required precision as soon as

$$
\frac1N\le5\cdot10^{-4}=\frac1{2000}.
$$

It is therefore sufficient to take $N=2000$, that is, to calculate the
**first 2000 terms**. This value is guaranteed by the bound obtained; we do
not claim here that no smaller number could suffice with a sharper estimate.

Barème: correct solution of the inequality, 1 pt; interpretation as a number
of terms and conclusion, 1 pt.

**What the examiner rewards.** The bound must be constructed from the
decrease of $1/x^2$, then translated into a numerical guarantee. A decimal
value calculated without estimating the remainder does not answer the
question.

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>

<ExamProblem title="Exercise 3 — True or false: hypotheses and counterexamples" points={6}>

For each statement, state “true” or “false”, then give a complete proof or a
counterexample. A counterexample must satisfy all the hypotheses of the
statement.

1. If the series $\sum u_n$ has <Terme id="convergence-absolue">absolute
   convergence</Terme>, then the series $\sum u_n^2$ converges.
2. Let $(a_n)$ be a sequence such that $a_n>0$ for every $n$. If
   $a_{n+1}/a_n\to1$, then the series $\sum a_n$ diverges.

<Collapsible title="Corrigé and indicative barème">

**1. True (3 pts).** The hypothesis means that $\sum |u_n|$ converges. In
particular, $u_n\to0$; there is therefore an index $N_0$ such that
$|u_n|\le1$ for every $n\ge N_0$. From this index onwards,

$$
0\le u_n^2=|u_n|^2\le|u_n|.
$$

The <Terme id="critere-de-comparaison">comparison test</Terme> with the
convergent series $\sum |u_n|$ proves that
$\sum_{n=N_0}^{+\infty}u_n^2$ converges. Adding the first finitely many terms
does not change the behaviour of a series. Thus $\sum u_n^2$ converges.

Barème: verdict, 0,5 pt; deduction of $u_n\to0$ from absolute convergence and
choice of the index, 1 pt; comparison and conclusion, 1,5 pt.

**2. False (3 pts).** Take

$$
a_n=\frac1{n^2}>0.
$$

All the hypotheses are satisfied, because

$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}
=\frac{n^2}{(n+1)^2}
=\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^2
\xrightarrow[n\to+\infty]{}1.
$$

Yet $\sum a_n=\sum 1/n^2$ converges: it is the Riemann series with exponent
$2>1$. The limit $1$ is precisely the
<Terme id="cas-douteux">inconclusive case</Terme> of the
<Terme id="regle-de-d-alembert">ratio test</Terme>; it implies neither
convergence nor divergence. The statement is therefore false.

Barème: verdict and choice of counterexample, 1 pt; verification of positivity
and of the ratio, 1 pt; named reference series and refutation, 1 pt.

**What the examiner rewards.** For the true statement, the comparison becomes
valid only after obtaining $|u_n|\le1$. For the false statement, merely
displaying a convergent series is not enough: you must also calculate its
ratio and verify that it does indeed tend to $1$.

</Collapsible>
