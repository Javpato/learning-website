<GuidingQuestion>

The integral $\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt$ has no antiderivative expressible with the usual functions. And yet its exact value is known. **How can making a problem more general — by adding a parameter that was not there — make it easier?**

</GuidingQuestion>

## 1. Adding a parameter that did not exist

Problem: faced with an integral without an antiderivative, every direct computation tool fails. We must change object — not simplify the integral, but **embed it in a family**.

**Definition 1 (Feynman's method).** <Def id="methode-de-feynman">**Feynman's method**</Def> consists in replacing the wanted integral $I$ by a <Terme id="integrale-a-parametre">parameter-dependent integral</Terme> $F(x)$ such that:

- $F(x_0)=I$ for some value $x_0$ of the parameter;
- $\partial_xf$ **simplifies** the integrand, so that $F'$ can be computed;
- another value of the parameter, or a limit, gives $F$ explicitly.

The idea is counter-intuitive: we solve a more general problem because it possesses a structure — a derivative — that the particular case does not have.

**Method 1 (the five moves).**

1. **Choose** $F(x)$ and identify the value $x_0$ that returns $I$.
2. **Justify** differentiation under the integral sign: this is Theorem 2 of lesson 10, with its domination of $\partial_xf$.
3. **Compute** $F'(x)$; this is where the parameter must have been well chosen.
4. **Integrate** in $x$ to recover $F$, up to a constant.
5. **Fix the constant** by a convenient value or limit — often $x\to+\infty$, where the integrand dies out.

**Example 1 (Dirichlet's integral).** Set, for $x\ge0$,

$$
F(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\,\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt .
$$

The factor $e^{-xt}$ is exactly the added ingredient: it equals $1$ at $x=0$, so $F(0)=I$, and it makes the integral absolutely convergent as soon as $x>0$.

On $[a,+\infty)$ with $a>0$,

$$
\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(e^{-xt}\frac{\sin t}{t}\right)\right|=\bigl|e^{-xt}\sin t\bigr|\le e^{-at},
$$

an integrable bound independent of $x$: Theorem 2 of lesson 10 applies and

$$
F'(x)=-\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\sin t\,dt=-\frac1{1+x^{2}},
$$

the last integral being computed by two integrations by parts. Hence $F(x)=-\arctan x+C$ on $(0,+\infty)$.

To fix $C$, let $x\to+\infty$: $|F(x)|\le\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}dt=1/x\to0$, so $F(x)\to0$ and $C=\pi/2$. Thus

$$
F(x)=\frac\pi2-\arctan x\qquad\text{for }x>0 .
$$

**Remark (the delicate point, and it is a real one).** Concluding $I=F(0)=\pi/2$ requires the **continuity of $F$ at $0$**, and Theorem 1 of lesson 10 does not provide it: at $x=0$ the integral is only conditionally convergent, and no integrable domination exists near $0$ — precisely the inconclusive case of that lesson. The correct justification goes through an Abel-type uniform convergence on $[0,+\infty)$, resting on the <Terme id="transformation-d-abel">Abel transformation</Terme> of lesson 04. We name that argument without developing it here; what matters is that something **is missing**, and where.

The result is

$$
\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt=\frac\pi2 .
$$

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If $\partial_xf$ does not simplify the integrand, the method gains nothing: we have replaced one impossible integral by a family of impossible integrals. Choosing the parameter is the whole of the work.

### Mini-exercises

1. In Example 1, why introduce $e^{-xt}$ rather than, say, $\sin(xt)/t$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because differentiating $e^{-xt}$ in $x$ brings down a factor $-t$ which **cancels the troublesome $1/t$**. The parameter is chosen so that its derivative erases the obstacle, not so that it resembles the integrand.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why is the constant fixed at $x\to+\infty$ rather than at $x=0$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because at $0$ the value of $F$ is precisely the unknown we are after. At $+\infty$, by contrast, the exponential crushes everything and the limit is bounded effortlessly.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Where to place the parameter

There is no universal recipe, but three insertions cover almost every exercise.

**Method 2 (three ways to introduce a parameter).**

1. **Replace a constant by $x$** — an exponent, a coefficient, a bound.
2. **Multiply by a damping factor** $e^{-xt}$ — useful when the integral is only conditionally convergent.
3. **Raise to a variable power** — replace $t$ by $t^x$ when a logarithm appears.

**Example 2 (a logarithm in the denominator).** Let us compute $\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{t^{a}-1}{\ln t}\,dt$ for $a>-1$.

Let $F(a)$ be that integral. The integrand extends by continuity at $t=1$, where it equals $a$, and tends to $0$ at $t=0$: the integral exists. On a segment $[\alpha,\beta]\subset(-1,+\infty)$,

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\left(\frac{t^{a}-1}{\ln t}\right)=\frac{t^{a}\ln t}{\ln t}=t^{a},
$$

bounded by $t^{\alpha}$, integrable on $(0,1)$ since $\alpha>-1$, and independent of $a$. Hence

$$
F'(a)=\int_0^1t^{a}\,dt=\frac1{a+1}.
$$

Since $F(0)=0$, we obtain $F(a)=\ln(a+1)$. The logarithm in the denominator, which made a direct computation impossible, disappeared at the first differentiation.

**Example 3 (Frullani).** The same mechanism gives, for $0<a<b$,

$$
\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-at}-e^{-bt}}{t}\,dt=\ln\frac ba .
$$

This is the exercise of lesson 10: we differentiate with respect to one of the two parameters, the $1/t$ disappears, and we integrate.

### Mini-exercises

1. What parameter would you introduce for $\displaystyle\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t^2}-e^{-4t^2}}{t^2}\,dt$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Set $F(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}\dfrac{e^{-t^2}-e^{-xt^2}}{t^2}\,dt$. Then $F'(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt^2}dt=\dfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2\sqrt x}$, and $F(1)=0$ fixes the constant: $F(x)=\sqrt\pi(\sqrt x-1)$. The answer sought is $F(4)=\sqrt\pi$.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why must the domination be established on a segment of parameters, and not at a single point?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because Theorem 2 of lesson 10 requires a bound valid for all $x$ in a neighbourhood: it is the mean value theorem, which evaluates $\partial_xf$ at an unknown point, that imposes this.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. When no exact value exists: describing the behaviour

Problem: most integrals have neither an antiderivative nor a parameter trick. What remains is to give their **asymptotic** behaviour, with a controlled error — otherwise it is only an impression.

**Definition 2 (asymptotic expansion).** An <Def id="developpement-asymptotique">**asymptotic expansion**</Def> of a function $I$ near a point (often $+\infty$) is an expression

$$
I(x)=c_1\varphi_1(x)+c_2\varphi_2(x)+\cdots+c_k\varphi_k(x)+o\bigl(\varphi_k(x)\bigr),
$$

where the $\varphi_j$ form a comparison scale, each <Terme id="negligeable">negligible</Terme> against the previous one.

**Method 3 (the four moves of asymptotics).**

1. **Locate the dominant region** — where the integrand really carries weight.
2. **Change variables** to reduce that region to a standard form.
3. **Integrate by parts** to extract the leading term.
4. **Bound the remainder** — the move that distinguishes a proof from a conjecture.

**Proposition 1 (the exponential integral).** As $x\to+\infty$,

$$
\int_x^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t}\,dt=\frac{e^{-x}}{x}\left(1+O\!\left(\frac1x\right)\right).
$$

<Proof title="one integration by parts, then an explicit bound on the remainder">

**Idea.** Integration by parts extracts exactly one term and leaves an integral of the same nature but of smaller order. All the work consists in **bounding** what remains, not in ignoring it.

Integrate by parts with $u=1/t$ and $v'=e^{-t}$, hence $v=-e^{-t}$:

$$
\int_x^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t}\,dt
=\left[-\frac{e^{-t}}{t}\right]_x^{+\infty}-\int_x^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t^{2}}\,dt
=\frac{e^{-x}}{x}-\int_x^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t^{2}}\,dt .
$$

It remains to bound the remainder. On $[x,+\infty)$ we have $t\ge x$, so $1/t^2\le1/x^2$ and

$$
0\le\int_x^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t^{2}}\,dt\le\frac1{x^{2}}\int_x^{+\infty}e^{-t}\,dt=\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{2}} .
$$

The remainder is therefore bounded by $e^{-x}/x^2$, that is $(e^{-x}/x)\cdot(1/x)$: it is indeed $O(1/x)$ times the leading term.

**What the proof teaches.** Integration by parts may be repeated to gain further terms, but each step must come with its bound. Without the last line we would have written a formula without knowing whether it describes anything at all. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If the integration by parts is repeated, we obtain $\frac{e^{-x}}{x}\left(1-\frac1x+\frac{2}{x^2}-\cdots\right)$, whose coefficients are $(-1)^kk!$. This series **diverges for every $x$**: it is an asymptotic series, not a convergent one. Adding terms improves the approximation up to a certain index, then degrades it. An asymptotic expansion is therefore **never** an infinite sum — it is a finite sum plus a bounded remainder, and the remainder is the only thing that gives it meaning.

### Mini-exercises

1. Obtain the next term of the expansion of Proposition 1.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   A second integration by parts on $\int_x^{+\infty}e^{-t}/t^2\,dt$ gives $e^{-x}/x^2-2\int_x^{+\infty}e^{-t}/t^3dt$, and the new remainder is bounded by $2e^{-x}/x^3$. Hence

   $$
   \int_x^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t}\,dt=\frac{e^{-x}}{x}-\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{2}}+O\!\left(\frac{e^{-x}}{x^{3}}\right).
   $$

   </Collapsible>

2. Why is the phrase "bounded remainder" more important than the word "expansion"?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because a sum of terms without a bound on the remainder says nothing: the series may diverge, as it does here. It is the bound that turns the expression into a mathematical statement and gives the order of magnitude of the error committed.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. Asymptotics of a sum: Euler's constant

The same spirit applies to sums. We have known since lesson 01 that the <Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme> diverges; the precise question is **how fast**.

**Theorem 1 (expansion of the harmonic sum).** There exists a constant $\gamma$, called Euler's constant, such that

$$
H_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac1n=\ln N+\gamma+o(1)\qquad(N\to+\infty).
$$

<Proof title="turn the divergence into a convergent series of gaps">

**Idea.** We cannot study a diverging quantity directly. So we subtract what diverges — the logarithm — and show that **the gap** converges.

Set

$$
u_n=\frac1n-\ln\frac{n+1}{n}=\frac1n-\ln\!\left(1+\frac1n\right).
$$

The Taylor expansion $\ln(1+h)=h-h^2/2+o(h^2)$ with $h=1/n$ gives

$$
u_n=\frac1{2n^{2}}+o\!\left(\frac1{n^{2}}\right)\sim\frac1{2n^{2}},
$$

and $u_n\ge0$ since $\ln(1+h)\le h$ (an inequality from the toolbox). This is a <Terme id="serie-a-termes-positifs">series with positive terms</Terme> equivalent to a <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> of exponent $2$: it **converges**; write $\gamma$ for its sum.

Now the partial sum telescopes:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_n=H_N-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigl(\ln(n+1)-\ln n\bigr)=H_N-\ln(N+1).
$$

Letting $N$ tend to infinity gives $H_N-\ln(N+1)\to\gamma$. Finally $\ln(N+1)-\ln N=\ln(1+1/N)\to0$, so $H_N-\ln N\to\gamma$, which is the statement.

**What the proof teaches.** The reusable move is this: *to describe a divergent quantity, subtract the known divergent behaviour and show that the rest converges*. It combines three tools already in hand — Taylor expansion, Riemann comparison, telescoping. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Stirling's formula.** The same mechanism applied to $\ln n!=\sum_{k\le n}\ln k$, compared with $\int\ln t\,dt$, produces <Terme id="formule-de-stirling">Stirling's formula</Terme>

$$
n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac ne\right)^{n},
$$

the constant $\sqrt{2\pi}$ requiring an extra argument (the Wallis integrals). It is used as soon as a factorial must be **compared** with a power — typically for a <Terme id="rayon-de-convergence">radius of convergence</Terme>.

### Mini-exercises

1. Deduce from Theorem 1 an equivalent of $\sum_{n=N+1}^{2N}1/n$.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   It is $H_{2N}-H_N=(\ln 2N+\gamma+o(1))-(\ln N+\gamma+o(1))=\ln2+o(1)$. The sum therefore tends to $\ln 2$, which incidentally recovers the sum of the alternating harmonic series.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why subtract $\ln N$ rather than any other divergent function?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because the series-integral comparison of lesson 02 indicates that $H_N$ diverges exactly like $\int_1^N dt/t=\ln N$. Subtracting a faster or slower divergence would leave a still-divergent gap.

   </Collapsible>

## Worked example — a parameter placed in the exponent

Let us compute $\displaystyle I=\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{\arctan(2t)-\arctan t}{t}\,dt$.

**Step 1 — place the parameter where it will produce a simplification.** Set

$$
F(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{\arctan(xt)-\arctan t}{t}\,dt,
$$

so that $I=F(2)$ and $F(1)=0$.

*Why?* The parameter replaces the constant $2$, and $F(1)=0$ will supply the constant of integration for free: we always choose a point where the value is obvious.

**Step 2 — justify the differentiation.** We have $\partial_x\bigl(\arctan(xt)/t\bigr)=1/(1+x^{2}t^{2})$. On a segment $[a,b]\subset(0,+\infty)$ this term is bounded by $1/(1+a^{2}t^{2})$, integrable on $(0,+\infty)$ and independent of $x$.

*Why?* This is hypothesis 3 of Theorem 2 of lesson 10; without it the derivative-integral interchange is not legitimate and everything that follows would be worthless.

**Step 3 — compute the derivative.** The term $\arctan t$ does not depend on $x$, so

$$
F'(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{dt}{1+x^{2}t^{2}}
=\frac1x\Bigl[\arctan(xt)\Bigr]_0^{+\infty}=\frac{\pi}{2x}.
$$

*Why?* This is where the choice of Step 1 pays off: differentiation removed the $1/t$ and the arctangent, leaving an elementary integral.

**Step 4 — go back up and conclude.** Integrating: $F(x)=\dfrac\pi2\ln x+C$, and $F(1)=0$ gives $C=0$. Hence

$$
I=F(2)=\frac\pi2\ln 2 .
$$

*Why?* The particular value chosen in Step 1 fixes the constant with no extra computation — which is why we identify it before starting.

<Collapsible title="Your turn — answer">

Compute $\displaystyle J=\int_0^{1}\frac{\ln(1+t)}{1+t^{2}}\,dt$ by setting $F(x)=\int_0^1\dfrac{\ln(1+xt)}{1+t^{2}}\,dt$.

We have $J=F(1)$ and $F(0)=0$. On $[0,1]$, $\partial_x=\dfrac{t}{(1+xt)(1+t^{2})}$ is continuous on the compact set $[0,1]\times[0,1]$, hence dominated by a constant: the differentiation is legitimate (the easy case of lesson 10, to be written down anyway).

Decomposing into partial fractions in $t$ and then integrating, we obtain

$$
F'(x)=\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\left(\frac{\ln 2}{2}+\frac{\pi x}{4}-\ln(1+x)\right).
$$

Integrating from $0$ to $1$ and noticing that the term $\int_0^1\frac{\ln(1+x)}{1+x^2}dx$ that appears is $J$ itself, we obtain an equation in $J$ whose solution is

$$
J=\frac{\pi\ln 2}{8}.
$$

The point of method: when the derivative makes the wanted integral reappear, we do not compute — we **solve**. That is exactly what happened at Step 3 of the worked example of lesson 10.

</Collapsible>

## Guided visualisation

No widget here: a table of values shows the most disconcerting property of an asymptotic expansion. We approximate $\int_x^{+\infty}e^{-t}/t\,dt$ by the sum of the first $k$ terms of Proposition 1, at $x=5$.

| Number of terms $k$ | approximate value (units of $e^{-5}$) | relative error |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | $0{,}2000$ | $12\ \%$ |
| $2$ | $0{,}1600$ | $10\ \%$ |
| $3$ | $0{,}1760$ | $1{,}3\ \%$ |
| $5$ | $0{,}1786$ | $0{,}2\ \%$ |
| $8$ | $0{,}1774$ | $0{,}5\ \%$ |
| $12$ | $0{,}1667$ | $6\ \%$ |
| $20$ | $-1{,}05$ | absurd |

**Cycle 1 — there is a best index.**

- **Predict.** Adding terms to an expansion: should the approximation always improve?
- **Act.** Run down the error column and find where it is smallest.
- **Observe.** The error decreases up to an optimal index — here around $k=5$, that is $k\approx x$ — then it **rises again**, and eventually diverges.
- **Connect.** This is the remark of section 3: the coefficients $k!$ eventually beat $1/x^k$. An asymptotic series is not a convergent series; the expansion makes sense only when truncated.

**Cycle 2 — the error is the one that was announced.**

- **Predict.** At $k=1$, Proposition 1 bounds the relative error by $1/x$. What is that bound at $x=5$?
- **Act.** Compare $1/5=20\ \%$ with the relative error actually observed on the first row.
- **Observe.** The real error, $12\ \%$, is well below the bound of $20\ \%$: the estimate is correct and not too pessimistic.
- **Connect.** This is the fourth move of Method 3. Without that bound, the first row of the table would be a mere number, with nothing guaranteeing that it approximates anything.

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Differentiating under the integral without a domination.** Feynman's method rests entirely on Theorem 2 of lesson 10. An unjustified computation of $F'$ may give a false result, and always gives a worthless script.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Fixing the constant at the point you are looking for.** If you use $F(x_0)$ to determine $C$, you are assuming what you want to prove. The constant is fixed elsewhere — at a trivial value or at a limit.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Treating an asymptotic expansion as a series.** The coefficients may grow like $k!$; adding terms eventually ruins everything. Truncate, and bound the remainder.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Writing an equivalent without an error bound.** "$I(x)\sim e^{-x}/x$" indicates no precision at a given $x$. It is the bound on the remainder that makes the statement numerically usable.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting that continuity at the boundary of the parameter is a hypothesis.** Example 1 obtains $F$ on $(0,+\infty)$; passing to $x=0$ requires an argument that domination does not supply.

</Pitfall>

<GuidingAnswer>

Generalising makes the problem easier because the particular case is **rigid**: it offers no handle. An isolated integral is a number, and a number cannot be differentiated. Embedded in a family $F(x)$, it becomes a function — and a function has a derivative, a differential equation, limiting values, all handles the particular case did not have.

The parameter is therefore not chosen to resemble anything: it is chosen so that **its derivative erases the obstacle**. In $\int_0^{+\infty}\sin t/t\,dt$ the obstacle is the $1/t$, and differentiating $e^{-xt}$ brings down exactly the factor $t$ that cancels it. The rest is mechanical: justify the differentiation by a domination, compute, integrate, fix the constant at a point where the answer is obvious.

When no trick works, we give up the exact value for the order of magnitude — but then the work shifts to the **bound on the remainder**, without which an asymptotic expansion states nothing. The table in the visualisation shows this bluntly: the later terms eventually destroy everything, and only the bound says how far one may go.

</GuidingAnswer>

## Summary — create a handle, or bound the error

<KeyResults title="The decision sheet">

| Situation | What we do | What must be justified | Inconclusive case |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| integral without an antiderivative | introduce $F(x)$ with $F(x_0)=I$ | domination of $\partial_xf$ (lesson 10) | if $\partial_xf$ does not simplify, nothing is gained |
| constant to determine | evaluate at a trivial point, or let $x$ tend | existence of the limit | never at the point sought |
| passing to the boundary of the parameter | continuity of $F$ there | not given by domination if the integral is only conditionally convergent there | an Abel-type argument is needed |
| no exact value | Method 3: locate, change variables, integrate by parts, **bound** | the bound on the remainder | without a bound, no statement at all |
| divergent sum | subtract the known divergence | convergence of the series of gaps | subtracting the wrong scale leaves a divergence |

**Proofs worth being able to reconstruct.** The integration by parts producing the leading term **and** its bound $\int_x^{+\infty}e^{-t}/t^2\le e^{-x}/x^2$; the decomposition $u_n=1/n-\ln(1+1/n)\sim1/(2n^2)$ which telescopes into $H_N-\ln(N+1)$ and manufactures $\gamma$.

**The idea to take away.** A well-placed parameter turns a number into a function, hence an obstacle into a differential equation. And when there is no trick, the thing of value is not the formula: it is the error bound that comes with it.

</KeyResults>
