# Designing the right parameter and guaranteeing the approximation

<LessonMeta id="math-an-c11" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

A difficult integral sometimes becomes elementary if you add a parameter chosen so that its derivative precisely removes the obstacle.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="A diffraction pattern observed with adjustable damping">

**Situation:** the amplitude at the centre of a diffraction pattern is described by $I=\int_0^{+\infty}\sin(t)/t\,dt$. In the laboratory, distant oscillations are attenuated by $e^{-xt}$ and the apparatus reads $F(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\sin(t)/t\,dt$ for $x>0$.
**Precise question:** what number does the reading tend to when the damping disappears? And, for $x=4$, can $F(4)$ be replaced by $1/4$ with a guaranteed error less than $0{,}006$?
**The obstacle:** the antiderivative of $\sin(t)/t$ is not elementary, and directly replacing $x$ by $0$ under an infinite sum is not a rule.
**The contract:** this chapter provides 3 tools (designing a parameter that simplifies, recovering the lost constant, bounding an approximation). By the end, we will calculate the reading without damping and certify the requested approximation.

</Mission>

*Feeling confident? [Go straight to the TD (tutorial sheet)](../td-6/) — the theory will still be here when you get stuck.*

## The battle plan

We are going to learn how to perform three actions, in this order.

1. **Design instead of guessing**: place a parameter where its derivative removes the troublesome factor.
2. **Recover the lost information**: integrate the derivative, then determine the constant using a convenient value or limit.
3. **Turn intuition into an estimate**: integrate by parts and bound the remainder, rather than merely naming it.

## 1. Design a parameter that truly simplifies

**Tool 1 — make the obstacle disappear through differentiation.** The parameter is not decoration: its position is determined backwards, starting from the factor you want to cancel.

Problem: introducing an arbitrary letter into the integrand creates a family, but nothing guarantees that this family is easier than the original integral.
We therefore need a design method that tests the parameter's usefulness before any calculation.

**Definition 1 (Feynman's trick).** <Def id="methode-de-feynman">**Feynman's trick**</Def> (adding a parameter so that differentiation turns a difficult integral into a simple one) consists in constructing a <Terme id="integrale-a-parametre">parameter-dependent integral</Terme> $F(x)$, calculating $F'(x)$ under verified hypotheses, and then reconstructing $F$ and the desired integral.

**Method 1 (design the parameter backwards).** Before differentiating, put the candidate through four questions.

1. **Target:** does a value or limit of $F(x)$ give exactly the desired integral?
2. **Cancellation:** what factor will $\partial_x$ produce, and does that factor remove the obstacle?
3. **Calculation:** does the integral of $\partial_x f(x,t)$ belong to a family that is already known?
4. **Anchor:** do we know $F(x_0)$, or a limit of $F(x)$, with which to determine the constant after integration?

**Example (physics) 1 — three candidate damping factors.** For the diffraction reading, the troublesome factor is $1/t$. Let us compare three possible placements of the same parameter.

| Candidate family | Effect of differentiation with respect to $x$ | Design verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $A(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-x}\dfrac{\sin t}{t}\,dt$ | $A'(x)=-A(x)$: the quotient $1/t$ remains | circular: the unknown integral has not disappeared |
| $B(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-x t^2}\dfrac{\sin t}{t}\,dt$ | $B'(x)=-\int_0^{+\infty}t e^{-x t^2}\sin t\,dt$ | $1/t$ disappears, but a new difficult kernel appears |
| $F(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\dfrac{\sin t}{t}\,dt$ | $F'(x)=-\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\sin t\,dt$ | useful: the factor $t$ created by the derivative cancels $1/t$ exactly |

The third candidate wins because it satisfies the cancellation and calculation questions **simultaneously**. It leads to an exponential–sine integral already mastered in the previous lesson.

**Remark (laboratory notation).** In optics, the damping coefficient would often be denoted by $\alpha$ or $\varepsilon$ rather than $x$. Here we keep $x$ throughout: the same physical parameter, dressed in different notation.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** A candidate that does not simplify does not prove that the integral cannot be calculated. It merely says: **redesign the parameter**. This is the mechanism in exercise math-an-td6-04, where several families recover the right target but only one produces a usable derivative.

For the right candidate, we still need permission to differentiate.

**Proposition 1 (differential equation for the damped reading).** For $x>0$, the function

$$F(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt$$

is differentiable and satisfies

$$F'(x)=-\frac{1}{1+x^2}.$$

**Hypothesis passport — differentiating $F$.** Fix $x_0>0$ and choose $a,b$ such that $0<a<x_0<b$.

- **Prior existence:** for $x\in[a,b]$, $|e^{-xt}\sin(t)/t|\le e^{-at}$, because $|\sin t|\le t$ for $t\ge0$; this envelope is integrable.
- **Differentiability with respect to $x$:** for every $t>0$, $\partial_x(e^{-xt}\sin(t)/t)=-e^{-xt}\sin t$.
- **Independent domination:** $|e^{-xt}\sin t|\le e^{-at}$ for every $x\in[a,b]$; this is a <Terme id="hypothese-de-domination">domination</Terme> independent of $x$.
- **Integrability:** $\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-at}\,dt=1/a<+\infty$.

**Theorem invoked:** <Terme id="derivation-sous-le-signe-integral">differentiating under the integral sign</Terme> from the previous lesson.

**Conclusion:**

$$F'(x)=-\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\sin t\,dt.$$

To calculate this last integral, set

$$S(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\sin t\,dt, \qquad C(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\cos t\,dt.$$

Two integrations by parts give $S(x)=1-xC(x)$ and $C(x)=xS(x)$. Thus $(1+x^2)S(x)=1$, so $S(x)=1/(1+x^2)$ and the stated formula follows.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** The passport above works around every $x_0>0$, but not on an interval containing $0$: the dominating function $e^{-at}$ requires $a>0$. This does not rule out the limit at $0$; it calls for a different proof, which we will construct in Section 2.

### Mini-exercises — tool 1

1. **The factor that must appear.** Which derivative makes the choice of $e^{-xt}$ in front of $\sin(t)/t$ useful?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   We have $\partial_x e^{-xt}=-t e^{-xt}$. The factor $-t$ cancels the denominator $t$:

   $$
   \partial_x\left(e^{-xt}\frac{\sin t}{t}\right)=-e^{-xt}\sin t.
   $$

   This is the cancellation question from Method 1.

   </Collapsible>

2. **Test a candidate before calculating.** For $J=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}(1-\cos t)/t\,dt$, why is placing a parameter in $\cos(at)$ promising?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   By setting $H(a)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}(1-\cos(at))/t\,dt$, we recover $J$ at $a=1$ and

   $$
   \partial_a\left(e^{-t}\frac{1-\cos(at)}{t}\right)=e^{-t}\sin(at).
   $$

   The factor $t$ produced by differentiating the cosine again cancels the denominator. The new integral is elementary: the candidate passes the target, cancellation and calculation questions.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Determine the constant, then remove the damping

**Tool 2 — anchor the antiderivative.** Knowing $F'$ determines the shape of $F$, but not its vertical position.

Problem: all functions $x\mapsto C-\arctan x$ have the derivative in Proposition 1; the calculation under the integral sign has therefore lost a constant.
We need to choose a value or limit at which the original integral becomes immediately readable.

**Proposition 2 (the two possible anchors).** If $F'(x)=h(x)$ and if $H'(x)=h(x)$, then $F(x)=H(x)+C$. The constant $C$ is determined as soon as we know:

- either a convenient value $F(x_0)$;
- or a convenient limit of $F(x)$ at one end of the domain.

**Example (physics) 2 — choose the limit that crushes the signal.** Proposition 1 gives

$$F(x)=C-\arctan x.$$

When $x$ becomes large, $|\sin t|/t\le1$ yields

$$|F(x)|\le\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\,dt=\frac1x\longrightarrow0.$$

The convenient limit is therefore $F(x)\to0$ as $x\to+\infty$. Since $\arctan x\to\pi/2$, we obtain $C=\pi/2$, then

$$F(x)=\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan x=\arctan\!\left(\frac1x\right), \qquad x>0.$$

**Remark.** Trying to determine the constant using “$F(0)=I$” would be circular: $I$ is precisely the unknown number. By contrast, the limit $x\to+\infty$ crushes the entire signal and equals $0$ without knowing $I$. A good anchor removes information; it does not ask for it.

We still need to prove that letting $x$ slide towards $0$ does indeed recover the integral without damping.

**Proposition 3 (remove the damping).** The integral $I=\int_0^{+\infty}\sin(t)/t\,dt$ exists and

$$I=\lim_{x\to0^+}F(x).$$

The difficulty is the oscillating tail. For $0\le x\le1$, set $h_x(t)=e^{-xt}/t$, which is positive and decreasing. Integration by parts on $[A,B]$ gives

$$\int_A^B h_x(t)\sin t\,dt =\left[-h_x(t)\cos t\right]_A^B +\int_A^B h_x'(t)\cos t\,dt.$$

Since $h_x'\le0$,

$$\left|\int_A^B e^{-xt}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt\right| \le h_x(A)+h_x(B)+\int_A^B|h_x'(t)|\,dt =2h_x(A) \le\frac{2}{A}.$$

The bound depends on neither $B$ nor $x$: the damped and undamped tails become small together.

**Split passport — letting $x$ tend to $0$.** First fix $A>0$.

- **Convergence on the interval:** for every $t\in[0,A]$, $e^{-xt}\sin(t)/t\to\sin(t)/t$ as $x\to0^+$, with the extended value $1$ at $t=0$.
- **Domination on the interval:** $|e^{-xt}\sin(t)/t|\le1$, and the constant $1$ is integrable on $[0,A]$.
- **Uniform damped tail:** for $0<x\le1$, $|\int_A^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\sin(t)/t\,dt|\le2/A$.
- **Target tail:** taking $x=0$ in the same calculation gives $|\int_A^{+\infty}\sin(t)/t\,dt|\le2/A$.

**Theorem invoked:** dominated convergence on the finite interval, followed by uniform control of both tails.

**Conclusion:** after this splitting, we may <Terme id="interversion-limite-integrale">move the limit inside the integral</Terme>, and $I=\lim_{x\to0^+}F(x)$.

<Collapsible title="Proof — the complete epsilon splitting">

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $A$ such that $4/A<\varepsilon/2$. On $[0,A]$, dominated convergence provides $x_\varepsilon>0$ such that, for $0<x<x_\varepsilon$, $|\int_0^A(e^{-xt}-1)\sin(t)/t\,dt|<\varepsilon/2$. The two tails contribute at most $4/A<\varepsilon/2$; hence $|F(x)-I|<\varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** The naive bound $|e^{-xt}\sin(t)/t|\le1/t$ provides no passport on $[1,+\infty)$, because $1/t$ is not integrable there. The failure of this domination does not disprove moving the limit inside: it signals that the finite interval and oscillating tail must be separated.

## 3. Approximate while keeping track of the error's size

**Tool 3 — turn a leading term into a guarantee.** A short formula is useful only if we know how much the omitted terms may contribute.

Problem: writing $F(x)\approx1/x$ for strong damping says neither why this approximation is true nor from what point it is accurate.
We therefore need a representation that ranks the corrections and isolates a controlled remainder.

**Definition 2 (asymptotic expansion).** An <Def id="developpement-asymptotique">**asymptotic expansion**</Def> (a leading term, followed by increasingly small corrections, with a controlled remainder) of $Q(x)$ is written

$$Q(x)=a_0\varphi_0(x)+a_1\varphi_1(x)+\cdots+a_p\varphi_p(x)+R_p(x),$$

where each $\varphi_{k+1}$ is <Terme id="negligeable">negligible</Terme> compared with $\varphi_k$ in the limit under study, and where a bound on $R_p$ indicates what is lost by truncating the expansion.

**Proposition 4 (two integrations by parts with a remainder).** As $x\to+\infty$,

$$F(x)=\frac1x+R(x), \qquad |R(x)|\le\frac{1}{3x^3}.$$

Set $\varphi(t)=\sin(t)/t$, extended by $\varphi(0)=1$. Two integrations by parts, with $dv=e^{-xt}\,dt$, give

$$F(x) =\frac{\varphi(0)}{x}+\frac1x\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\varphi'(t)\,dt =\frac{\varphi(0)}{x}+\frac{\varphi'(0)}{x^2} +\frac1{x^2}\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\varphi''(t)\,dt.$$

Now

$$\varphi(t)=\int_0^1\cos(st)\,ds, \qquad \varphi(0)=1, \qquad \varphi'(0)=0,$$

and

$$|\varphi''(t)| =\left|\int_0^1-s^2\cos(st)\,ds\right| \le\int_0^1s^2\,ds =\frac13.$$

The remainder is therefore explicitly

$$R(x)=\frac1{x^2}\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\varphi''(t)\,dt,$$

and its absolute value satisfies

$$|R(x)| \le\frac1{3x^2}\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\,dt =\frac{1}{3x^3}.$$

In particular, $|R(x)|/(1/x)\le1/(3x^2)\to0$: we obtain the <Terme id="equivalent">asymptotic equivalent</Terme> $F(x)\sim1/x$.

<Collapsible title="Proof — the boundary terms in the two integrations by parts">

The representation $\varphi(t)=\int_0^1\cos(st)\,ds$ shows that $\varphi$ and $\varphi'$ are bounded. The two boundary terms are $[-e^{-xt}\varphi(t)/x]_0^{+\infty}=\varphi(0)/x$ and $[-e^{-xt}\varphi'(t)/x]_0^{+\infty}=\varphi'(0)/x=0$. The contributions at $+\infty$ vanish thanks to the exponential. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Example (physics) 3 — certify a strongly damped reading.** For $x=4$, Proposition 4 gives

$$\left|F(4)-\frac14\right| \le\frac{1}{3\cdot4^3} =\frac1{192} \approx0{,}00521.$$

Thus $1/4$ is indeed an approximation with error less than $0{,}006$. The exact value $F(4)=\arctan(1/4)\approx0{,}24498$ confirms the difference $0{,}00502$, but the **guarantee** comes from the bound on the remainder, not from the calculator.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** An integration by parts that produces “leading term + remaining integral” does not yet prove an asymptotic equivalent. Until the remaining integral is bounded and compared with the leading term, it could be of the same order. This is the central point of exercise math-an-td6-05.

## Worked example — design, differentiate, then anchor

**Example 4 — complete calculation of a logarithmic integral.** Let us return to

$$H(a)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}\frac{1-\cos(at)}{t}\,dt, \qquad a\ge0.$$

**Step 1 — place the parameter in the phase.** Replace $\cos t$ with $\cos(at)$: the target is recovered at $a=1$ and the integrand vanishes at $a=0$.

*Why?* Method 1 calls for both a factor $t$ that will cancel the denominator and an anchor that will determine the constant without knowing the answer.

**Step 2 — complete the differentiation passport.** On every bounded interval of values of $a$, the integral exists; the integrand is differentiable with respect to $a$; its derivative $e^{-t}\sin(at)$ is bounded by $e^{-t}$; finally, $\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}\,dt=1$.

*Why?* The formal derivative calculation is not enough: the theorem from the previous lesson requires these four checks before the interchange.

**Step 3 — calculate the simplified derivative.** The passport allows

$$H'(a)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}\sin(at)\,dt=\frac{a}{1+a^2}.$$

*Why?* The choice of parameter removed $1/t$; the remaining integral is the sine transform calculated in the previous lesson.

**Step 4 — integrate and determine the constant.** An antiderivative of $a/(1+a^2)$ is $\tfrac12\ln(1+a^2)$. Since $H(0)=0$, Proposition 2 gives

$$H(a)=\frac12\ln(1+a^2).$$

*Why?* The derivative determines $H$ only up to an additive constant; the convenient value $a=0$ restores exactly the information that was lost.

**Step 5 — return to the target.** At $a=1$,

$$\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}\frac{1-\cos t}{t}\,dt=H(1)=\frac12\ln2.$$

*Why?* The target question in Method 1 requires returning to the parameter value that reproduces the original integral.

**Example 5 — your turn, with less help.** Let

$$K(a)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}\frac{\sin(at)}{t}\,dt, \qquad a\ge0.$$

**Your turn:** check the passport, calculate $K'(a)$, then use the convenient value $K(0)$.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

- **Prior existence:** near $0$, $|\sin(at)|/t\le a$; away from $0$, the exponential ensures integrability.
- **Differentiability with respect to $a$:** the derivative of the integrand is $e^{-t}\cos(at)$.
- **Independent domination:** $|e^{-t}\cos(at)|\le e^{-t}$ on every interval of parameter values.
- **Integrability:** the integral of $e^{-t}$ equals $1$.

**Theorem invoked:** differentiating under the integral sign.

**Conclusion:**

$$K'(a)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}\cos(at)\,dt=\frac{1}{1+a^2}.$$

Thus $K(a)=\arctan a+C$. Since $K(0)=0$, we have $C=0$ and

$$K(a)=\arctan a.$$

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Understanding question — answer">

**Why was the candidate $A(x)=e^{-x}I$ from the first table unable to determine $I$, even though it does satisfy $A'(x)=-A(x)$?**

Because solving this equation gives only $A(x)=Ce^{-x}$. The constant $C=A(0)$ is precisely $I$, the unknown number: no convenient anchor eliminates it. A useful parameter must simplify the integrand **and** provide an independent way to determine the constant; satisfying just one of these conditions is not enough.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Adding a parameter that merely decorates.** Multiplying the entire integral by $e^{-x}$ produces a true but circular equation. Always write what the integrand becomes **after** differentiation before accepting the candidate.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Differentiating before completing the passport.** The factor $t$ that cancels $1/t$ makes the calculation tempting, but it grants no permission. Fix an interval $[a,b]$ with $a>0$, dominate the derivative by $e^{-at}$, check its integrability, then invoke the theorem.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the constant of integration.** From $F'(x)=-1/(1+x^2)$, it does not follow that $F(x)=-\arctan x$. You must write $F(x)=C-\arctan x$ and then use the limit $F(x)\to0$ as $x\to+\infty$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Setting $x=0$ in a dominating function that requires $x>0$.** The function $e^{-xt}$ is integrable for $x>0$, but becomes $1$ at $x=0$. Removing the damping requires the interval–tail splitting from Proposition 3.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Claiming an asymptotic equivalent without controlling the remainder.** Integration by parts gives an identity, not automatically an approximation. Isolate $R(x)$, bound $|R(x)|$, then compare this bound with the leading term.

</Pitfall>

**1. Design the useful parameter.** According to Method 1 and the table in Example (physics) 1, placing $x$ in $e^{-xt}$ produces the factor $-t$ that cancels $1/t$. The passport in Proposition 1 then permits differentiation and gives

$$F'(x)=-\frac{1}{1+x^2}.$$

**2. Recover the constant.** Proposition 2 requires us to write $F(x)=C-\arctan x$. Since $|F(x)|\le1/x$, the reading tends to $0$ when the damping becomes very strong. We therefore obtain $C=\pi/2$ and

$$F(x)=\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan x.$$

**3. Remove the damping.** The split passport in Proposition 3 controls the tails by $2/A$ and justifies the limit $x\to0^+$. Consequently,

$$I=\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt =\lim_{x\to0^+}F(x) =\frac{\pi}{2} \approx1{,}570796.$$

The reading without damping is therefore exactly $\pi/2$.

**4. Certify the requested approximation.** Proposition 4, obtained through two integrations by parts, gives at $x=4$

$$\left|F(4)-\frac14\right| \le\frac1{192} \approx0{,}00521 <0{,}006.$$

Yes: for damping $x=4$, replacing the reading with $1/4$ satisfies the stated tolerance. The two numbers from the mission have now been established, together with their justifications.

## Lesson summary

You can now perform the three actions announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="Key results">

- **Design the parameter:** start from the factor to be removed, anticipate $\partial_x f$, check that the new integral is known and identify an anchor in advance.
- **Differentiate legitimately:** existence, pointwise differentiability, a dominating function independent of the parameter, integrability; only then invoke the theorem and write $F'$.
- **Determine the constant:** use a value at which the integrand vanishes or a limit at which the damping crushes the integral.
- **Remove the damping:** if global domination fails, separate a finite interval from an oscillating tail controlled uniformly.
- **Bound the remainder:** after integration by parts, isolate $R(x)$ and compare it with the leading term; here $F(x)=1/x+R(x)$ with $|R(x)|\le1/(3x^3)$.
- **Diffraction result:** $F(x)=\arctan(1/x)$ for $x>0$ and $\int_0^{+\infty}\sin(t)/t\,dt=\pi/2$.

</KeyResults>
