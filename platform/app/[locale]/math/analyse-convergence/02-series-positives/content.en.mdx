# Deciding whether the power of all the harmonics remains bounded

<LessonMeta id="math-an-c02" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

Tiny contributions can still produce an immense total: their sign is precisely what turns this concern into reliable comparisons.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="The power radiated by the harmonics of a vibrating string">

**Situation:** on its $n$-th harmonic, a string radiates power $P_n=12/n^{3/2}$ W; the model retains every harmonic, however far we continue.
**Question:** can we certify that their total power remains below $40$ W? And if the exponent $3/2$ were replaced by $1$, would a fixed ceiling still suffice?
**The obstacle:** each power eventually becomes tiny, but infinitely many still remain to be added and their exact total is unknown.
**The contract:** this chapter provides 3 tools (bounding a sum that can only grow, comparing it with a known model, replacing its tail with an area). By the end, we will decide between the two regimes and certify the $40$ W threshold, with figures to support the conclusion.

</Mission>

*Feeling confident? [Go straight to the TD (tutorial sheet)](../td-1/) — the theory will still be here when you get stuck.*

## The battle plan

To make a decision without knowing the exact total, we need three capabilities.

1. **Use the sign of the contributions**: a sum that can only grow has highly constrained behaviour.
2. **Recognise a reference model**: transfer its verdict through an inequality or asymptotic behaviour.
3. **Turn terms into areas**: decide logarithmic cases and bound the error after a given index.

## 1. Use a sum that can only grow

**Tool 1 — reduce infinitely many terms to a bound.** When every new contribution is positive, the successive totals all move in the same direction.

Problem: a <Terme id="serie-numerique">numerical series</Terme> can contain cancellations between signs; here no harmonic removes power.
We therefore need a term that isolates this more rigid case.

**Definition 1 (series with positive terms).** A <Def id="serie-a-termes-positifs">**series with positive terms**</Def> (a sum whose terms are all positive from some index onwards) is a series $\sum u_n$ such that there is an $n_0$ for which $u_n\ge0$ whenever $n\ge n_0$.

**Example (physics) 1.** For the string, $P_n=12/n^{3/2}>0$ at every index. Its first totals are

$$S_1=12,\qquad S_2=12+\frac{12}{2^{3/2}}\approx16{,}24,\qquad S_3\approx18{,}55\ \text{W}.$$

They increase: adding a harmonic can never reduce the power already counted.

**Remark.** “From some index onwards” is intentional. Changing, deleting or adding finitely many terms may change the value of the total, but never whether or not it is bounded.

**Remark (laboratory notation).** In a TP (laboratory practical), you would instead write $P(f_n)$, with $f_n=n f_1$, for the power measured at frequency $f_n$. Same object, different clothing: here the index $n$ simply numbers the spectral lines.

Problem: observing that the totals rise does not yet tell us whether they stop below a ceiling.
We therefore need to relate their behaviour to a property of increasing sequences.

**Theorem 1 (criterion for increasing partial sums).** A series with positive terms is <Terme id="serie-convergente">convergent</Terme> if and only if its sequence of <Terme id="somme-partielle">partial sums</Terme> is bounded above. If it is not bounded above, its partial sums tend to $+\infty$.

<Collapsible title="Proof — the theorem on monotone sequences does all the work">

From index $n_0$ onwards, we have

$$S_{N+1}-S_N=u_{N+1}\ge0.$$

The sequence $(S_N)$ is therefore increasing from that index onwards. An increasing sequence that is bounded above has a finite limit: the series converges. If it is not bounded above, then for every real number $A$ there is an index $N_A$ such that $S_{N_A}>A$; monotonicity then implies $S_N>A$ for every $N\ge N_A$. Thus $S_N\to+\infty$. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Theorem 1 does not itself provide the upper bound: it only says what we would need to find. Without positivity, the partial sums are no longer increasing and this reasoning disappears; sign cancellations must then be studied.

**Method 1 (write the initial diagnosis).** Always write the following four elements in this order:

1. **sign**: $u_n\ge0$ from some index onwards;
2. **dominant behaviour**: a known term, inequality or <Terme id="equivalent">asymptotic equivalent</Terme>;
3. **named reference**: state precisely which model is being used;
4. **verdict**: the series converges, or its partial sums tend to $+\infty$.

## 2. Choose a reference that really decides

**Tool 2 — transfer a known verdict.** A good reference resembles the term under study and lies on the correct side of the inequality.

Problem: Theorem 1 requires a global bound, which is often difficult to invent directly.
We therefore need a mechanism for term-by-term comparison with a sum that has already been classified.

**Definition 2 (compare positive terms).** The <Def id="critere-de-comparaison">**comparison test**</Def> (transferring the behaviour of a reference series to the series under study) consists in bounding, from some index onwards, two positive terms $u_n$ and $v_n$.

**Theorem 2 (direct comparison).** Suppose $0\le u_n\le v_n$ from some index onwards.

- if $\sum v_n$ converges, then $\sum u_n$ converges;
- if $\sum u_n$ diverges, then $\sum v_n$ diverges.

<Collapsible title="Proof — subtract the finite beginnings, then compare the totals">

Choose an index $n_0$ from which $0\le u_n\le v_n$. For $N\ge n_0$,

$$0\le\sum_{n=n_0}^{N}u_n\le\sum_{n=n_0}^{N}v_n.$$

If the sums on the right are bounded above, so are those on the left; Theorem 1 gives the first implication. The second is its contrapositive: if the larger series converged, the smaller one would converge as well. Terms before $n_0$ have no effect on the nature of the series. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Example 2.** For $n\ge1$,

$$
0\le\frac{1}{2^n+1}\le\frac{1}{2^n}.
$$

The <Terme id="serie-geometrique">geometric series</Terme> $\sum 2^{-n}$ converges; Theorem 2 therefore implies the convergence of $\sum 1/(2^n+1)$.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** An upper bound by a divergent series tells us nothing. For example, $1/n^2\le1/n$, but the smaller series converges and the larger one diverges. Always ask yourself: **does the model placed on the correct side have the verdict I need?**

Problem: for the powers $1/n^\alpha$, it would be costly to reconstruct a comparison for every new exponent.
We therefore need a reference family classified once and for all.

**Definition 3 (Riemann series).** For a real number $\alpha$, the <Def id="serie-de-riemann">**Riemann series**</Def> (the reference series for powers) is

$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{n^\alpha}.$$

**Theorem 3 (Riemann classification).** The series $\sum 1/n^\alpha$ converges if and only if $\alpha>1$. It diverges for $\alpha\le1$.

<Collapsible title="Proof — group the terms into dyadic blocks">

If $\alpha\le0$, the term $1/n^\alpha$ does not tend to $0$, so the series diverges.

If $0<\alpha\le1$, we have $1/n^\alpha\ge1/n$. The <Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme> $\sum 1/n$ diverges; Theorem 2 transfers this divergence.

Finally, suppose $\alpha>1$. In the block $2^k\le n<2^{k+1}$, there are $2^k$ terms and each is at most $2^{-k\alpha}$. The sum of the block is therefore at most

$$2^k2^{-k\alpha}=2^{-k(\alpha-1)}.$$

These upper bounds form a geometric series with ratio $2^{-(\alpha-1)}<1$. Theorem 2 gives convergence. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Example (physics) 3.** The power $P_n=12/n^{3/2}$ is a multiple of a Riemann term with exponent $3/2>1$: the total power has a finite value. If the exponent is $1$, we obtain $12$ times the harmonic series: no fixed ceiling then contains all the successive totals.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Theorem 3 classifies terms exactly of the form $1/n^\alpha$, but not a complicated expression that only resembles them. A comparison or an asymptotic equivalent must then be established before citing Riemann.

Problem: an exact inequality is sometimes artificial, whereas the leading-order behaviour of $u_n$ is immediately apparent.
We therefore need to turn an asymptotic resemblance into a solid comparison.

**Proposition 1 (comparison using an asymptotic equivalent).** If $u_n$ and $v_n$ are positive from some index onwards and if $u_n$ is asymptotically equivalent to $v_n$, then $\sum u_n$ and $\sum v_n$ have the same nature.

**Justification.** Since $u_n/v_n\to1$, from some index onwards we have

$$\frac12v_n\le u_n\le\frac32v_n.$$

Two applications of Theorem 2 transfer both convergence and divergence.

**Example 4.** Since $\sin x/x\to1$ when $x\to0$,

$$\frac{\sin(1/n)}{n}\sim\frac1{n^2}.$$

The terms are positive for sufficiently large $n$, and the Riemann reference has exponent $2$: the series converges.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If the ratio $u_n/v_n$ does not tend to a strictly positive constant, Proposition 1 does not apply. Never replace a term with an approximation without writing the asymptotic-equivalence symbol and naming the reference series.

## 3. Replace the tail with an area

**Tool 3 — decide and quantify.** A positive decreasing curve lets us read both the nature of the series and the size of what has not yet been added.

Problem: logarithmic powers and truncation errors often resist a simple direct comparison.
We therefore need to relate rectangles of width $1$ to the area under a curve.

**Definition 4 (integral test).** The <Def id="comparaison-serie-integrale">**integral test**</Def> (bounding a discrete sum by areas) associates the term $u_n$ with a function $f$ such that $u_n=f(n)$.

**Theorem 4 (integral test).** Let $f$ be continuous, positive and decreasing on $[1,+\infty)$, and set $u_n=f(n)$. For every $N\ge1$,

$$\int_1^{N+1}f(x)\,dx\le\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_n\le f(1)+\int_1^N f(x)\,dx.$$

Thus $\sum u_n$ and the improper integral $\int_1^{+\infty}f(x)\,dx$ have the same nature. If they converge, the <Terme id="reste-d-une-serie">remainder</Terme> $R_N=\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}u_n$ satisfies

$$\int_{N+1}^{+\infty}f(x)\,dx\le R_N\le\int_N^{+\infty}f(x)\,dx.$$

<Collapsible title="Proof — draw the rectangles above and below">

On $[n,n+1]$, monotonicity gives

$$f(n+1)\le f(x)\le f(n).$$

After integrating and then summing from $n=1$ to $N$, we obtain

$$\sum_{n=2}^{N+1}f(n)\le\int_1^{N+1}f(x)\,dx\le\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(n),$$

which gives the first bounds after reindexing. Starting the same argument at $N$ and letting the final index tend to infinity gives the bounds on the remainder. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Example (physics) 5.** For $f(x)=12x^{-3/2}$, all three hypotheses of Theorem 4 are satisfied on $[1,+\infty)$. We obtain

$$
12\int_1^{+\infty}x^{-3/2}\,dx
\le \sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{12}{n^{3/2}}
\le 12\left(1+\int_1^{+\infty}x^{-3/2}\,dx\right).
$$

The integral equals $2$, so

$$24\ \text{W}\le P_{\mathrm{tot}}\le36\ \text{W}.$$

Without knowing $P_{\mathrm{tot}}$ exactly, we have already certified the $40$ W threshold.

**Example (physics) 6.** After retaining the first $N$ harmonics, the omitted power satisfies

$$0\le R_N\le12\int_N^{+\infty}x^{-3/2}\,dx=\frac{24}{\sqrt N}\ \text{W}.$$

With $N=576$, the error is therefore at most $1$ W. Here the area provides more than a verdict: it provides usable precision.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If $f$ is not positive and decreasing from some point onwards, Theorem 4 does not apply in this form. Do not force the integral: return to Theorem 2 or Proposition 1, or look for a comparable function that does have these properties.

**Example 7 (the reference logarithmic family).** For $\beta\in\mathbb R$, the family traditionally called the Bertrand series is

$$\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{n(\ln n)^\beta}.$$

With $f(x)=1/[x(\ln x)^\beta]$, the change of variable $t=\ln x$ gives

$$
\int_2^{+\infty}\frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^\beta}
=\int_{\ln 2}^{+\infty}\frac{dt}{t^\beta}.
$$

If $\beta\ne1$, an antiderivative of $t^{-\beta}$ is $t^{1-\beta}/(1-\beta)$: it has a finite limit at $+\infty$ exactly when $\beta>1$. For $\beta=1$, the antiderivative $\ln t$ diverges. Thus the family converges exactly for $\beta>1$: one logarithm in the denominator is not enough; a power of the logarithm strictly greater than $1$ is required.

## Worked example — simplify an expression before choosing its reference

**Example 8 — a difference of roots.** Let us study

$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\sqrt{n^2+n}-n}{n}.$$

**Step 1 — check the sign.** For $n\ge1$, we have $n^2+n>n^2$, so $\sqrt{n^2+n}-n>0$.

*Why?* Theorem 2 and Proposition 1 require positive terms; this line permits us to use them.

**Step 2 — eliminate the unstable subtraction.** Multiply by the conjugate quantity:

$$
u_n=\frac{\sqrt{n^2+n}-n}{n}
=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n^2+n}+n}.
$$

*Why?* The aim is to reveal a power of $n$ in the denominator, so that we can recognise a reference from Theorem 3.

**Step 3 — establish the asymptotic equivalent.** Compare with $1/(2n)$:

$$
\frac{u_n}{1/(2n)}
=\frac{2n}{\sqrt{n^2+n}+n}
=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+1/n}+1}\longrightarrow1.
$$

Thus $u_n\sim1/(2n)$.

*Why?* Proposition 1 does not ask for a vague resemblance: this ratio tending to $1$ proves that the two series have the same nature.

**Step 4 — name the reference and conclude.** The series $\sum 1/(2n)$ is a multiple of the Riemann series with exponent $1$; it diverges by Theorem 3. Proposition 1 therefore gives the divergence of the series under study.

*Why?* A complete answer must end with the reference and then the verdict; “$u_n\sim1/(2n)$” alone does not answer the question.

**Example 9 — your turn, with less help.** Study

$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{n+1}{n^3+2}.$$

**Your turn:** check the sign, find the simplest asymptotic equivalent, name the reference and conclude.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

The terms are positive. Dividing numerator and denominator by their dominant powers,

$$
\frac{n+1}{n^3+2}\sim\frac{n}{n^3}=\frac1{n^2}.
$$

The Riemann series with exponent $2$ converges; Proposition 1 therefore gives convergence of the series under study. This reduction — positivity, asymptotic equivalent, reference, verdict — prepares you for the six classifications in exercise math-an-td1-03.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Understanding question — answer">

**Why is the asymptotic equivalent $u_n\sim1/(2n)$ from Example 8 useful, even though the terms of the two series are never equal?**

Because the ratio tends to $1$. From some index onwards, every term $u_n$ is therefore trapped between two fixed multiples of $1/(2n)$. Theorem 2 turns this bound into a shared verdict; relative differences that vanish cannot change the nature of a positive series.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**“The term tends to zero, so the total stabilises.”** No: $1/n\to0$, but the harmonic series diverges. Individual smallness is necessary, never sufficient; we need a comparison that applies to the whole series.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Comparing in the wrong direction.** From $0\le u_n\le v_n$ and the divergence of $\sum v_n$, nothing follows about $\sum u_n$. The standard counterexample is $1/n^2\le1/n$: the smaller series converges, while the larger one diverges.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Writing only an asymptotic equivalent.** An exam answer must contain four pieces: positivity, asymptotic equivalent, named reference series, verdict. Omitting the reference conceals the very theorem being used.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Integrating without checking monotonicity.** The integral test relies on the ordering of the rectangles. If the function oscillates, the bounds in Theorem 4 are no longer justified, even if calculating the integral is easy.

</Pitfall>

**1. Classify the contributions.** The powers $P_n=12/n^{3/2}$ are positive. By Definition 1 and Theorem 1, their partial sums increase; it remains to show that they are bounded above.

**2. Choose the reference.** We have

$$
P_n=12\frac1{n^{3/2}}.
$$

Theorem 3 classifies the Riemann series with exponent $3/2>1$: the total power is finite. If the exponent becomes $1$, the reference is the divergent harmonic series; the partial sums then exceed every fixed ceiling.

**3. Certify the stated threshold.** The function $f(x)=12x^{-3/2}$ is continuous, positive and decreasing. Theorem 4 and Example (physics) 5 give

$$
24\ \text{W}\le P_{\mathrm{tot}}\le36\ \text{W}<40\ \text{W}.
$$

**Yes, the model with exponent $3/2$ guarantees total power below $40$ W, even without knowing its exact value. With exponent $1$, no fixed ceiling suffices.** The two requested decisions have therefore been made, with a certified margin of at least $4$ W in the first regime.

## Lesson summary

You now have the three capabilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="Key results">

- **Use the sign:** for a series with positive terms, the partial sums increase; convergence is equivalent to their being bounded above (Theorem 1).
- **Compare in the correct direction:** a convergent series is a useful upper bound; a divergent series is a useful lower bound (Theorem 2).
- **Name Riemann:** $\sum1/n^\alpha$ converges exactly when $\alpha>1$; exponent $1$ lies on the divergent side (Theorem 3).
- **Simplify an expression:** positivity, asymptotic equivalent, named reference, verdict — these four lines form the complete write-up.
- **Compare with an area:** for positive decreasing $f$, the sum and integral have the same nature, and the integrals bound the remainder (Theorem 4).
- **Recognise the logarithm:** $\sum1/[n(\ln n)^\beta]$ converges exactly when $\beta>1$.

</KeyResults>
