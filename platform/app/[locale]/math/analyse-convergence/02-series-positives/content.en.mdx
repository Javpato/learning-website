<GuidingQuestion>

The two series $\sum1/n$ and $\sum1/n^2$ have positive terms tending to $0$, yet one diverges and the other converges. **How can we compare their speed of accumulation without knowing their sums?**

</GuidingQuestion>

## 1. Positivity: turning convergence into a boundedness problem

For an arbitrary series the partial sums may move forwards and backwards. With positive terms they can only increase: that constraint changes the problem entirely.

Problem: "the partial sums stay bounded" will be used several times. We must first say what it means.

**Definition 1 (sequence bounded above).** A sequence $(x_N)$ is **bounded above** if there is a real $M$ with $x_N\le M$ for every $N$. It is **bounded** if $|x_N|\le M$ for some $M$ and every $N$.

An upper bound does not say that we know the smallest one; it says only that a common ceiling exists.

Problem: we need a name for those series whose running totals can never come back down.

**Definition 2 (positive series).** A <Def id="serie-a-termes-positifs">**series with positive terms**</Def> is a <Terme id="serie-numerique">numerical series</Terme> such that $u_n\ge0$ from some index on.

Changing or deleting finitely many terms does not change its behaviour: the <Terme id="somme-partielle">partial sums</Terme> are merely translated by a constant.

**Theorem 1 (fundamental criterion for positive series).** A series with positive terms converges if and only if the sequence of its partial sums is bounded above. If it is not, then $S_N\to+\infty$.

<Proof title="increasing and bounded above implies convergent">

From the index where $u_n\ge0$,

$$
S_{N+1}-S_N=u_{N+1}\ge0.
$$

The sequence $(S_N)$ is therefore increasing.

- If the series converges, $(S_N)$ has a finite limit and is necessarily bounded above.
- Conversely, if $(S_N)$ is bounded above, the monotone convergence theorem gives that $(S_N)$ converges to its supremum.
- If it is not bounded above, its increase implies that, for every $A$, all sufficiently late $S_N$ exceed $A$: $S_N\to+\infty$.

**What the proof teaches.** Positivity prevents cancellations. It is what turns a mere ceiling into a proof of convergence. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** For terms of varying sign, bounded partial sums do not suffice: they could oscillate without a limit. Positivity is not decorative.

### Mini-exercises

1. If $u_n\ge0$ and $S_N\le7$ for every $N$, what can be concluded?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The series converges, by Theorem 1. We cannot conclude that its sum equals $7$; only that it is at most $7$.

   </Collapsible>

2. The partial sums of $\sum(-1)^n$ are bounded. Does the series converge?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No: they alternate between two values. This example shows exactly why Theorem 1 requires positivity.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Transmitting a behaviour by comparison

Problem: if a positive series is smaller than one whose total is finite, it too ought to stay under a ceiling. We must write that intuition in the right direction.

**Definition 3 (comparison).** The <Def id="critere-de-comparaison">**comparison test**</Def> sets two positive terms $u_n$ and $v_n$ side by side from some index on.

**Theorem 2 (direct comparison).** Suppose $0\le u_n\le v_n$ from some index on.

1. If $\sum v_n$ converges, then $\sum u_n$ converges.
2. If $\sum u_n$ diverges, then $\sum v_n$ diverges.

<Proof title="comparing positive tails">

Ignore the finite beginning before the index where the inequality holds. For every later $N$,

$$
0\le \sum u_n\le \sum v_n,
$$

where both sums are finite and run over the same indices.

If the partial sums of $\sum v_n$ are bounded above, so are those of $\sum u_n$; Theorem 1 concludes. The second point is the contrapositive of the first, with the roles correctly ordered.

**What the proof teaches.** Convergence travels down to the smaller series; divergence travels up to the larger one. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Being smaller than a divergent series, or larger than a convergent one, gives no verdict at all.

**Example 1.** For $n\ge2$,

$$
0\le\frac1{n^2}\le\frac1{n(n-1)}=\frac1{n-1}-\frac1n.
$$

The dominating series telescopes, so $\sum1/n^2$ converges.

Problem: an exact inequality is sometimes hard to guess, whereas a quotient tends to $1$.

**Proposition 1 (comparison by an equivalent).** If $u_n,v_n>0$ from some index on and $u_n\sim v_n$, then $\sum u_n$ and $\sum v_n$ behave the same way.

<Proof title="turning a limit of a quotient into a double inequality">

The <Terme id="equivalent">equivalent</Terme> $u_n\sim v_n$ means $u_n/v_n\to1$. Taking a margin of $1/2$, say, there exists $N_0$ such that, for $n\ge N_0$,

$$
\frac12v_n\le u_n\le\frac32v_n.
$$

Theorem 2 applied in both directions therefore transmits convergence and divergence.

**What the proof teaches.** An equivalent is not a magic substitution: the limit produces the positive inequalities that comparison needs. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Without eventual positivity, two equivalent terms may generate series of different behaviour. That fragility will be studied with alternating series.

### Mini-exercises

1. Study $\sum n/(n^3+1)$.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The term is positive and equivalent to $1/n^2$. As soon as the reference series is classified below, Proposition 1 will give convergence.

   </Collapsible>

2. From $0\le1/n^2\le1/n$, can anything be concluded?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No. The series on the right diverges: being smaller than it is the inconclusive case.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. Building Riemann's reference scale

Problem: the powers $1/n^\alpha$ appear after almost every rational equivalent. We must know their frontier exactly.

**Definition 4 (Riemann series).** For $\alpha\in\mathbb R$, the <Def id="serie-de-riemann">**Riemann series**</Def> is

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^\alpha}.
$$

**Theorem 3 (Riemann classification).** It converges if and only if $\alpha>1$.

To prove that frontier, we build a more general tool.

Problem: a positive decreasing function can be trapped between rectangles of width $1$.

**Definition 5 (series-integral comparison).** The <Def id="comparaison-serie-integrale">**series-integral comparison**</Def> associates $u_n=f(n)$ with the area under a function $f$ that is continuous, positive and decreasing.

**Theorem 4 (integral test).** Under those three hypotheses, for $N\ge1$,

$$
\int_1^{N+1}f(t)\,dt\le\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(n)\le f(1)+\int_1^{N}f(t)\,dt.
$$

The series $\sum f(n)$ and the improper integral $\int_1^{+\infty}f(t)\,dt$ therefore behave the same way.

<Proof title="trapping each rectangle by the neighbouring area">

On $[n,n+1]$, decrease gives

$$
f(n+1)\le f(t)\le f(n).
$$

Integrating over that interval of length $1$,

$$
f(n+1)\le\int_n^{n+1}f(t)\,dt\le f(n).
$$

Adding these inequalities from $n=1$ to $N$ supplies the two announced bounds. If the integral stays bounded, so do the partial sums; if it tends to $+\infty$, the lower bound forces the sums to do the same.

**What the proof teaches.** The hypotheses "positive and decreasing" are exactly the ones that place the curve between the rectangles. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Proof of Theorem 3.** Take $f(t)=t^{-\alpha}$.

- If $\alpha>1$, then $f$ is positive decreasing and $\int_1^{+\infty}t^{-\alpha}\,dt=1/(\alpha-1)$ is finite.
- If $0<\alpha\le1$, the integral diverges.
- If $\alpha\le0$, the term $1/n^\alpha$ does not even tend to $0$.

Theorem 4 gives exactly the frontier $\alpha=1$. $\blacksquare$

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If $f$ is not decreasing, the picture of the rectangles no longer justifies the bounds. The series may nevertheless converge or diverge for some other reason.

**Example 2 (Bertrand).** For $n\ge2$,

$$
\sum\frac1{n(\ln n)^\beta}
$$

behaves like $\int_2^{+\infty}dt/[t(\ln t)^\beta]$. With $y=\ln t$ we obtain $\int dy/y^\beta$: convergence exactly for $\beta>1$.

### Mini-exercises

1. Classify $\sum1/n^{1{,}01}$ and then $\sum1/n^{0{,}99}$.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The first converges and the second diverges. The frontier depends on $\alpha>1$, even though the exponents are very close.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why does "$1/[n\ln n]<1/n$" not prove convergence?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because the dominating harmonic series diverges. The integral test moreover shows that $\sum1/[n\ln n]$ diverges too.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. Choosing the right first move

| Signal in $u_n$ | First move | Reference or proof |
| --- | --- | --- |
| rational fraction, root | dominant equivalent | Riemann series |
| natural inequality | direct comparison | geometric, telescoping or Riemann |
| logarithm near the frontier | series-integral comparison | Bertrand series |
| factorial, product, $n$-th power | do not force it | ratio or root, next lesson |

**Worked example.** Let us study

$$
\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}\frac{\sqrt{n^2+3n}}{n^3+1}.
$$

**Step 1 — check the sign.** The term is positive.

*Why?* Comparison by an equivalent requires eventual positivity.

**Step 2 — extract the dominant powers.** We have $\sqrt{n^2+3n}\sim n$ and $n^3+1\sim n^3$, so

$$
u_n\sim\frac1{n^2}.
$$

*Why?* We reduce the expression to a reference series already classified by Theorem 3.

**Step 3 — cite the transfer.** Both terms are positive and $\sum1/n^2$ converges; Proposition 1 gives the convergence of $\sum u_n$.

*Why?* A complete write-up names the sign, the equivalent, the reference and the verdict.

**Step 4 — bound the remainder.** For $n$ large enough, $u_n\le2/n^2$, so

$$
0\le R_N\le2\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2}\le2\int_N^{+\infty}\frac{dt}{t^2}=\frac2N.
$$

*Why?* The <Terme id="reste-d-une-serie">remainder</Terme> becomes a numerical error thanks to the same comparison tool.

<Collapsible title="Your turn — answer">

Study $\sum (3n+1)/(n^3-2)$ from an index where the denominator is positive.

The term is positive from $n=2$ on and equivalent to $3/n^2$. The series converges by comparison with an equivalent, against the Riemann series of exponent $2$.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Comparing in the wrong direction.** Smaller than a divergent series and larger than a convergent one are both inconclusive situations.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Using an equivalent without constant sign.** The proof of Proposition 1 rests on a positive double inequality.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Saying only "like Riemann".** Write the equivalent, the positivity and the reference exponent.

</Pitfall>

<GuidingAnswer>

For a positive series, deciding convergence amounts to bounding its partial sums above. A reference series supplies that ceiling by comparison; a positive equivalent supplies the necessary inequalities; the integral builds Riemann's scale. We generally do not compute the sum: **we prove that it cannot escape**.

</GuidingAnswer>

## Summary — choosing and justifying

<KeyResults title="The decision sheet">

| Situation | Hypotheses to write | Conclusion | Inconclusive case |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| positive series | $u_n\ge0$ and $(S_N)$ bounded above | <Terme id="serie-convergente">convergence</Terme> | without positivity |
| $0\le u_n\le v_n$ | from some index on | convergence travels down, divergence travels up | smaller than a divergent one |
| $u_n\sim v_n$ | eventual positivity | same behaviour | varying signs |
| $u_n=f(n)$ | $f$ continuous, positive, decreasing | same behaviour as the integral | decrease absent |
| $1/n^\alpha$ | none other | converges if $\alpha>1$ | none: complete classification |

**Proofs worth being able to reconstruct:** the increase of $S_N$; the transmission of a bound; the double inequality coming from an equivalent; rectangles under a decreasing function. The <Terme id="serie-geometrique">geometric series</Terme> and the <Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme> remain the two extreme landmarks.

</KeyResults>
