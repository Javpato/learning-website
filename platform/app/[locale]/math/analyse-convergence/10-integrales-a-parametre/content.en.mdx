# Justify a filter response before calculating it

<LessonMeta id="math-an-c10" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

An integral formula can produce a beautiful curve and still be unjustified: before manipulating it, we check that it exists, that it has no jumps and that its slope really has the stated meaning.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="The frequency response of an RC filter with unit time constant">

**Situation:** an RC filter receives an oscillation with reduced angular frequency $x$; its in-phase response is modelled by $G(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}\cos(xt)\,dt$.
**Precise question:** can we use this formula for every $x\ge0$, without jumps or corners? What does it give at $x=0$, $x=1$ and $x=2$, and how quickly does the response change near $x=1$?
**The obstacle:** the sum extends over an infinite duration, while $x$ moves inside the cosine; the rules learned on a finite interval provide no automatic permission.
**The contract:** this chapter provides 3 tools (certifying that the formula yields a number, moving to nearby frequencies, bringing the slope calculation under the integral sign). By the end, we will answer every question with numerical evidence.

</Mission>

*Feeling confident? [Go straight to the TD (tutorial sheet)](../td-6/) — the theory will still be here when you get stuck.*

## The battle plan

To make the model rigorous, we will always follow this order.

1. **Existence first**: find an integrable envelope that does not depend on frequency.
2. **Nearby frequencies next**: control the passage from one value of $x$ to another with a complete checklist.
3. **Slope last**: dominate the derivative of the integrand, and only then differentiate the response.

## 1. Certify that the formula yields a number

**Tool 1 — establish existence before any manipulation.** Until the improper integral has been justified, the function $G$ is still only a candidate expression.

Problem: the variable $x$ remains outside the calculation in $t$, but it changes the integrand and therefore potentially its convergence.
We therefore need a name for the function obtained when this integral exists.

**Definition 1 (parameter-dependent integral).** Let $I$ be an interval and $f:I\times[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb R$. A <Def id="integrale-a-parametre">**parameter-dependent integral**</Def> (a function constructed by integrating with respect to $t$ while keeping $x$ free) is a function

$$F(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}f(x,t)\,dt.$$

defined for those $x$ in $I$ for which the improper integral exists.

**Remark.** There are two variables, but only one is integrated: $t$ is the integration variable and $x$ is the parameter. In TP (lab sessions), you will often write $H(\omega)$ and $h(t)$ instead of $G(x)$ and $e^{-t}$; same object, different attire.

Problem: checking every value of $x$ separately would take forever.
We therefore need a single envelope that controls the whole family.

**Definition 2 (domination hypothesis).** On a parameter set $I$, a <Def id="hypothese-de-domination">**domination hypothesis**</Def> (an envelope common to all the curves) is an inequality

$$|f(x,t)|\le g(t)\qquad\text{pour tout }x\in I\text{ et tout }t\ge0.$$

where $g$ is integrable and, crucially, **independent of the parameter $x$**.

**Example (physics) 1.** For the filter, set $f(x,t)=e^{-t}\cos(xt)$. Since $|\cos(xt)|\le1$,

$$|f(x,t)|\le e^{-t}=g_0(t),\qquad \int_0^{+\infty}g_0(t)\,dt=1.$$

The same function $g_0$ works for every real frequency.

**Proposition 1 (existence by domination).** If, for every $x$ in $I$, the function $t\mapsto f(x,t)$ is piecewise <Terme id="continuite">continuous</Terme> on every closed interval, and if there is an integrable function $g$ such that $|f(x,t)|\le g(t)$, then $F(x)$ exists absolutely for every $x$ in $I$.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** The absence of a common dominating function does not prove that the integral diverges. This test is sufficient, not necessary: we can then restrict the parameter set, split the domain in $t$, or study each $x$ separately.

**Hypothesis checklist — existence of $G$.**

- **Regularity in $t$:** for every $x\in\mathbb R$, $t\mapsto e^{-t}\cos(xt)$ is continuous on $[0,+\infty)$.
- **Bound:** $|e^{-t}\cos(xt)|\le g_0(t)$ for every $t\ge0$.
- **Independence:** $g_0(t)=e^{-t}$ does not contain the parameter $x$.
- **Integrability:** $\int_0^{+\infty}g_0(t)\,dt=1<+\infty$.

**Theorem invoked:** Proposition 1, the existence test by domination.

**Conclusion:** $G(x)$ exists absolutely for every $x\in\mathbb R$.

<Collapsible title="Proof — why domination gives existence">

Fix $x\in I$. For every $A>0$, piecewise continuity allows us to form $\int_0^A|f(x,t)|\,dt$. Domination gives

$$0\le\int_0^A|f(x,t)|\,dt\le\int_0^A g(t)\,dt\le\int_0^{+\infty}g(t)\,dt.$$

As $A$ grows, the left-hand member is increasing and bounded above, so it has a finite limit. The integral of $|f(x,t)|$ converges, hence that of $f(x,t)$ converges absolutely. Since the same $g$ works for every $x$, the argument holds throughout $I$. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## 2. Move from one frequency to nearby ones

**Tool 2 — guarantee the absence of jumps.** We want to establish the continuity of the response, meaning that nearby frequencies produce nearby values.

Problem: $f(x,t)$ varies smoothly with $x$ at each fixed instant, but adding over an infinite duration could amplify this variation.
We therefore need a theorem that allows a limit in $x$ to pass through the integral.

**Theorem 1 (continuity under the integral sign).** Let $F(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}f(x,t)\,dt$ on an interval $I$. Suppose that:

1. the integral defining $F(x)$ exists for every $x\in I$;
2. for every $t\ge0$, the function $x\mapsto f(x,t)$ is continuous on $I$;
3. there is an integrable dominating function $g(t)$, independent of $x$, such that $|f(x,t)|\le g(t)$ on $I$.

Then $F$ is continuous on $I$.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** A bound $|f(x,t)|\le g_x(t)$ whose envelope depends on $x$ does not allow Theorem 1 to be applied throughout $I$. Look for local domination around the point being studied, or restrict $I$ to an interval on which one function works everywhere.

**Example (physics) 2 — continuity checklist for $G$.**

- **Prior existence:** $G(x)$ exists for every $x\in\mathbb R$ by the checklist in section 1.
- **Regularity in $x$:** for every $t\ge0$, $x\mapsto e^{-t}\cos(xt)$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$.
- **Independent domination:** $|e^{-t}\cos(xt)|\le g_0(t)=e^{-t}$, with no $x$ in $g_0$.
- **Integrability:** $\int_0^{+\infty}g_0(t)\,dt=1$.

**Theorem invoked:** Theorem 1 on continuity under the integral sign.

**Conclusion:** $G$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$; the filter response jumps at no frequency.

<Collapsible title="Proof — passing the limit under the integral sign">

Fix $x_0\in I$ and a sequence $x_n\to x_0$. For every fixed $t$, the continuity of $f$ with respect to $x$ gives

$$f(x_n,t)\longrightarrow f(x_0,t).$$

Moreover,

$$|f(x_n,t)-f(x_0,t)|\le |f(x_n,t)|+|f(x_0,t)|\le2g(t).$$

and $2g$ is integrable. The dominated convergence theorem then allows us to <Terme id="interversion-limite-integrale">swap the limit and the integral</Terme>:

$$F(x_n)-F(x_0)=\int_0^{+\infty}\bigl(f(x_n,t)-f(x_0,t)\bigr)\,dt\longrightarrow0.$$

Thus $F$ is continuous at $x_0$, and therefore on $I$. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## 3. Bring the slope calculation under the integral

**Tool 3 — calculate a legitimate derivative.** The integrand is easy to differentiate with respect to $x$, but that algebraic ease is not permission.

Problem: the difference quotient of $F$ is itself an integral, and its limit must cross an infinite domain.
We therefore need a name and a checklist for this operation.

**Definition 3 (differentiating under the integral sign).** <Def id="derivation-sous-le-signe-integral">**Differentiating under the integral sign**</Def> (calculating the slope of the integrated function by differentiating its integrand) is the identity

$$F'(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}\partial_x f(x,t)\,dt,$$

when a theorem justifies this equality.

**Theorem 2 (differentiating under the integral sign).** Let $F(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}f(x,t)\,dt$ on an interval $I$. Suppose that:

1. $F(x)$ exists for every $x\in I$;
2. for every $t\ge0$, the function $x\mapsto f(x,t)$ is differentiable on $I$;
3. there is an integrable function $g_1(t)$, independent of $x$, such that $|\partial_x f(x,t)|\le g_1(t)$ on $I$.

Then $F$ is differentiable on $I$ and

$$F'(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}\partial_x f(x,t)\,dt.$$

If, in addition, $x\mapsto\partial_x f(x,t)$ is continuous for every $t$, then $F'$ is continuous.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Dominating $f$ is not enough to differentiate: we must dominate $\partial_x f$. If the dominating function for the derivative depends on $x$, work on a fixed neighbourhood of $x_0$; if it is not integrable, the theorem is inconclusive and another method is needed.

**Example (physics) 3 — differentiation checklist for $G$.** First calculate

$$\partial_x\bigl(e^{-t}\cos(xt)\bigr)=-t e^{-t}\sin(xt).$$

Then complete the checklist without skipping a line.

- **Prior existence:** $G(x)$ exists on $\mathbb R$ by Proposition 1.
- **Differentiability in $x$:** for every $t\ge0$, $x\mapsto e^{-t}\cos(xt)$ is differentiable.
- **Independent domination:** $|{-t e^{-t}\sin(xt)}|\le g_1(t)=t e^{-t}$, with no $x$ in $g_1$.
- **Integrability:** $\int_0^{+\infty}g_1(t)\,dt=1$.

**Theorem invoked:** Theorem 2 on differentiating under the integral sign.

**Conclusion:** $G$ is differentiable on $\mathbb R$ and

$$G'(x)=-\int_0^{+\infty}t e^{-t}\sin(xt)\,dt.$$

The same dominating function, together with the continuity of $x\mapsto-t e^{-t}\sin(xt)$, also shows that $G'$ is continuous.

<Collapsible title="Proof — where the dominating function for the derivative comes from">

Fix $x\in I$ and take $h\ne0$ small enough that $x+h\in I$. The difference quotient is

$$\frac{F(x+h)-F(x)}{h}=\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{f(x+h,t)-f(x,t)}{h}\,dt.$$

For every $t$, the quotient under the integral tends to $\partial_x f(x,t)$. By the mean value theorem applied to $x\mapsto f(x,t)$, there is a point between $x$ and $x+h$ at which the absolute value of $\partial_x f$ equals that of this quotient; it is therefore bounded by $g_1(t)$.

The dominated convergence theorem allows us to pass to the limit under the integral. We obtain

$$F'(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}\partial_x f(x,t)\,dt.$$

The checklist requires a dominating function for the derivative, not merely for $f$, precisely in order to control the difference quotient. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Example 4.** Return to $K(a)=\int_0^{+\infty}\dfrac{e^{-t}}{1+a t}\,dt$ for $a\ge0$. For the slope, calculate

$$\partial_a\left(\frac{e^{-t}}{1+a t}\right)=-\frac{t e^{-t}}{(1+a t)^2},\qquad \left|\partial_a\left(\frac{e^{-t}}{1+a t}\right)\right|\le t e^{-t}.$$

- **Prior existence:** $0\le e^{-t}/(1+a t)\le e^{-t}$, so $K(a)$ exists.
- **Differentiability in $a$:** the displayed derivative exists for every $t\ge0$.
- **Independent domination:** its absolute value is bounded by $g_1(t)=t e^{-t}$, with no $a$.
- **Integrability:** $\int_0^{+\infty}g_1(t)\,dt=1$.

**Theorem invoked:** Theorem 2.

**Conclusion:**

$$K'(a)=-\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{t e^{-t}}{(1+a t)^2}\,dt<0,$$

so $K$ is decreasing. Moreover, $K(0)=1$ and $K'(0)=-1$; its first-order <Terme id="developpement-limite">Taylor expansion</Terme> is

$$K(a)=1-a+o(a)\qquad(a\to0^+).$$

This is the mechanism of exercise math-an-td6-01: the checklist gives the derivative, then its sign gives monotonicity and its value gives the first-order term. The change of dominating function between $f$ and $\partial_a f$ also prepares exercise math-an-td6-02.

## Worked example — complete the checklist, then find the filter formula

**Example 5 — complete calculation of $G$.** Also set

$$S(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}\sin(xt)\,dt.$$

**Step 1 — certify both integrals.** For every $x\in\mathbb R$,

$$|e^{-t}\cos(xt)|\le e^{-t},\qquad |e^{-t}\sin(xt)|\le e^{-t}.$$

and $e^{-t}$ is integrable. Proposition 1 therefore gives the absolute existence of $G(x)$ and $S(x)$.

*Why?* The integrations by parts that follow only make sense after establishing the existence of the objects being manipulated.

**Step 2 — relate $G$ to $S$.** Integration by parts on $[0,A]$, followed by passage to the limit $A\to+\infty$, gives

$$G(x)=1-xS(x).$$

*Why?* The aim is to make the same pair of functions appear in two algebraic relations; the factor $x$ arises when $\cos(xt)$ is differentiated with respect to $t$.

**Step 3 — relate $S$ to $G$.** Applying the same operation to the sine gives

$$S(x)=xG(x).$$

*Why?* This second relation will eliminate the auxiliary integral $S$; a single relation containing two unknowns was not enough.

**Step 4 — solve the equation.** Replacing $S(x)$ with $xG(x)$ in the first relation,

$$G(x)=1-x^2G(x),\qquad (1+x^2)G(x)=1,$$

hence

$$G(x)=\frac{1}{1+x^2}.$$

*Why?* The calculation reduces the integral to an algebraic equation whose coefficient $1+x^2$ never vanishes on $\mathbb R$.

**Step 5 — read off slope and variation.** The closed-form expression gives

$$G'(x)=-\frac{2x}{(1+x^2)^2}.$$

Thus $G$ is decreasing on $[0,+\infty)$ and, near $x=1$,

$$G(1+h)=\frac12-\frac12h+o(h).$$

*Why?* Theorem 2 made the derivative legitimate; the closed-form expression now allows us to read off its sign and value without retaining an oscillatory integral.

**Example 6 — your turn, with less help.** Return to

$$J(a)=\int_0^1\frac{t^a-1}{\ln t}\,dt\qquad(a>-1).$$

**Your turn:** differentiate under the integral sign, then use $J(0)=0$ to identify $J(a)$.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

Around $a_0>-1$, enclose $a$ in $[b,c]\subset(-1,+\infty)$ and complete the local checklist.

- **Prior existence:** the identity $(t^a-1)/\ln t=\int_0^a t^s\,ds$ extends the quotient at $t=1$ and bounds it by $C t^{\min(b,0)}$, which is integrable on $(0,1)$.
- **Differentiability in $a$:** $\partial_a((t^a-1)/\ln t)=t^a$ for every $t\in(0,1)$.
- **Independent domination:** since $a\ge b$ and $0<t<1$, we have $0<t^a\le t^b$, with no $a$ in the bound.
- **Integrability:** $b>-1$ gives $\int_0^1t^b\,dt=1/(b+1)<+\infty$.

**Theorem invoked:** Theorem 2.

**Conclusion:**

$$J'(a)=\int_0^1t^a\,dt=\frac{1}{a+1}.$$

Since $J(0)=0$, we obtain

$$J(a)=\ln(a+1).$$

The apparent singularity at $t=1$ has been extended and existence handled before differentiation: this is the mechanism of exercise math-an-td6-03.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Understanding question — answer">

**Why is $e^{-t}$ enough for the existence and continuity of $G$, but not for its differentiation?**

Because the objects to be controlled are not the same. For existence and continuity, we bound the integrand $e^{-t}\cos(xt)$. For differentiation, the difference quotient is controlled by the derivative with respect to $x$, which contains the factor $t$: we therefore need the new dominating function $t e^{-t}$. A checklist can be reused only if its lines correspond exactly to the theorem invoked.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Calculating before proving existence.** Integration by parts on $[0,A]$ does not by itself justify passage to $A\to+\infty$. Write the existence checklist and its parameter-independent dominating function first.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Reusing the wrong dominating function.** The bound $|f(x,t)|\le e^{-t}$ controls $G$, but not automatically $G'$. After differentiation the factor $t$ appears: the correct envelope becomes $t e^{-t}$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Leaving the parameter in the envelope.** Writing $|f(x,t)|\le g_x(t)$ can establish existence pointwise, but not the uniform checklist for an interchange theorem. The stated dominating function must be explicit and independent of $x$ on the interval being studied.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the parameter domain.** For $\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\,dt$, domination by $e^{-at}$ is valid only on $x\ge a>0$. As $a$ tends to $0$, no common integrable dominating function remains: interior first, boundary second.

</Pitfall>

<MissionSolved>

**1. Certify the formula for every frequency.** The checklist in section 1 uses

$$|e^{-t}\cos(xt)|\le e^{-t},\qquad \int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}\,dt=1.$$

The dominating function is explicit and independent of $x$; Proposition 1 proves that $G(x)$ exists absolutely for every $x\in\mathbb R$.

**2. Rule out jumps and corners.** The checklist in Example (physics) 2 reuses the dominating function $g_0(t)=e^{-t}$ and Theorem 1: $G$ is continuous. For the slope, Example (physics) 3 controls

$$|\partial_x(e^{-t}\cos(xt))|=|{-t e^{-t}\sin(xt)}|\le t e^{-t},\qquad \int_0^{+\infty}t e^{-t}\,dt=1.$$

Theorem 2 therefore gives a continuous derivative on $\mathbb R$. The response has neither jumps nor corners.

**3. Give the requested values.** Example 5 establishes

$$G(x)=\frac{1}{1+x^2},\qquad G'(x)=-\frac{2x}{(1+x^2)^2}.$$

Thus

$$G(0)=1,\qquad G(1)=\frac12,\qquad G(2)=\frac15=0{,}2,\qquad G'(1)=-\frac12.$$

Near $x=1$, an angular-frequency increase of $0{,}1$ therefore lowers the response by about $0{,}05$: $G(1{,}1)\approx0{,}45$, compared with the exact value $0{,}45249$. The model is valid at every frequency, varies without discontinuity and does have the stated slope; every question in the mission has been answered.

</MissionSolved>

## Lesson summary

You can now apply the three announced tools, in the correct order.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Existence first:** identify $f(x,t)$, then exhibit $|f(x,t)|\le g_0(t)$ with $g_0$ integrable and independent of $x$.
- **Continuity next:** check existence, continuity in $x$ for fixed $t$, common domination and integrability; name Theorem 1, then conclude.
- **Differentiation last:** calculate $\partial_x f$, find $|\partial_x f(x,t)|\le g_1(t)$ with $g_1$ integrable and independent of $x$, complete the checklist, then invoke Theorem 2.
- **Filter dominating functions:** $g_0(t)=e^{-t}$ for existence and continuity; $g_1(t)=t e^{-t}$ for the first derivative.
- **Filter response:** $G(x)=1/(1+x^2)$, $G'(x)=-2x/(1+x^2)^2$; at positive frequencies, the response decreases.
- **Inconclusive case:** failure of a checklist does not prove failure of the property; it calls for local domination, splitting the domain, or a direct calculation.

</KeyResults>
