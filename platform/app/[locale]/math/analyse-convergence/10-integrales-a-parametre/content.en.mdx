<GuidingQuestion>

With $x$ fixed, $\displaystyle\int_I f(x,t)\,dt$ is merely a number. Letting $x$ vary manufactures a **function** $F$. Does it inherit the regularity of $f$? **And why does the answer depend on a hypothesis that speaks neither of continuity nor of differentiability, but of a mere bound?**

</GuidingQuestion>

## 1. First of all, existing

**Definition 1 (parameter-dependent integral).** Let $I$ be an interval and $f$ a function of two variables. A <Def id="integrale-a-parametre">**parameter-dependent integral**</Def> is a function of the form

$$
F(x)=\int_I f(x,t)\,dt ,
$$

defined for those values of $x$ for which $t\mapsto f(x,t)$ is integrable on $I$.

Problem: on an unbounded interval, or near a point where the integrand blows up, the integral is an <Terme id="integrale-impropre">improper integral</Terme>. Its existence depends on $x$, and that is the first thing to establish: a regularity theorem applied to a function that does not exist proves nothing.

**Method 1 (domain of definition).** With $x$ **fixed**, cut $I$ at each problematic endpoint and compare with the two reference scales of the toolbox: $\int_1^{+\infty}dt/t^\alpha$ converges for $\alpha>1$, and $\int_0^1dt/t^\alpha$ converges for $\alpha<1$.

**Example 1 (the Gamma function).** For which $x$ does the integral

$$
\Gamma(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}t^{\,x-1}e^{-t}\,dt
$$

exist? Cut at $1$ and treat the two pieces separately.

- Near $0$: $e^{-t}\to1$, so $t^{x-1}e^{-t}\sim t^{x-1}$. The integral $\int_0^1t^{x-1}dt$ converges if and only if $1-x<1$, that is $x>0$.
- Near $+\infty$: $t^{x-1}e^{-t}=o(1/t^2)$ whatever the value of $x$, since the exponential crushes every power (<Terme id="croissances-comparees">comparative growth</Terme>). The integral therefore always converges on that side.

Conclusion: $\Gamma$ is defined exactly on $(0,+\infty)$.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Establishing existence with $x$ fixed gives **no** information about the regularity of $F$. The next two sections show that a perfectly well-defined integral can define a discontinuous function.

### Mini-exercises

1. For which $x$ does $\displaystyle\int_1^{+\infty}\frac{dt}{t^{\,x}\ln t}$ converge near $+\infty$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   For $x>1$: the logarithm changes nothing in the comparison, since one can interpose $1/t^\beta$ with $1<\beta<x$. For $x\le1$ the integral diverges, the Bertrand series giving the exact frontier.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why is the integral of Example 1 cut into two pieces?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because the two endpoints pose **opposite** problems: at $0$ one must blow up slowly ($x>0$), at $+\infty$ one must decay fast. A single comparison cannot handle both, and the conditions are not the same.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Continuity, and why a domination is needed

Problem: saying that $F$ is continuous at $x_0$ means writing

$$
\lim_{x\to x_0}\int_I f(x,t)\,dt=\int_I \lim_{x\to x_0}f(x,t)\,dt .
$$

This is again an interchange between a limit and an integral — the problem of lesson 07. But here $I$ is in general **not a segment**, and lesson 07 showed that uniform convergence itself is then not enough.

**Counterexample 1 (mass escaping).** On $I=[0,+\infty)$, set for $x>0$

$$
f(x,t)=x\,e^{-xt},\qquad\text{and}\qquad f(0,t)=0 .
$$

With $x>0$ fixed the integral computes exactly:

$$
F(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}x\,e^{-xt}\,dt=\Bigl[-e^{-xt}\Bigr]_0^{+\infty}=1 .
$$

And $F(0)=0$. Yet with $t>0$ fixed, $f(x,t)=xe^{-xt}\to0$ as $x\to0^+$: the integrand converges pointwise to the zero function. So

$$
\lim_{x\to0^+}F(x)=1\ne0=\int_0^{+\infty}\Bigl(\lim_{x\to0^+}f(x,t)\Bigr)dt .
$$

**The implication that fails is "pointwise convergence of the integrand $\Rightarrow$ continuity of $F$".** The reason is visible: the bump $xe^{-xt}$ flattens in height ($x\to0$) but spreads in width (its scale is $1/x$), so that its area stays equal to $1$. It is the same mechanism as the counterexample $e^{-t/n}/n$ of lesson 07: the mass does not vanish, it **runs off to infinity**.

So a hypothesis is needed that prevents it.

**Definition 2 (domination hypothesis).** A <Def id="hypothese-de-domination">**domination hypothesis**</Def> on a set $A$ of parameters is the data of a function $g$, **integrable on $I$ and independent of $x$**, such that

$$
|f(x,t)|\le g(t)\qquad\text{for every }x\in A\text{ and almost every }t\in I .
$$

The important words are **independent of $x$**: it is the same word "uniform" as throughout arc 2, and the same rule as in the toolbox — a useful bound no longer contains the variable.

**Theorem 1 (continuity under the integral sign).** Assume that

1. for each $x\in A$, the function $t\mapsto f(x,t)$ is piecewise continuous and integrable on $I$;
2. for almost every $t\in I$, the function $x\mapsto f(x,t)$ is continuous on $A$;
3. there is a domination $|f(x,t)|\le g(t)$ in the sense of Definition 2.

Then $F$ is **continuous** on $A$.

<Proof title="reduce continuity to sequences, then dominate">

**Idea.** We can do nothing directly with a limit that is continuous in $x$. We replace it by sequences — continuity is tested sequence by sequence — and we then obtain a sequence of functions of $t$, to which a convergence theorem applies.

Let $x_0\in A$ and let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in $A$ tending to $x_0$. Set $\varphi_n(t)=f(x_n,t)$.

- By hypothesis 2, $\varphi_n(t)\to f(x_0,t)$ for almost every $t$: **pointwise** convergence in $t$.
- By hypothesis 3, $|\varphi_n(t)|\le g(t)$ for every $n$, with $g$ integrable and independent of $n$.

The **dominated convergence theorem** — which we take for granted here, since its proof belongs to measure theory — gives exactly, under these two conditions,

$$
\int_I\varphi_n(t)\,dt\longrightarrow\int_I f(x_0,t)\,dt,
\qquad\text{that is}\qquad F(x_n)\to F(x_0).
$$

Since this holds for **every** sequence $x_n\to x_0$, the function $F$ is continuous at $x_0$.

**What the proof teaches.** Domination enters at a single place, and it is the only place where Counterexample 1 fails: there, no integrable $g$ bounds $xe^{-xt}$ for all $x>0$, since one would need $g(t)\ge\sup_{x>0}xe^{-xt}=1/(et)$, which is not integrable at $+\infty$. The hypothesis is not a schoolroom precaution: it is precisely what forbids the mass to escape. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (the comfortable case).** If $I=[a,b]$ is a **segment** and $f$ is continuous on $A\times[a,b]$ with $A$ compact, the domination is **automatic**: a continuous function on a compact set is bounded, say by $M$, and the constant $g=M$ is integrable on a segment. It then suffices to say so — but it must be said.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If no domination can be found, nothing is concluded: perhaps $F$ is continuous anyway, perhaps not. The right reaction is to **restrict** $A$, exactly as we restricted the domain in lesson 05. For $\Gamma$, we shall not dominate on the whole of $(0,+\infty)$, but on each $[a,b]\subset(0,+\infty)$.

### Mini-exercises

1. Why must the domination be independent of $x$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because the dominated convergence theorem requires a bound common to the whole sequence $\varphi_n$. A bound $|f(x,t)|\le g_x(t)$ depending on $x$ provides no common bound, exactly as a bound still containing the variable proves nothing about uniformity (lesson 05).

   </Collapsible>

2. On $A=[1,2]$ and $I=[0,1]$, with $f$ continuous on $A\times I$, must a domination be sought?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No, but it must be **justified in one sentence**: $f$ is continuous on the compact set $A\times I$, hence bounded by some $M$, and the constant $M$ is integrable on the segment $[0,1]$. The domination is available, but it must appear in the write-up.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. Differentiating under the integral sign

Problem: a derivative is a difference quotient, hence again a limit. The pattern of section 2 will repeat — but this time the domination must bear on the **partial derivative**, not on $f$.

**Definition 3 (differentiation under the integral sign).** <Def id="derivation-sous-le-signe-integral">**Differentiation under the integral sign**</Def> is the equality

$$
F'(x)=\int_I\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,t)\,dt .
$$

**Theorem 2 (class $C^1$).** Assume that

1. for each $x$, $t\mapsto f(x,t)$ is integrable on $I$;
2. $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ exists and, for almost every $t$, is continuous in $x$;
3. there exists $h$ **integrable and independent of $x$** with $\left|\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,t)\right|\le h(t)$.

Then $F$ is of class $C^1$ and the equality of Definition 3 holds.

<Proof title="the difference quotient becomes a derivative at an unknown point">

**Idea.** The difference quotient of $F$ is the integral of a difference quotient. The mean value theorem turns it into a partial derivative evaluated at an **unknown** point — and it is precisely because that point is unknown that the bound must hold for **all** $x$.

Fix $x$ and let $(u_n)$ be a sequence of non-zero reals tending to $0$. Set

$$
\psi_n(t)=\frac{f(x+u_n,t)-f(x,t)}{u_n}.
$$

Then $\dfrac{F(x+u_n)-F(x)}{u_n}=\displaystyle\int_I\psi_n(t)\,dt$ by linearity.

- By hypothesis 2, $\psi_n(t)\to\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,t)$ for almost every $t$.
- The mean value theorem, applied to $s\mapsto f(s,t)$ between $x$ and $x+u_n$, gives a point $c_{n,t}$ **between** $x$ and $x+u_n$ such that

$$
\psi_n(t)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(c_{n,t},t),
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
|\psi_n(t)|\le h(t)
$$

by hypothesis 3 — and this is where independence of $x$ is indispensable, since we do not know where $c_{n,t}$ lies.

The dominated convergence theorem applies to $(\psi_n)$ and gives

$$
\frac{F(x+u_n)-F(x)}{u_n}\longrightarrow\int_I\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x,t)\,dt .
$$

Since this holds for every sequence $u_n\to0$, $F$ is differentiable at $x$ with the stated derivative. That derivative is itself continuous by Theorem 1 applied to $\partial f/\partial x$, so $F$ is of class $C^1$.

**What the proof teaches.** A domination on $f$ is of no use here: it is $\partial f/\partial x$ that must be bounded. This is the same shift of object as in lesson 07, where $\sum u_n'$ had to be controlled rather than $\sum u_n$. The mean value theorem is what forces the bound to be uniform in $x$. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** A domination on $f$ alone gives the continuity of $F$ and **nothing more**. Conversely, dominating $\partial f/\partial x$ without $F$ existing at any point defines no function. As in lesson 07, the two hypotheses bear on different objects and do not replace each other.

**Example 2 (differentiating Gamma).** On a segment $[a,b]\subset(0,+\infty)$ we have $\partial_x\bigl(t^{x-1}e^{-t}\bigr)=t^{x-1}\ln t\,e^{-t}$, and for $x\in[a,b]$

$$
\bigl|t^{x-1}\ln t\,e^{-t}\bigr|\le\bigl(t^{a-1}+t^{b-1}\bigr)|\ln t|\,e^{-t}=h(t),
$$

separating the cases $t\le1$ and $t\ge1$. This $h$ is integrable on $(0,+\infty)$ and does not depend on $x$. Theorem 2 therefore gives, for every $x>0$,

$$
\Gamma'(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}t^{\,x-1}\ln t\ e^{-t}\,dt .
$$

Note the manoeuvre: we do not dominate on $(0,+\infty)$, but on each segment — which suffices, differentiability being a local property.

### Mini-exercises

1. To apply Theorem 2 on $A=(0,+\infty)$, what does one do in practice?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   One works on an arbitrary segment $[a,b]$ of $(0,+\infty)$, where domination is achievable, then concludes that $F$ is $C^1$ on $[a,b]$; since $[a,b]$ is arbitrary and differentiability is local, $F$ is $C^1$ on $(0,+\infty)$.

   </Collapsible>

2. Where exactly does the mean value theorem intervene in the proof of Theorem 2?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   It turns the difference quotient $\psi_n(t)$ into a partial derivative evaluated at an unknown intermediate point. That is what allows the bound $h$ to be applied — and it is also what forces $h$ not to depend on $x$.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. Writing it up: the domination passport

**Method 2 (the passport).** Four lines, always in this order:

1. **Existence** — with $x$ fixed, $t\mapsto f(x,t)$ is piecewise continuous and integrable on $I$; say why.
2. **Regularity in $x$** — $x\mapsto f(x,t)$ is continuous, or $\partial_xf$ exists and is continuous in $x$.
3. **Domination** — exhibit $g$ (or $h$), check that it is integrable **and independent of $x$**, stating on which set of parameters.
4. **Theorem named and conclusion** — "by the theorem on continuity (or differentiation) under the integral sign, $F$ is continuous (or $C^1$) on … and $F'(x)=\dots$".

The shortcut worth knowing: **on a segment, with $f$ and $\partial_xf$ continuous, the domination is automatic by continuity on a compact set.** It must still be written down — one line is enough.

## Worked example — computing an integral by differentiating it

Let us compute, for $x\in\mathbb R$,

$$
F(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t^2}\cos(xt)\,dt .
$$

**Step 1 — check existence, then dominate.** With $x$ fixed, $|e^{-t^2}\cos(xt)|\le e^{-t^2}$, which is integrable on $[0,+\infty)$. The same bound, independent of $x$, is the domination of Theorem 1: $F$ is defined and continuous on $\mathbb R$.

*Why?* Existence and continuity are handled at once here, because the same bound $e^{-t^2}$ serves both — this is the favourable case where $x$ appears only inside a bounded cosine.

**Step 2 — dominate the partial derivative.** We have $\partial_x\bigl(e^{-t^2}\cos(xt)\bigr)=-t\,e^{-t^2}\sin(xt)$, so

$$
\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Bigl(e^{-t^2}\cos(xt)\Bigr)\right|\le t\,e^{-t^2}=h(t),
$$

integrable on $[0,+\infty)$ and independent of $x$. Theorem 2 gives $F\in C^1(\mathbb R)$ and $F'(x)=-\int_0^{+\infty}t\,e^{-t^2}\sin(xt)\,dt$.

*Why?* This is hypothesis 3 of Theorem 2, and it is that hypothesis — not the one on $f$ — which authorises writing $F'$.

**Step 3 — integrate by parts to make $F$ reappear.** With $u=\sin(xt)$ and $v'=t\,e^{-t^2}$, hence $v=-\tfrac12e^{-t^2}$:

$$
F'(x)=-\left(\Bigl[-\tfrac12e^{-t^2}\sin(xt)\Bigr]_0^{+\infty}+\frac x2\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t^2}\cos(xt)\,dt\right)=-\frac x2\,F(x),
$$

the bracket vanishing at both endpoints.

*Why?* The goal is not to compute $F'$ but to relate it to $F$: a differential equation is easier to solve than an integral.

**Step 4 — solve and fix the constant.** The equation $F'=-xF/2$ gives $F(x)=F(0)\,e^{-x^2/4}$. Now $F(0)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t^2}dt=\sqrt\pi/2$, the Gaussian integral. Hence

$$
F(x)=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\,e^{-x^{2}/4}.
$$

*Why?* A first-order differential equation leaves one constant free; a particular value is needed to fix it, and $x=0$ is the choice that simplifies the integrand most.

<Collapsible title="Your turn — answer">

Justify and then compute $\displaystyle G(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}-e^{-xt}}{t}\,dt$ for $x>0$.

Existence: near $0$ the numerator is equivalent to $(x-1)t$, so the integrand is bounded; near $+\infty$ both exponentials are integrable. On a segment $[a,b]\subset(0,+\infty)$ the partial derivative equals $e^{-xt}$, dominated by $e^{-at}$, integrable and independent of $x$. Theorem 2 gives

$$
G'(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-xt}\,dt=\frac1x .
$$

So $G(x)=\ln x+C$, and $G(1)=0$ forces $C=0$: $G(x)=\ln x$. This is Frullani's integral, obtained without ever finding an antiderivative of the original integrand.

</Collapsible>

## Guided visualisation

No widget is needed here: it is a **table of values** that makes the phenomenon visible. Take Counterexample 1 again, $F(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}xe^{-xt}\,dt$, and look at where the area sits.

| $x$ | height at $t=0$ | characteristic width | total area | $f(x,1)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1$ | $1$ | $1$ | $1$ | $0{,}368$ |
| $0{,}1$ | $0{,}1$ | $10$ | $1$ | $0{,}090$ |
| $0{,}01$ | $0{,}01$ | $100$ | $1$ | $0{,}0099$ |
| $0{,}001$ | $0{,}001$ | $1000$ | $1$ | $0{,}000999$ |

**Cycle 1 — every point dies out, the area does not move.**

- **Predict.** Reading the last column, the integrand at a fixed point visibly tends to $0$. Should the area do the same?
- **Act.** Run down the "total area" column and compare it with the $f(x,1)$ column.
- **Observe.** The integrand vanishes at each fixed $t$, but the area stays exactly $1$: height and width compensate each other.
- **Connect.** This is Counterexample 1 and the failure of "pointwise convergence $\Rightarrow$ continuity of $F$". A finite table proves nothing, but it shows where to look: the mass moves off towards large $t$.

**Cycle 2 — hunting for the bound that does not exist.**

- **Predict.** To apply Theorem 1 we would need $g(t)\ge f(x,t)$ for **all** $x>0$. With $t$ fixed, what is the largest value taken by $xe^{-xt}$ as $x$ varies?
- **Act.** Differentiate $x\mapsto xe^{-xt}$ with $t$ fixed; the maximum is at $x=1/t$ and equals $1/(et)$.
- **Observe.** The smallest possible bound is therefore $g(t)=1/(et)$, whose integral diverges at $+\infty$.
- **Connect.** The domination hypothesis is not merely absent: it is **impossible**. The theorem declines because the situation forbids it, not out of excessive caution.

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Applying a regularity theorem before establishing existence.** If the integral diverges for some $x$, $F$ is not defined and there is nothing to differentiate. The study of the domain always comes first.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**A domination that still depends on $x$.** This is the same mistake as the bound containing the variable in lesson 05. Write the bound, then re-read it: is there still an $x$ inside?

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Dominating $f$ when you want to differentiate.** Theorem 2 requires a domination of $\partial_x f$. Dominating $f$ gives only continuity.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Trying to dominate on the whole open interval.** For $\Gamma$ on $(0,+\infty)$, no global bound works. We dominate on each segment and conclude locally — differentiability is a local property.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting to justify the easy case.** On a segment with a continuous integrand the domination is automatic, but it must appear in one line on the paper. An examiner cannot guess that you know it.

</Pitfall>

<GuidingAnswer>

$F$ does not automatically inherit the regularity of $f$, because its definition contains a hidden limit: an integral over an unbounded interval is already a limit. Letting $x$ vary adds a second limit, and lesson 07 showed that two limits do not commute for free.

What is missing is neither continuity nor differentiability — the integrand of Counterexample 1 is analytic in everything — but a control that prevents the **mass from escaping**. Domination plays that role and no other: an integrable function $g$, fixed once and for all, under which the whole family must fit. When no $g$ exists, as for $xe^{-xt}$ whose best bound is the non-integrable $1/(et)$, the conclusion is genuinely false.

Finally, the domination bears on the object you want to bring out of the integral: $f$ for continuity, $\partial_xf$ for the derivative. This is the same shift as in lesson 07, where the series of derivatives was controlled rather than the series itself — and the reason is identical, a mean value theorem that evaluates the derivative at a point you do not know.

</GuidingAnswer>

## Summary — three questions, one hypothesis to find

<KeyResults title="The decision sheet">

| Question | Hypotheses to write | Conclusion | Inconclusive case |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| is $F$ defined? | with $x$ fixed, integrability on $I$, compared with the reference scales | domain of definition | says nothing about regularity |
| is $F$ continuous? | $x\mapsto f(x,t)$ continuous, domination of $f$ by an integrable $g$ | $F$ continuous | without domination: no verdict |
| is $F$ of class $C^1$? | $\partial_xf$ continuous in $x$, domination of $\partial_xf$ by an integrable $h$ | $F$ of class $C^1$, derivative under the integral | dominating $f$ alone is not enough |
| $I$ a segment, $f$ continuous, $A$ compact | continuity on a compact set | domination automatic | it must still be written |
| no global domination | restrict to a segment of parameters | local conclusion, then glue | never conclude globally in one step |

**Proofs worth being able to reconstruct.** Replacing a continuous limit by sequences so as to apply dominated convergence; turning a difference quotient into a partial derivative by the mean value theorem, which forces the bound to be uniform; and the mechanism of the counterexample, where height and width compensate exactly.

**The idea to take away.** Domination is the "integral" version of the rule you have been applying since lesson 05: **a bound is useful only if it no longer contains the parameter**. Here it must in addition be integrable, because it is what stops the mass from running off to infinity.

</KeyResults>
