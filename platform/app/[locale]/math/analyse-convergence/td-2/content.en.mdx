<ExerciseView id="math-an-td2-01">
<Statement>

For each of the following series with positive terms, determine its behaviour
by applying the <Terme id="regle-de-d-alembert">ratio test</Terme>.
The quotient calculation and its limit must be shown.

1. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{n!}{n^n}$ ;
2. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(n!)^2}{(2n)!}$ ;
3. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{2^n n!}{n^n}$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>In each quotient, write $(n+1)!=(n+1)n!$ before simplifying.</Hint>
<Hint>For the second series, expand only $(2n+2)!=(2n+2)(2n+1)(2n)!$.</Hint>
<Hint>The first and third series involve $\left(\dfrac{n}{n+1}\right)^n\to e^{-1}$; only a factor $2$ distinguishes them.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

All three series converge: the limits of the ratios are respectively $e^{-1}$, $1/4$ and $2/e$, all strictly less than $1$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

All three general terms are strictly positive. The ratio test therefore
applies as soon as the limit of the quotient has been calculated.

**1. First series.** Let $a_n=n!/n^n$. Then

$$
\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}
=\frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1)^{n+1}}\frac{n^n}{n!}
=\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n
\longrightarrow e^{-1}<1.
$$

By the ratio test, $\sum a_n$ converges.

**2. Second series.** Let $b_n=(n!)^2/(2n)!$. We obtain

$$
\frac{b_{n+1}}{b_n}
=\frac{(n+1)^2}{(2n+2)(2n+1)}
=\frac{n+1}{2(2n+1)}
\longrightarrow\frac14<1.
$$

The ratio test gives the convergence of $\sum b_n$.

**3. Third series.** Let $c_n=2^n n!/n^n$. Then

$$
\frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n}
=2\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n
\longrightarrow\frac2e<1.
$$

The ratio test therefore also gives the convergence of $\sum c_n$. The
factor $2^n$ slows the decay compared with the first series, but the limit
of the ratio remains strictly below $1$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Replacing $\left(\dfrac{n}{n+1}\right)^n$ by a limit equal to $1$.** The base tends to $1$, but the exponent varies: the limit is $e^{-1}$.
- **Writing $(2n+2)!=(2n+2)(2n)!$.** The factor $2n+1$ is missing; this omission changes the limit in the second question.
- **Concluding before comparing the limit with $1$.** The theorem requires the explicit verdict “limit strictly less than $1$, hence convergence.”

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td2-02">
<Statement>

Determine the behaviour of the following three series using the
<Terme id="regle-de-cauchy">root test</Terme>:

1. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n^2}$ ;
2. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{\ln n}{n}\right)^n$ ;
3. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}\left(1-\frac1n\right)^{n^2}$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>The $n$-th root of $A_n^{\,n^2}$ is $A_n^n$, not $A_n^{n^2}$.</Hint>
<Hint>For the first series, write $\dfrac{n}{n+1}=1-\dfrac1{n+1}$.</Hint>
<Hint>The useful limits are $\left(1-\dfrac1n\right)^n\to e^{-1}$ and $\dfrac{\ln n}{n}\to0$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

All three series converge. The $n$-th roots tend respectively to $e^{-1}$, $0$ and $e^{-1}$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

All the terms are positive at the indices under consideration. For each
general term, we calculate its $n$-th root.

**1. First series.** With $a_n=\left(n/(n+1)\right)^{n^2}$,

$$
\sqrt[n]{a_n}
=\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n
=\left(1-\frac1{n+1}\right)^n
\longrightarrow e^{-1}<1.
$$

By the root test, $\sum a_n$ converges.

**2. Second series.** With $b_n=(\ln n/n)^n$,

$$
\sqrt[n]{b_n}=\frac{\ln n}{n}\longrightarrow0<1.
$$

The root test gives the convergence of $\sum b_n$.

**3. Third series.** With $c_n=(1-1/n)^{n^2}$,

$$
\sqrt[n]{c_n}=\left(1-\frac1n\right)^n
\longrightarrow e^{-1}<1.
$$

The root test gives the convergence of $\sum c_n$. In all three cases,
the cited result is indeed the root test: a limit strictly less than $1$
forces the convergence of the series with positive terms.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Taking the square root instead of the $n$-th root.** The root test concerns $\sqrt[n]{a_n}$.
- **Removing the exponent $n$ after taking the root.** Since $n^2/n=n$, a power $n$ remains in the first and third questions.
- **Saying only that the root is less than $1$ for every $n$.** Its limit must be calculated and compared strictly with $1$.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td2-03">
<Statement>

In this exercise, $a\in\mathbb R$ is fixed.

1. For $v_n=\dfrac{n!\,e^n}{n^{n+a}}$, verify that the
   <Terme id="regle-de-d-alembert">ratio test</Terme> leads to an
   <Terme id="cas-douteux">inconclusive case</Terme>, then use Stirling's formula to determine, according to $a$, the behaviour of
   $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}v_n$.
2. For $w_n=\left(1-\dfrac an\right)^n$, defined from some sufficiently
   large index onwards, show that $w_{n+1}/w_n\to1$. Explain why the ratio
   gives no conclusion, then determine the behaviour of $\sum w_n$ using logarithms and a
   <Terme id="developpement-limite">Taylor expansion</Terme>.

</Statement>

<Collapsible title="Given formula — Stirling">

You may use without proof, as $n\to+\infty$,

$$
n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac ne\right)^n.
$$

</Collapsible>

<HintLadder>
<Hint>For $v_n$, the quotient simplifies to $e\left(\dfrac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n+a}$.</Hint>
<Hint>After Stirling, $v_n$ is equivalent to a constant times $n^{1/2-a}$: reduce the problem to a Riemann series.</Hint>
<Hint>For $w_n$, use $\ln(1-x)=-x-\dfrac{x^2}{2}+O(x^3)$ with $x=a/n$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

$\sum v_n$ converges if and only if $a>3/2$. For every $a\in\mathbb R$, $w_n\to e^{-a}\ne0$, so $\sum w_n$ diverges; in both questions, the ratio tends to $1$ and is inconclusive.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Compensated factorial.** The terms $v_n$ are positive and

$$
\frac{v_{n+1}}{v_n}
=e\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n+a}
\longrightarrow e\,e^{-1}=1.
$$

The ratio test is therefore inconclusive: a limit equal to $1$ proves
neither convergence nor divergence. Stirling's formula then gives

$$
v_n
=\frac{n!e^n}{n^{n+a}}
\sim\frac{\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^n e^n}{n^{n+a}}
=\sqrt{2\pi}\,n^{1/2-a}
=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{n^{a-1/2}}.
$$

Since the terms are positive, equivalence transfers the behaviour. The
reference series is the <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme>
$\sum 1/n^p$, which converges if and only if $p>1$. Here $p=a-1/2$; hence,

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}v_n\text{ converge}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a-\frac12>1
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad a>\frac32.
$$

At the threshold $a=3/2$, the equivalent is $\sqrt{2\pi}/n$: this is
exactly the divergent harmonic case.

**2. A power whose term does not vanish.** For every fixed $a$,
$1-a/n>0$ from some index onwards. We may therefore take the logarithm. The
Taylor expansion gives

$$
\ln w_n
=n\ln\left(1-\frac an\right)
=-a-\frac{a^2}{2n}+O\!\left(\frac1{n^2}\right).
$$

In particular, $\ln w_n\to-a$, so $w_n\to e^{-a}$. To verify the requested
inconclusive case, we write

$$
\ln\left(\frac{w_{n+1}}{w_n}\right)
=\ln w_{n+1}-\ln w_n\longrightarrow0,
$$

hence $w_{n+1}/w_n\to1$. The ratio test therefore gives no conclusion. On
the other hand, the preceding calculation shows that $w_n\to e^{-a}>0$: the
general term does not tend to $0$. By the necessary condition for a series
to converge, $\sum w_n$ diverges for every $a\in\mathbb R$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Declaring divergence as soon as the ratio tends to $1$.** This is an inconclusive case: the series $\sum 1/n$ and $\sum 1/n^2$ both have a ratio that tends to $1$, but different behaviours.
- **Losing the factor $\sqrt n$ in Stirling's formula.** It produces the exponent $1/2-a$ and sets the threshold at $a=3/2$, not at $a=1$.
- **Looking for a sophisticated test for $\sum w_n$.** Once $w_n\to e^{-a}\ne0$ has been established, the necessary condition on the general term is enough to conclude divergence.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td2-04">
<Statement>

For each of the following <Terme id="serie-alternee">alternating series</Terme>,
study convergence, state whether it is absolute, then bound the remainder
$R_N=\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}a_n$:

1. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt n}$ ;
2. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}(-1)^n\frac{\ln n}{n}$ ;
3. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n+(-1)^n}$.

For the third series, verify explicitly that the term magnitudes fail to
decrease: the <Terme id="critere-de-leibniz">Leibniz test</Terme>
does not apply directly. Handle it by expanding the general term.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>For the first two series, check separately positivity, decrease from some index onwards and the amplitude's zero limit.</Hint>
<Hint>The function $x\mapsto\ln(x)/x$ is decreasing for $x>e$.</Hint>
<Hint>In the third series, expand $\dfrac1{1+x}$ to order $2$ with $x=(-1)^n/n$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

All three series converge but not absolutely: they are conditionally convergent. For the first two, $|R_N|\le1/\sqrt{N+1}$ and, if $N\ge3$, $|R_N|\le\ln(N+1)/(N+1)$. For the third, the Leibniz test fails, but a decomposition notably gives $|R_N|\le1/(N+1)+1/N+1/N^2$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Amplitude $1/\sqrt n$.** Let $b_n=1/\sqrt n$. The sequence $(b_n)$ is
positive, decreasing and tends to $0$. The Leibniz test therefore gives
convergence and the remainder bound

$$
|R_N|\le b_{N+1}=\frac1{\sqrt{N+1}}.
$$

On the other hand, $\sum |a_n|=\sum 1/n^{1/2}$ is a divergent Riemann series,
because its exponent is $1/2\le1$. The original series is therefore
<Terme id="semi-convergence">conditionally convergent</Terme>.

**2. Amplitude $\ln n/n$.** Let $b_n=\ln n/n$. It is positive for
$n\ge2$, tends to $0$, and

$$
\left(\frac{\ln x}{x}\right)'=\frac{1-\ln x}{x^2}<0
\qquad\text{pour }x>e.
$$

Thus $(b_n)$ decreases from index $3$ onwards. The Leibniz test gives
convergence and, for $N\ge3$,

$$
|R_N|\le b_{N+1}=\frac{\ln(N+1)}{N+1}.
$$

The series of absolute values diverges, because $\ln n/n\ge1/n$ for $n\ge3$
and the harmonic series diverges. The alternating series is therefore
conditionally convergent.

**3. A sawtooth alternation.** The magnitude is

$$
\frac1{n+(-1)^n}.
$$

For $n=2k$, it is $1/(2k+1)$, whereas at the next index it is
$1/(2k)$: the magnitude increases between indices $2k$ and $2k+1$. The
decrease required by the Leibniz test fails.

Use the exact identity $1/(1+x)=1-x+x^2/(1+x)$ with
$x=(-1)^n/n$. For $n\ge2$,

$$
\frac{(-1)^n}{n+(-1)^n}
=\frac{(-1)^n}{n}-\frac1{n^2}+r_n,
\qquad
r_n=\frac{(-1)^n}{n^3\left(1+(-1)^n/n\right)}.
$$

Since $1+(-1)^n/n\ge1/2$, we have $|r_n|\le2/n^3$. The series formed by the
first part converges by the Leibniz test; the series $\sum 1/n^2$ and $\sum r_n$
converge absolutely by comparison with the Riemann series of exponents
$2$ and $3$. The series under study therefore converges.

For $N\ge2$, the same decomposition and the remainder bounds give

$$
|R_N|
\le\frac1{N+1}
+\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2}
+2\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^3}
\le\frac1{N+1}+\frac1N+\frac1{N^2}.
$$

Finally, the magnitude of the general term is equivalent to $1/n$; its series
diverges like the harmonic series. This third series is also conditionally
convergent.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Applying the Leibniz test to the third series because the signs alternate.** Alternation is not enough: the magnitudes increase at every transition from an even index to an odd index.
- **Claiming that $\ln n/n$ decreases from $n=2$ onwards.** It still increases from $2$ to $3$; a decrease from index $3$ onwards had to be established, which is sufficient for the behaviour and subsequent remainders.
- **Expanding the third series while forgetting the term $-1/n^2$.** It is a term of constant sign; isolating it explains why a simple Leibniz argument cannot be invoked for the original term.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<Statement>

For $n\ge2$, let

$$
u_n=\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt n}.
$$

1. Determine the behaviour of $\sum u_n$.
2. Study <Terme id="convergence-absolue">absolute convergence</Terme>, that is, determine the behaviour of $\sum |u_n|$.
3. Determine the behaviour of the perturbed series

$$
\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}\frac{u_n}{1+u_n}.
$$

The conclusion must rely on an exact decomposition and then on the behaviour
of each of the two parts.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>For $\sum u_n$, check the three hypotheses of the Leibniz test; for $\sum |u_n|$, name the resulting Riemann series.</Hint>
<Hint>Subtract $u_n$ from $\dfrac{u_n}{1+u_n}$ and simplify the difference exactly.</Hint>
<Hint>The correction $\dfrac{u_n^2}{1+u_n}$ is positive and equivalent to $u_n^2=1/n$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

$\sum u_n$ converges by the Leibniz test but $\sum |u_n|$ diverges: there is conditional convergence. In contrast, $\sum u_n/(1+u_n)$ diverges and its partial sums tend to $-\infty$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Original series.** Write $u_n=(-1)^n b_n$ with $b_n=1/\sqrt n$. The
sequence $(b_n)$ is positive, decreasing and tends to $0$. The Leibniz test
therefore gives the convergence of $\sum u_n$.

**2. Series of absolute values.** We have

$$
|u_n|=\frac1{\sqrt n}=\frac1{n^{1/2}}.
$$

The reference series is the Riemann series with exponent $1/2\le1$; it
diverges. Thus $\sum u_n$ converges, but not absolutely: it is
<Terme id="semi-convergence">conditionally convergent</Terme>.

**3. Perturbation.** For $n\ge2$, $|u_n|\le1/\sqrt2<1$, so $1+u_n>0$.
The fraction is well defined and the correction below is positive. The key
identity is

$$
\frac{u_n}{1+u_n}
=u_n-\frac{u_n^2}{1+u_n}.
$$

Let $v_n=u_n^2/(1+u_n)$. Since $u_n\to0$,

$$
v_n>0,
\qquad
v_n\sim u_n^2=\frac1n.
$$

The series $\sum v_n$ diverges to $+\infty$ by equivalence with the harmonic
series. To conclude without formally manipulating two infinite sums, return
to the partial sums:

$$
U_N=\sum_{n=2}^{N}u_n,
\qquad
V_N=\sum_{n=2}^{N}v_n,
\qquad
W_N=\sum_{n=2}^{N}\frac{u_n}{1+u_n}.
$$

The exact identity gives $W_N=U_N-V_N$. Now $(U_N)$ tends to a finite limit,
whereas $V_N\to+\infty$. Consequently,

$$
W_N\longrightarrow-\infty.
$$

The perturbed series diverges to $-\infty$. The quadratic term, invisible
in the signed equivalent $u_n/(1+u_n)\sim u_n$, accumulates like the harmonic
series.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Writing $u_n/(1+u_n)\sim u_n$, then transferring convergence.** Equivalence preserves behaviour for positive terms under its hypotheses; here the signs and their cancellation are decisive. The exact identity had to be used.
- **Forgetting the minus sign before the correction.** The positive part $u_n^2/(1+u_n)$ is subtracted; that is why the partial sums tend to $-\infty$.
- **Starting at index $1$.** We would have $u_1=-1$ and $1+u_1=0$; the perturbed series must start at index $2$.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td2-06">
<Statement>

Let $\theta\in\mathbb R$ not be a multiple of $2\pi$.

1. Show that the partial sums of $\sin(n\theta)$ are bounded.
2. Using <Terme id="transformation-d-abel">Abel summation</Terme>, show that

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\sin(n\theta)}n
$$

converges. Give a bound for a tail of the series.
3. Compare with $\sum |\sin(n\theta)|/n$: is convergence absolute
   for all allowed values of $\theta$?

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>Take the imaginary part of the geometric sum $\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{in\theta}$.</Hint>
<Hint>In Abel summation, sum the factor $\sin(n\theta)$ and move the difference onto $1/n$.</Hint>
<Hint>For absolute convergence, use $|\sin x|\ge\sin^2 x$ and $\sin^2 x=(1-\cos(2x))/2$; treat the case $\theta\in\pi\mathbb Z$ separately.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

The signed series converges for every $\theta\notin2\pi\mathbb Z$. It is identically zero, hence absolutely convergent, if $\theta\in(2\mathbb Z+1)\pi$; for every other allowed value, the series of absolute values diverges and convergence is only conditional.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Trigonometric partial sums.** Let

$$
S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sin(n\theta).
$$

The complex geometric sum, followed by taking its imaginary part, gives

$$
S_N
=\frac{\sin(N\theta/2)\sin((N+1)\theta/2)}{\sin(\theta/2)}.
$$

Since $\theta\notin2\pi\mathbb Z$, the denominator is non-zero. Thus

$$
|S_N|\le M_\theta,
\qquad
M_\theta=\frac1{|\sin(\theta/2)|},
$$

independently of $N$.

**2. Abel summation.** Fix $q\ge p\ge1$ and let
$A_n^{(p)}=\sum_{k=p}^{n}\sin(k\theta)$. Since
$A_n^{(p)}=S_n-S_{p-1}$, we have $|A_n^{(p)}|\le2M_\theta$. Abel summation
with $b_n=1/n$ gives

$$
\sum_{n=p}^{q}\frac{\sin(n\theta)}n
=\frac{A_q^{(p)}}q
+\sum_{n=p}^{q-1}A_n^{(p)}\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}\right).
$$

The sequence $(1/n)$ is positive, decreasing and tends to $0$. Consequently,

$$
\left|\sum_{n=p}^{q}\frac{\sin(n\theta)}n\right|
\le2M_\theta\left[
\frac1q+\sum_{n=p}^{q-1}\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}\right)
\right]
=\frac{2M_\theta}{p}.
$$

This bound tends to $0$ as $p\to+\infty$, uniformly in $q\ge p$.
The Cauchy criterion for series therefore gives the convergence of
$\sum\sin(n\theta)/n$.

**3. Absolute values.** An exceptional case must be distinguished. If
$\theta\in(2\mathbb Z+1)\pi$, then $\sin(n\theta)=0$ for every $n$: the series
is zero and converges absolutely.

Now suppose $\theta\notin\pi\mathbb Z$. Then $2\theta$ is not a
multiple of $2\pi$, and the partial sums of $\cos(2n\theta)$ are bounded.
The same Abel theorem shows that $\sum\cos(2n\theta)/n$ converges. But

$$
|\sin(n\theta)|\ge\sin^2(n\theta)
=\frac{1-\cos(2n\theta)}2.
$$

Thus,

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\sin^2(n\theta)}n
=\frac12\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac1n
-\frac12\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\cos(2n\theta)}n
\longrightarrow+\infty,
$$

because the first series is the divergent harmonic series and the second
converges. By comparison, $\sum|\sin(n\theta)|/n$ diverges. For
$\theta\notin\pi\mathbb Z$, the signed series is therefore conditionally
convergent.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Invoking the Leibniz test because the sine sometimes changes sign.** The signs of $\sin(n\theta)$ do not generally follow a regular alternation; Abel summation uses the bounded partial sums, which are the right information.
- **Saying that the partial sums are bounded because each sine is bounded.** A sum of bounded terms can grow like $N$; the trigonometric sum had to be calculated to obtain a bound independent of $N$.
- **Announcing absolute divergence without treating $\theta=\pi$ modulo $2\pi$.** For these values allowed by the statement, all the sines vanish and the series converges absolutely.

</CommonErrors>
