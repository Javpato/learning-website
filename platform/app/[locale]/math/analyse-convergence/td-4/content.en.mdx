<ExerciseView id="math-an-td4-01">
<Statement>

For $n\ge1$, let $u_n(x)=e^{-n x}/n^2$.

1. Show that the <Terme id="serie-de-fonctions">series of functions</Terme> $\sum_{n\ge1}u_n$ converges <Terme id="convergence-normale">normally</Terme> on $[0,+\infty)$.
2. Calculate $u_n'$. Let $a>0$: show that the series $\sum_{n\ge1}u_n'$ converges normally on $[a,+\infty)$.
3. Study its normal convergence on $]0,+\infty[$. Explain why the results obtained for each $a>0$ do not automatically fit together.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>On $[0,+\infty)$, the factor $e^{-n x}$ is maximal at the endpoint $x=0$.</Hint>
<Hint>On $[a,+\infty)$, replace $e^{-n x}$ with $e^{-n a}$, then recognize a geometric series.</Hint>
<Hint>On the open interval $]0,+\infty[$, the supremum of $e^{-n x}$ is still $1$, even though it is not attained.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

$\sum u_n$ converges normally on $[0,+\infty)$; $u_n'(x)=-e^{-n x}/n$ and $\sum u_n'$ converges normally on every $[a,+\infty)$, but not on $]0,+\infty[$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Series of functions.** On $[0,+\infty)$, we have $0<e^{-n x}\le1$, with equality at $x=0$. Thus

$$
\|u_n\|_\infty=\sup_{x\ge0}\frac{e^{-n x}}{n^2}=\frac1{n^2}.
$$

The series $\sum 1/n^2$ is a <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> with exponent $2$, so it converges. By the definition of normal convergence — or by the <Terme id="critere-de-weierstrass">Weierstrass M-test</Terme> with $M_n=1/n^2$ — the series $\sum u_n$ converges normally on $[0,+\infty)$, hence uniformly and pointwise on this domain.

**2. Series of derivatives away from the endpoint.** We calculate

$$
u_n'(x)=-\frac{e^{-n x}}n.
$$

For $x\ge a>0$,

$$
|u_n'(x)|=\frac{e^{-n x}}n\le\frac{e^{-n a}}n\le e^{-n a}.
$$

The series $\sum e^{-n a}$ is a <Terme id="serie-geometrique">geometric series</Terme> with ratio $e^{-a}\in,]0,1[$: it converges. The Weierstrass M-test therefore gives the normal convergence of $\sum u_n'$ on $[a,+\infty)$.

**3. On the open interval.** For every $n$,

$$
\|u_n'\|_{\infty,\,]0,+\infty[}
=\sup_{x>0}\frac{e^{-n x}}n
=\frac1n.
$$

The supremum is not attained, but it is indeed $1/n$ because $e^{-n x}\to1$ as $x\to0^+$. Yet the <Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme> $\sum1/n$ diverges. The series of derivatives therefore does not converge normally on $]0,+\infty[$.

The majorant obtained on $[a,+\infty)$ depends on $a$ and deteriorates as $a\to0^+$. Having normal convergence on every domain that remains a positive distance from the endpoint does not provide a convergent series of norms on their union.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Writing that the supremum on $]0,+\infty[$ is less than $1/n$ because it is not attained.** A supremum need not be a maximum: here the values get as close as desired to $1/n$.
- **Using $\sum e^{-n a}/n$ without specifying that $a>0$.** For $a=0$, this majorant becomes the harmonic series again; the distance from the endpoint is the decisive datum.
- **Deducing “not uniform” from “not normal” without another proof.** The converse from normal to uniform is false; here the calculation of the norms concludes only about normal convergence.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td4-02">
<Statement>

On $\mathbb R$, consider

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n+x^2}.
$$

1. Show that the series converges pointwise.
2. By applying the <Terme id="critere-de-leibniz">Leibniz test</Terme> to the remainder, show that the convergence is <Terme id="convergence-uniforme">uniform</Terme> on $\mathbb R$.
3. Show that it does not converge <Terme id="convergence-normale">normally</Terme>. How does this example prevent reversing the implication “normal implies uniform”?

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>For fixed $x$, study the positive sequence $b_n(x)=1/(n+x^2)$.</Hint>
<Hint>The Leibniz test gives more than convergence: the remainder is bounded by the first omitted term.</Hint>
<Hint>For normal convergence, the absolute value removes the alternation; calculate the supremum over $x$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

The remainder satisfies $\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}|S(x)-S_N(x)|\le1/(N+1)\to0$: the series converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$, but not normally because the series of its norms is harmonic.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

Let $b_n(x)=1/(n+x^2)$ and denote by $S_N$ the partial sum of order $N$.

**1. Pointwise convergence.** For each fixed $x\in\mathbb R$, the sequence $(b_n(x))$ is positive, decreasing in $n$, and tends to $0$. The Leibniz test therefore applies to the alternating series $\sum(-1)^n b_n(x)$: it converges at every point, hence pointwise on $\mathbb R$.

**2. Uniform convergence.** Above all, the same test provides an estimate of the <Terme id="reste-d-une-serie">remainder</Terme>: for every $x\in\mathbb R$,

$$
|S(x)-S_N(x)|\le b_{N+1}(x)=\frac1{N+1+x^2}\le\frac1{N+1}.
$$

The last majorant does not depend on $x$ and tends to $0$. By the <Terme id="norme-uniforme">uniform norm</Terme> criterion, $S_N\to S$ uniformly on $\mathbb R$.

**3. Failure of normal convergence.** For the term $v_n(x)=(-1)^n/(n+x^2)$,

$$
\|v_n\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}\frac1{n+x^2}=\frac1n,
$$

with the maximum attained at $x=0$. The series of norms is therefore the <Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme> $\sum1/n$, which diverges. By definition, $\sum v_n$ does not converge normally.

This example satisfies the hypothesis “uniform convergence” while the conclusion “normal convergence” fails. It therefore refutes exactly the converse; only the implication from normal to uniform is always true.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Applying the Leibniz test pointwise and then claiming uniform convergence.** Convergence at every point is not enough; the remainder bound had to be retained and its supremum taken.
- **Keeping the sign $(-1)^n$ in the norm.** The absolute value removes the alternation, hence $\|v_n\|_\infty=1/n$ and harmonic divergence.
- **Only saying that the majorant $1/n$ diverges.** Here $1/n$ is not a rough bound: it is the exact norm. This equality is what proves the failure of normal convergence.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td4-03">
<Statement>

Let

$$
S(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n+x^2},\qquad x\in\mathbb R.
$$

1. Justify the <Terme id="continuite">continuity</Terme> of $S$ on $\mathbb R$.
2. Show that the series of derivatives converges normally on $\mathbb R$.
3. Justify the <Terme id="derivation-terme-a-terme">term-by-term differentiation</Terme> with a complete checklist, then give $S'(x)$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>For continuity, reuse the uniform remainder bound obtained from the Leibniz test.</Hint>
<Hint>For the derivative, maximize $h_n(x)=2x/(n+x^2)^2$ on $[0,+\infty)$.</Hint>
<Hint>The differentiation checklist requires three hypotheses: class $\mathcal C^1$, convergence at one point, and uniform convergence of the series of derivatives.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

$S$ is continuous and differentiable on $\mathbb R$, with $S'(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}2(-1)^{n+1}x/(n+x^2)^2$; the series of derivatives converges normally because its norm at order $n$ is $3\sqrt3/(8n^{3/2})$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

Let $u_n(x)=(-1)^n/(n+x^2)$.

**1. Continuity checklist.**

1. **Hypotheses listed.** Each $u_n$ must be continuous on $\mathbb R$ and the series $\sum u_n$ must converge uniformly there.
2. **Hypotheses verified.** The denominator $n+x^2$ does not vanish, so each $u_n$ is continuous; the <Terme id="critere-de-leibniz">Leibniz test</Terme> gives $|S(x)-S_N(x)|\le1/(N+1+x^2)\le1/(N+1)$, so the convergence is uniform.
3. **Named theorem.** We apply the theorem on the continuity of the sum of a uniformly convergent series of continuous functions.
4. **Conclusion.** The function $S$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$.

**2. Controlling the series of derivatives.** We calculate

$$
u_n'(x)=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}x}{(n+x^2)^2}.
$$

The function $h_n(x)=2x/(n+x^2)^2$ on $[0,+\infty)$ satisfies

$$
h_n'(x)=\frac{2(n-3x^2)}{(n+x^2)^3}.
$$

It therefore attains its maximum at $x=\sqrt{n/3}$, hence

$$
\|u_n'\|_\infty
=\frac{2\sqrt{n/3}}{(n+n/3)^2}
=\frac{3\sqrt3}{8n^{3/2}}.
$$

The series $\sum n^{-3/2}$ is a <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> with exponent $3/2$, so it converges. Thus the series $\sum u_n'$ converges <Terme id="convergence-normale">normally</Terme>, hence uniformly, on $\mathbb R$.

**3. Differentiation checklist.** Fix $A>0$ and work on the interval $[-A,A]$.

1. **Hypotheses listed.** The $u_n$ must be of class $\mathcal C^1$ on $[-A,A]$, the series $\sum u_n(0)$ must converge, and the series $\sum u_n'$ must converge uniformly on this interval.
2. **Hypotheses verified.** The $u_n$ are of class $\mathcal C^1$; $\sum u_n(0)=\sum(-1)^n/n$ converges by the Leibniz test; finally, the normal bound from question 2 gives uniform convergence of the derivatives, even on all of $\mathbb R$.
3. **Named theorem.** We apply the theorem on term-by-term differentiation of a series of functions on a closed interval.
4. **Conclusion.** The sum $S$ is differentiable on $[-A,A]$ and

$$
S'(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}x}{(n+x^2)^2}.
$$

Since $A>0$ is arbitrary, this formula holds on all of $\mathbb R$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Differentiating because the original series converges uniformly.** This control transfers continuity, not differentiability; the series of derivatives had to be controlled uniformly.
- **Bounding $|u_n'(x)|$ by $2|x|/n^2$ on $\mathbb R$.** This bound depends on $x$ and becomes arbitrarily large; the function had to be maximized over $x$.
- **Forgetting convergence at one point in the checklist.** Derivatives determine functions only up to an additive constant; here $x_0=0$ provides the convergent alternating series $\sum(-1)^n/n$.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td4-04">
<Statement>

Justify the <Terme id="integration-terme-a-terme">term-by-term integration</Terme> in the identity

$$
\int_0^1\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n^2}\,dx
=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2(n+1)},
$$

then calculate the numerical sum on the right-hand side by <Terme id="decomposition-en-elements-simples">partial fraction decomposition</Terme>.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>On $[0,1]$, bound $x^n/n^2$ by a term of a convergent Riemann series.</Hint>
<Hint>Write the four lines of the checklist before moving the integral sign.</Hint>
<Hint>Look for a decomposition of the form $A/n+B/n^2+C/(n+1)$, then sum up to order $N$ before taking the limit.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

The exchange is allowed by uniform convergence on $[0,1]$, and $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}1/[n^2(n+1)]=\pi^2/6-1$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

Let $u_n(x)=x^n/n^2$ on $[0,1]$.

**1. Term-by-term integration checklist.**

1. **Hypotheses listed.** Each $u_n$ must be continuous on $[0,1]$ and the series $\sum u_n$ must converge uniformly there.
2. **Hypotheses verified.** The $u_n$ are polynomials and $|u_n(x)|\le1/n^2$ on $[0,1]$; the <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> $\sum1/n^2$ converges, so the <Terme id="critere-de-weierstrass">Weierstrass M-test</Terme> gives <Terme id="convergence-normale">normal convergence</Terme>, then <Terme id="convergence-uniforme">uniform convergence</Terme>.
3. **Named theorem.** We apply the theorem on the integration of a uniformly convergent series of continuous functions on a closed interval.
4. **Conclusion.** The exchange is valid and

$$
\int_0^1\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n^2}\,dx
=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\int_0^1\frac{x^n}{n^2}\,dx
=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2(n+1)}.
$$

**2. Calculating the sum.** The partial fraction decomposition is

$$
\frac1{n^2(n+1)}=\frac1{n^2}-\frac1n+\frac1{n+1}.
$$

The partial sum of order $N$ is therefore

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac1{n^2(n+1)}
=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac1{n^2}-1+\frac1{N+1}.
$$

Since the Basel value $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}1/n^2=\pi^2/6$ is known, taking the limit gives

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2(n+1)}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}-1.
$$

The structure is complete: uniform control, named exchange theorem, calculation of the integrals, telescoping, then the reference value.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Directly moving $\sum$ outside the integral.** Immediate linearity applies only to a finite sum; the infinite exchange required the uniform convergence checklist.
- **Writing $1/[n^2(n+1)]=1/n^2-1/(n+1)$.** This decomposition is false; putting the terms over a common denominator checks the three coefficients.
- **Also telescoping $\sum1/n^2$.** Only the part $-1/n+1/(n+1)$ telescopes; the quadratic part gives the Basel value.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td4-05">
<Statement>

On $[0,1]$, define the <Terme id="serie-de-fonctions">series of functions</Terme>

$$
S(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n^2}.
$$

1. Show that $S$ is <Terme id="continuite">continuous</Terme> on $[0,1]$, in particular at $1$.
2. Let $0<a<1$. Justify the <Terme id="derivation-terme-a-terme">term-by-term differentiation</Terme> on $[0,a]$ and calculate $S'(x)$ as a series.
3. Identify $S'(x)$ for $0<x<1$, then show that $S'$ is unbounded as $x\to1^-$. Explain precisely why the endpoint had to be treated separately.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>For continuity up to the endpoint, the majorant $1/n^2$ works on the entire closed interval.</Hint>
<Hint>On $[0,a]$, the derivative of the term is $x^{n-1}/n$ and is bounded by $a^{n-1}$.</Hint>
<Hint>Integrate the geometric series between $0$ and $x$ to recognize $\sum x^n/n$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

$S$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ with $S(1)=\pi^2/6$; for $0<x<1$, $S'(x)=-\ln(1-x)/x$, so $S'(x)\to+\infty$ as $x\to1^-$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

Let $u_n(x)=x^n/n^2$.

**1. The closed interval: continuity checklist.**

1. **Hypotheses listed.** Each $u_n$ must be continuous on $[0,1]$ and the series $\sum u_n$ must converge uniformly there.
2. **Hypotheses verified.** The $u_n$ are polynomials and $\|u_n\|_\infty=1/n^2$; the <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> with exponent $2$ converges, so the series of functions converges <Terme id="convergence-normale">normally</Terme>, then uniformly.
3. **Named theorem.** We apply the theorem on the continuity of the sum of a uniformly convergent series of continuous functions.
4. **Conclusion.** The function $S$ is continuous on $[0,1]$; in particular

$$
\lim_{x\to1^-}S(x)=S(1)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6},
$$

where the last equality is the Basel value.

**2. The interior: differentiation checklist.** Fix $a\in,]0,1[$.

1. **Hypotheses listed.** The $u_n$ must be of class $\mathcal C^1$ on $[0,a]$, the series $\sum u_n(0)$ must converge, and the series $\sum u_n'$ must converge uniformly on $[0,a]$.
2. **Hypotheses verified.** The $u_n$ are polynomials, $u_n(0)=0$, and

$$
\|u_n'\|_{\infty,[0,a]}=\sup_{0\le x\le a}\frac{x^{n-1}}n
=\frac{a^{n-1}}n\le a^{n-1}.
$$

The <Terme id="serie-geometrique">geometric series</Terme> $\sum a^{n-1}$ converges; the Weierstrass M-test therefore gives uniform convergence of the series of derivatives.
3. **Named theorem.** We apply the theorem on term-by-term differentiation on a closed interval.
4. **Conclusion.** The sum $S$ is differentiable on $[0,a]$ and

$$
S'(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{n-1}}n.
$$

Since every $x<1$ belongs to such an interval, this formula holds throughout the interior $[0,1[$.

**3. Identifying the slope: checklist for integrating the geometric series.** Fix again $a\in,]0,1[$.

1. **Hypotheses listed.** The functions $t\mapsto t^k$ must be continuous on $[0,a]$ and their series must converge uniformly there.
2. **Hypotheses verified.** They are polynomials and $|t^k|\le a^k$; the geometric series $\sum a^k$ converges, so the Weierstrass M-test gives uniform convergence of $\sum t^k$ to $1/(1-t)$.
3. **Named theorem.** We apply the theorem on term-by-term integration of a uniformly convergent series on a closed interval.
4. **Conclusion.** For $0\le x\le a$,

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{k+1}}{k+1}
=\int_0^x\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}t^k\,dt
=\int_0^x\frac{dt}{1-t}
=-\ln(1-x).
$$

After changing the index $n=k+1$, for $0<x<1$ we obtain

$$
S'(x)=\frac1x\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n}
=-\frac{\ln(1-x)}x.
$$

As $x\to1^-$, we have $-\ln(1-x)\to+\infty$ and $x\to1$, so

$$
S'(x)\xrightarrow[x\to1^-]{}+\infty.
$$

The slope is unbounded near the endpoint. This behavior corresponds to the <Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme> $\sum u_n'(1)=\sum1/n$, which diverges. The differentiation checklist is therefore valid on every interior interval $[0,a]$, but not on $[0,1]$; continuity at the endpoint, however, remains guaranteed by the summable series $\sum1/n^2$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Confusing the value and the slope at the endpoint.** The convergence of $\sum1/n^2$ makes $S(1)$ finite and $S$ continuous; it does not control the series of derivatives $\sum1/n$.
- **Applying the differentiation checklist directly on $[0,1]$.** At the endpoint, $\|u_n'\|_\infty=1/n$ and the harmonic series diverges; the work first had to be done on $[0,a]$ with $a<1$.
- **Concluding that $S'$ blows up only because the checklist fails at $1$.** The failure of a theorem proves nothing by itself; it is the formula $S'(x)=-\ln(1-x)/x$ that establishes the blow-up.

</CommonErrors>
