<Statement>

Consider the <Terme id="integrale-a-parametre">parameter-dependent integral</Terme>

$$
F(x)=\int_0^1\frac{e^{xt}}{1+t}\,dt,
\qquad x\in\mathbb R.
$$

1. Show that $F$ is of class $\mathcal C^1$ on $\mathbb R$. At each point considered, explicitly give dominating functions for the integrand and its derivative that are independent of the parameter on a fixed neighbourhood.
2. Study the monotonicity of $F$ on $\mathbb R$.
3. Calculate $F(0)$ and $F'(0)$, then give the first-order expansion of $F$ at $0$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>Around a point $x_0$, restrict $x$ to $[x_0-1,x_0+1]$ and set $A=|x_0|+1$.</Hint>
<Hint>On this interval, bound $e^{xt}/(1+t)$ and $t e^{xt}/(1+t)$ separately by functions of $t$ that contain $A$, but no longer contain $x$.</Hint>
<Hint>Once you have obtained $F'(x)$, its sign is visible under the integral; at $0$, use $t/(1+t)=1-1/(1+t)$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

$F$ is of class $\mathcal C^1$ and strictly increasing on $\mathbb R$;

$$
F(0)=\ln2,\qquad F'(0)=1-\ln2,\qquad
F(x)=\ln2+(1-\ln2)x+o(x).
$$

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Existence and class $\mathcal C^1$.** Fix $x_0\in\mathbb R$ and work on the interval $I=[x_0-1,x_0+1]$. Set $A=|x_0|+1$. For $x\in I$ and $t\in[0,1]$, we have $|x|\le A$, so

$$
\left|\frac{e^{xt}}{1+t}\right|
\le \frac{e^{At}}{1+t}=g_0(t),
\qquad
\left|\partial_x\!\left(\frac{e^{xt}}{1+t}\right)\right|
=\frac{t e^{xt}}{1+t}
\le \frac{t e^{At}}{1+t}=g_1(t).
$$

The functions $g_0$ and $g_1$ are continuous, and therefore integrable, on $[0,1]$; they depend on the fixed point $x_0$ through $A$, but they are **independent of the variable parameter $x\in I$**. In particular, the first bound establishes the absolute existence of $F(x)$ on $I$.

Here is the checklist for <Terme id="derivation-sous-le-signe-integral">differentiating under the integral sign</Terme>.

- **Prior existence:** $F(x)$ exists on $I$ thanks to the explicit dominating function $g_0(t)=e^{At}/(1+t)$.
- **Differentiability with respect to $x$:** for every $t\in[0,1]$, the function $x\mapsto e^{xt}/(1+t)$ is differentiable on $I$.
- **Independent domination:** the derivative is bounded by $g_1(t)=t e^{At}/(1+t)$, which does not contain the variable parameter $x$.
- **Integrability:** $g_1$ is continuous on the segment $[0,1]$, and therefore integrable.

**Theorem invoked:** the theorem on differentiation under the integral sign.

**Conclusion:** $F$ is differentiable on $I$ and

$$
F'(x)=\int_0^1\frac{t e^{xt}}{1+t}\,dt.
$$

For each $t$, the integrand of $F'$ is continuous in $x$ and remains bounded on $I$ by the same integrable function $g_1$, independent of $x$. The theorem on continuity under the integral sign therefore shows explicitly that $F'$ is continuous on $I$. Since $x_0$ was arbitrary, $F$ is of class $\mathcal C^1$ on $\mathbb R$.

**2. Monotonicity.** For every $x\in\mathbb R$, the integrand $t e^{xt}/(1+t)$ is non-negative on $[0,1]$ and strictly positive on $]0,1]$. Thus

$$
F'(x)>0.
$$

The function $F$ is therefore strictly increasing on $\mathbb R$.

**3. First order at $0$.** We calculate

$$
F(0)=\int_0^1\frac{dt}{1+t}=\ln2
$$

and, thanks to the differentiation already justified,

$$
F'(0)=\int_0^1\frac{t}{1+t}\,dt
=\int_0^1\left(1-\frac1{1+t}\right)dt
=1-\ln2.
$$

By the first-order <Terme id="formule-de-taylor-young">Taylor–Young formula</Terme>, which applies because $F$ is differentiable at $0$,

$$
F(x)=F(0)+xF'(0)+o(x)
=\ln2+(1-\ln2)x+o(x)
\qquad(x\to0).
$$

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Writing $e^{xt}\le e^x$ for every $x$.** This inequality reverses when $x<0$, and the purported bound still contains the parameter; on an interval around $x_0$, you had to use $e^{At}$ with $A=|x_0|+1$.
- **Bounding the integrand, then differentiating without a new control.** Existence uses $g_0$; the differentiation theorem requires the distinct dominating function $g_1(t)=t e^{At}/(1+t)$ for the derivative.
- **Concluding only that $F'(x)\ge0$.** The integrand is strictly positive on a set of positive length, so the integral is strictly positive and $F$ is strictly increasing.

</CommonErrors>

<Statement>

For $x\in\mathbb R$, set

$$
G(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}\cos(xt)\,dt.
$$

1. Justify the existence of $G(x)$ with an explicit <Terme id="hypothese-de-domination">domination hypothesis</Terme> independent of $x$.
2. Justify <Terme id="derivation-sous-le-signe-integral">differentiating under the integral sign</Terme> and give $G'(x)$.
3. By introducing an auxiliary integral involving sine, perform two integrations by parts and show that $(1+x^2)G(x)=1$.
4. Deduce $G(x)$, then check that the derivative of the resulting formula agrees with the one given by the theorem.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>The inequalities $|\cos(xt)|\le1$ and $|\sin(xt)|\le1$ provide an envelope that is valid simultaneously for every real $x$.</Hint>
<Hint>After differentiating with respect to $x$, the additional factor is $t$: check that $t e^{-t}$ is integrable.</Hint>
<Hint>Set $S(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}\sin(xt)\,dt$. One integration by parts gives $G=1-xS$, and the other gives $S=xG$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

With the dominating functions $e^{-t}$ and $t e^{-t}$,

$$
G(x)=\frac1{1+x^2},\qquad
G'(x)=-\int_0^{+\infty}t e^{-t}\sin(xt)\,dt
=-\frac{2x}{(1+x^2)^2}.
$$

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Existence.** For every $x\in\mathbb R$, the function $t\mapsto e^{-t}\cos(xt)$ is continuous on $[0,+\infty)$ and

$$
|e^{-t}\cos(xt)|\le g_0(t)=e^{-t},
\qquad
\int_0^{+\infty}g_0(t)\,dt=1.
$$

The dominating function $g_0$ is integrable and independent of the parameter $x$. By the existence theorem using domination, $G(x)$ exists absolutely for every $x\in\mathbb R$.

**2. Differentiation.** First calculate

$$
\partial_x\bigl(e^{-t}\cos(xt)\bigr)=-t e^{-t}\sin(xt).
$$

The checklist is complete.

- **Prior existence:** it follows from the dominating function $g_0(t)=e^{-t}$ above.
- **Differentiability with respect to $x$:** for every $t\ge0$, the function $x\mapsto e^{-t}\cos(xt)$ is differentiable on $\mathbb R$.
- **Independent domination:** $|{-t e^{-t}\sin(xt)}|\le g_1(t)=t e^{-t}$, with no $x$ in $g_1$.
- **Integrability:** an integration by parts gives $\int_0^{+\infty}g_1(t)\,dt=1$.

**Theorem invoked:** the theorem on differentiation under the integral sign.

**Conclusion:** $G$ is differentiable on $\mathbb R$ and

$$
G'(x)=-\int_0^{+\infty}t e^{-t}\sin(xt)\,dt.
$$

**3. Two integrations by parts.** Introduce

$$
S(x)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}\sin(xt)\,dt.
$$

This integral also exists absolutely, because

$$
|e^{-t}\sin(xt)|\le e^{-t},
$$

an integrable dominating function independent of $x$. Apply the integration-by-parts theorem on the segment $[0,A]$. For cosine,

$$
\int_0^A e^{-t}\cos(xt)\,dt
=1-e^{-A}\cos(xA)-x\int_0^A e^{-t}\sin(xt)\,dt.
$$

Since $|e^{-A}\cos(xA)|\le e^{-A}\to0$ and both improper integrals exist thanks to the dominating function $e^{-t}$, taking $A\to+\infty$ gives

$$
G(x)=1-xS(x).
$$

For sine, the second integration by parts gives

$$
\int_0^A e^{-t}\sin(xt)\,dt
=-e^{-A}\sin(xA)+x\int_0^A e^{-t}\cos(xt)\,dt.
$$

The bound $|e^{-A}\sin(xA)|\le e^{-A}\to0$ and the same dominating function $e^{-t}$ allow us to take the limit:

$$
S(x)=xG(x).
$$

Substituting $S$ into the first relation,

$$
G(x)=1-x^2G(x),
\qquad
(1+x^2)G(x)=1.
$$

**4. Closed form and derivative check.** Since $1+x^2>0$ on $\mathbb R$,

$$
G(x)=\frac1{1+x^2}.
$$

The ordinary derivative of the right-hand side is

$$
G'(x)=-\frac{2x}{(1+x^2)^2}.
$$

But the theorem on differentiation under the integral sign has already established

$$
G'(x)=-\int_0^{+\infty}t e^{-t}\sin(xt)\,dt.
$$

The two expressions therefore agree; more precisely,

$$
\int_0^{+\infty}t e^{-t}\sin(xt)\,dt
=\frac{2x}{(1+x^2)^2}.
$$

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Using $e^{-t}$ to justify the derivative as well.** After differentiation, the factor $t$ appears; the correct dominating function is $t e^{-t}$, which is integrable and independent of $x$.
- **Integrating by parts directly between $0$ and $+\infty$.** The theorem first applies on $[0,A]$; you must then let $A$ tend to infinity and control the boundary terms by $e^{-A}$.
- **Dividing a relation by $x$.** That would lose the case $x=0$; the relations $G=1-xS$ and $S=xG$ lead to $(1+x^2)G=1$ without any division by $x$.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td6-03">
<Statement>

For $x>-1$, consider

$$
H(x)=\int_0^1\frac{t^x-1}{\ln t}\,dt.
$$

1. Show that this <Terme id="integrale-a-parametre">parameter-dependent integral</Terme> is well defined. Study separately the apparent singularities as $t\to1$ and as $t\to0$.
2. Justify <Terme id="derivation-sous-le-signe-integral">differentiating under the integral sign</Terme> on $]-1,+\infty[$ and show that

$$
H'(x)=\frac1{x+1}.
$$

3. Deduce a closed form for $H(x)$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>For $0<t<1$, write the quotient as an integral in a new parameter: $(t^x-1)/\ln t=\int_0^x t^s\,ds$.</Hint>
<Hint>Around an $x_0>-1$, choose $-1<a\le x_0\le b$; every exponent encountered is then bounded below by $m=\min(a,0)>-1$.</Hint>
<Hint>The derivative of the integrand with respect to $x$ is $t^x$, dominated on $[a,b]$ by $t^a$; then use $H(0)=0$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

For $x>-1$, $H$ is well defined and of class $\mathcal C^1$, and

$$
H'(x)=\frac1{x+1},\qquad H(0)=0,\qquad H(x)=\ln(1+x).
$$

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Study of the two endpoints and existence.** For $0<t<1$, the fundamental theorem of calculus applied to $s\mapsto t^s$ gives the identity

$$
\frac{t^x-1}{\ln t}=\int_0^x t^s\,ds,
$$

where the integral on the right is oriented if $x<0$.

Near $t=1$, this identity directly gives

$$
\lim_{t\to1}\frac{t^x-1}{\ln t}
=\int_0^x1\,ds=x.
$$

The singularity at $1$ is therefore removable: extend the integrand by the value $x$.

Near $0$, integrability must be controlled, especially when $x<0$. Fix $x_0>-1$ and choose a segment $[a,b]\subset]-1,+\infty[$ containing $x_0$. Set

$$
m=\min(a,0)>-1,
\qquad
C=\max(|a|,|b|).
$$

For $x\in[a,b]$, the numbers $s$ lying between $0$ and $x$ satisfy $s\ge m$. Since $0<t<1$, we have $t^s\le t^m$, and then

$$
\left|\frac{t^x-1}{\ln t}\right|
=\left|\int_0^x t^s\,ds\right|
\le C t^m=g_0(t).
$$

The function $g_0(t)=C t^m$ is independent of the variable parameter $x\in[a,b]$ and

$$
\int_0^1g_0(t)\,dt=\frac{C}{m+1}<+\infty.
$$

The existence theorem using domination therefore shows that $H(x)$ exists absolutely on $[a,b]$. Since $x_0$ is arbitrary, $H$ is well defined for every $x>-1$. This same bound explicitly handles the only real difficulty at $0$: when $x<0$, the integrand may become unbounded, but it remains locally dominated by an integrable power with exponent strictly greater than $-1$.

**2. Differentiation and regularity.** For $0<t<1$,

$$
\partial_x\!\left(\frac{t^x-1}{\ln t}\right)=t^x.
$$

Return to the fixed segment $[a,b]\subset]-1,+\infty[$.

- **Prior existence:** it is guaranteed on $[a,b]$ by the dominating function $g_0(t)=C t^m$, independent of $x$.
- **Differentiability with respect to $x$:** for every $t\in]0,1[$, the quotient is differentiable with respect to $x$ and its derivative is $t^x$.
- **Independent domination:** since $x\ge a$ and $0<t<1$, $0<t^x\le g_1(t)=t^a$, with no $x$ in $g_1$.
- **Integrability:** $a>-1$ and $\int_0^1g_1(t)\,dt=1/(a+1)<+\infty$.

**Theorem invoked:** the theorem on differentiation under the integral sign.

**Conclusion:** $H$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$ and

$$
H'(x)=\int_0^1t^x\,dt
=\left[\frac{t^{x+1}}{x+1}\right]_0^1
=\frac1{x+1}.
$$

Furthermore, $x\mapsto t^x$ is continuous for every $t$, and the same integrable dominating function $g_1(t)=t^a$, independent of $x\in[a,b]$, allows us to apply the theorem on continuity under the integral sign to $H'$. Thus $H'$ is continuous on $[a,b]$. Since the segment is arbitrary, $H$ is of class $\mathcal C^1$ on $]-1,+\infty[$.

**3. Identification.** We have

$$
H(0)=\int_0^1 0\,dt=0.
$$

The function $x\mapsto\ln(1+x)$ has the same derivative $1/(1+x)$ on $]-1,+\infty[$ and also takes the value $0$ at $x=0$. By the theorem stating that two antiderivatives of the same function on an interval differ by a constant,

$$
H(x)=\ln(1+x)
\qquad(x>-1).
$$

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Saying only that the quotient tends to $x$ at $t=1$.** This removes the singularity at $1$, but does not address integrability at $0$ when $-1<x<0$; the local dominating function $C t^m$ with $m>-1$ was needed.
- **Bounding $t^x$ by $1$ for every $x>-1$.** This bound is false if $x<0$; on a segment $[a,b]$, the correct dominating function independent of $x$ is $t^a$.
- **Differentiating the numerator and denominator with respect to $t$.** The requested differentiation is with respect to the parameter $x$; it gives $t^x$, and only then does the theorem on differentiation under the integral sign allow it to be integrated.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<Statement>

We want to calculate

$$
I=\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}-e^{-2t}}{t}\,dt
$$

using <Terme id="methode-de-feynman">Feynman's trick</Terme>. Three parameterisations are proposed:

$$
F(a)=\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}-e^{-at}}{t}\,dt
\quad(1\le a\le2),
$$

$$
P(a)=\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-at}-e^{-2at}}{t}\,dt
\quad\left(\frac12\le a\le2\right),
$$

and


$$
Q(a)=\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}-e^{-2t}}{t+a}\,dt
\quad(a\ge0).
$$

1. For each candidate, identify the link with $I$, the available starting value and the form obtained after differentiation.
2. Justify every existence and every differentiation used with an integrable dominating function independent of the parameter on the interval considered.
3. Choose the genuinely useful parameterisation and calculate $I$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>A good parameterisation must combine three qualities: it must contain $I$, have a simple initial value and have a derivative that is easier than the original integral.</Hint>
<Hint>Use $(e^{-t}-e^{-at})/t=\int_1^a e^{-st}\,ds$ and $(e^{-at}-e^{-2at})/t=\int_a^{2a}e^{-st}\,ds$ to remove the singularity at $t=0$.</Hint>
<Hint>For $F$, the derivative of the integrand is $e^{-at}$, dominated by $e^{-t}$ on $1\le a\le2$; then $F(1)=0$ and $F(2)=I$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

The only parameterisation that provides both an elementary derivative and a known initial value is $F$; since $F'(a)=1/a$ and $F(1)=0$,

$$
I=F(2)=\int_1^2\frac{da}{a}=\ln2.
$$

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. First candidate: the parameter affects only one exponential.** For $1\le a\le2$,

$$
\frac{e^{-t}-e^{-at}}{t}=\int_1^a e^{-st}\,ds.
$$

Thus

$$
0\le\frac{e^{-t}-e^{-at}}{t}\le(a-1)e^{-t}\le g_F(t)=e^{-t}.
$$

The function $g_F$ is integrable on $[0,+\infty)$ and independent of $a$; the existence theorem using domination therefore establishes the absolute existence of $F(a)$ on $[1,2]$. In particular, the singularity at $t=0$ is removable.

Complete the differentiation checklist.

- **Prior existence:** it is guaranteed by $g_F(t)=e^{-t}$.
- **Differentiability with respect to $a$:** for every $t>0$, the derivative of $(e^{-t}-e^{-at})/t$ is $e^{-at}$.
- **Independent domination:** $0<e^{-at}\le e^{-t}$ for $1\le a\le2$.
- **Integrability:** $\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-t}\,dt=1$.

**Theorem invoked:** the theorem on differentiation under the integral sign.

**Conclusion:**

$$
F'(a)=\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-at}\,dt=\frac1a.
$$

This candidate also has both desired anchor points: $F(1)=0$ is known and $F(2)=I$.

**2. Second candidate: a scaling that preserves the unknown.** For $1/2\le a\le2$,

$$
\frac{e^{-at}-e^{-2at}}{t}=\int_a^{2a}e^{-st}\,ds
\le 2e^{-t/2}=g_P(t).
$$

The dominating function $g_P$ is integrable and independent of $a$; $P(a)$ therefore exists absolutely on this interval by the existence theorem using domination.

For differentiation:

- **Prior existence:** it follows from $g_P(t)=2e^{-t/2}$.
- **Differentiability with respect to $a$:** the derivative of the integrand is $-e^{-at}+2e^{-2at}$.
- **Independent domination:**

$$
|{-e^{-at}+2e^{-2at}}|
\le e^{-t/2}+2e^{-t}
\le g_{P,1}(t)=3e^{-t/2}.
$$

- **Integrability:** $\int_0^{+\infty}g_{P,1}(t)\,dt=6$.

**Theorem invoked:** the theorem on differentiation under the integral sign.

**Conclusion:**

$$
P'(a)=\int_0^{+\infty}\bigl(-e^{-at}+2e^{-2at}\bigr)dt
=-\frac1a+\frac1a=0.
$$

We recover the fact that $P$ is constant — the change of variable $u=at$ would show this as well. But $P(1)=I$, and no value of $P$ is known any more simply: differentiation has removed all information about the constant sought. This parameterisation is valid, but useless for calculating $I$.

**3. Third candidate: a correct anchor point, but a harder derivative.** For $t>0$,

$$
\frac{e^{-t}-e^{-2t}}{t}=\int_1^2e^{-st}\,ds\le e^{-t}.
$$

Since $0<t/(t+a)\le1$ for $a\ge0$,

$$
0\le\frac{e^{-t}-e^{-2t}}{t+a}
=\frac{e^{-t}-e^{-2t}}{t}\,\frac{t}{t+a}
\le g_Q(t)=e^{-t}.
$$

The dominating function $g_Q$ is integrable and independent of $a$; the existence theorem using domination gives the absolute existence of $Q(a)$ for every $a\ge0$. We do have $Q(0)=I$.

However, on an interval $a\ge a_0>0$, the differentiation checklist gives only:

- **Prior existence:** it is ensured by $g_Q(t)=e^{-t}$.
- **Differentiability with respect to $a$:** the formal derivative is $-(e^{-t}-e^{-2t})/(t+a)^2$.
- **Independent domination:** using the preceding identity,

$$
\left|\frac{e^{-t}-e^{-2t}}{(t+a)^2}\right|
\le \frac{e^{-t}}{a_0}=g_{Q,1}(t)
\qquad(a\ge a_0).
$$

- **Integrability:** $\int_0^{+\infty}g_{Q,1}(t)\,dt=1/a_0$.

**Theorem invoked:** the theorem on differentiation under the integral sign on $[a_0,+\infty[$.

**Conclusion:** for $a>0$,

$$
Q'(a)=-\int_0^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}-e^{-2t}}{(t+a)^2}\,dt,
$$

an integral that is no simpler than the original one. At the anchor point $a=0$, the differentiated integrand behaves like $1/t$ near $0$: no common integrable dominating function is available all the way to that point. This candidate does contain $I$, but it does not make its calculation easier.

**4. Choice and conclusion.** The first candidate is the only one that has both a known initial value and an elementary derivative. By the fundamental theorem of calculus,

$$
I=F(2)=F(1)+\int_1^2F'(a)\,da
=\int_1^2\frac{da}{a}
=\ln2.
$$

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Choosing $P$ because $P(1)=I$.** This link is not enough: $P'(a)=0$ does not determine the unknown constant, because there is no simple initial value.
- **Differentiating $F$ before dealing with the quotient by $t$.** The identity involving $\int_1^a e^{-st}\,ds$ first provides the dominating function $e^{-t}$ and removes the apparent singularity at $0$.
- **Keeping $Q$ solely because $Q(0)=I$.** Its derivative retains a more complicated integral and the differentiation checklist does not extend to $a=0$; the parameter must simplify the problem, not merely move it.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td6-05">
<Statement>

For $x>0$, set

$$
E(x)=\int_x^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t}\,dt.
$$

1. Justify the existence of $E(x)$ with an explicit dominating function independent of the parameter $x$ on every interval $[a,+\infty[$, where $a>0$ is fixed.
2. Perform an integration by parts and isolate the leading term $e^{-x}/x$.
3. Explicitly bound the remainder and deduce the <Terme id="equivalent">asymptotic equivalent</Terme> of $E(x)$ as $x\to+\infty$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>On a fixed interval $x\ge a>0$, extend the integrand by $0$ between $a$ and $x$; it is then bounded by $e^{-t}/a$ on $[a,+\infty[$.</Hint>
<Hint>In the integration by parts, take $u=1/t$ and $dv=e^{-t}dt$.</Hint>
<Hint>If $R(x)=\int_x^{+\infty}e^{-t}/t^2\,dt$, use $1/t^2\le1/x^2$ for $t\ge x$, then compare $R(x)$ with $e^{-x}/x$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

For $x>0$,

$$
E(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}x-R(x),\qquad
0\le R(x)\le\frac{e^{-x}}{x^2},\qquad
E(x)\sim\frac{e^{-x}}x
\quad(x\to+\infty).
$$

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Existence.** Fix $a>0$ and consider all parameters $x\in[a,+\infty[$. On the common domain $[a,+\infty[$, write

$$
f_x(t)=\mathbf 1_{[x,+\infty[}(t)\frac{e^{-t}}{t}.
$$

Then

$$
0\le f_x(t)\le g_a(t)=\frac{e^{-t}}a,
\qquad
\int_a^{+\infty}g_a(t)\,dt=\frac{e^{-a}}a<+\infty.
$$

The dominating function $g_a$ is integrable and independent of the variable parameter $x\in[a,+\infty[$. By the existence theorem using domination,

$$
E(x)=\int_a^{+\infty}f_x(t)\,dt
$$

exists absolutely for every $x\ge a$. Since every $x>0$ belongs to such an interval, $E$ is well defined on $]0,+\infty[$.

**2. Integration by parts.** Apply the integration-by-parts theorem on the segment $[x,A]$, with

$$
u(t)=\frac1t,\qquad dv(t)=e^{-t}dt,\qquad
du(t)=-\frac{dt}{t^2},\qquad v(t)=-e^{-t}.
$$

This gives

$$
\int_x^A\frac{e^{-t}}t\,dt
=\left[-\frac{e^{-t}}t\right]_x^A
-\int_x^A\frac{e^{-t}}{t^2}\,dt.
$$

The term $e^{-A}/A$ tends to $0$. Moreover, over the fixed family $x\ge a$, the improper remainder exists because, for $t\ge x$,

$$
0\le\frac{e^{-t}}{t^2}\le g_{a,2}(t)=\frac{e^{-t}}{a^2}.
$$

This dominating function is integrable and independent both of the parameter $x$ and of the upper bound $A$. Letting $A$ tend to $+\infty$,

$$
E(x)=\frac{e^{-x}}x-R(x),
\qquad
R(x)=\int_x^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-t}}{t^2}\,dt\ge0.
$$

**3. Remainder estimate and asymptotic equivalent.** For $t\ge x$,

$$
0\le\frac1{t^2}\le\frac1{x^2}.
$$

By comparison with the reference exponential integral,

$$
0\le R(x)
\le\frac1{x^2}\int_x^{+\infty}e^{-t}\,dt
=\frac{e^{-x}}{x^2}.
$$

After dividing by the positive leading term $e^{-x}/x$,

$$
0\le
\frac{\frac{e^{-x}}x-E(x)}{\frac{e^{-x}}x}
=\frac{R(x)}{e^{-x}/x}
\le\frac1x
\xrightarrow[x\to+\infty]{}0.
$$

By the squeeze theorem,

$$
\frac{E(x)}{e^{-x}/x}\longrightarrow1,
$$

and therefore

$$
E(x)\sim\frac{e^{-x}}x
\qquad(x\to+\infty).
$$

The identity even provides the beginning of an <Terme id="developpement-asymptotique">asymptotic expansion</Terme> with a relative remainder between $0$ and $1/x$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Writing $E(x)\approx e^{-x}/x$ immediately.** An integration by parts also produces the remainder $R(x)$; without its explicit bound, no asymptotic equivalence has been proved.
- **Losing the sign of the remainder.** The correct formula is $E(x)=e^{-x}/x-R(x)$ with $R(x)\ge0$; in particular, it gives $E(x)\le e^{-x}/x$.
- **Bounding $R(x)$ by a quantity of the same order without concluding.** The bound $R(x)\le e^{-x}/x^2$ is useful because its ratio to the leading term is $1/x\to0$.

</CommonErrors>
