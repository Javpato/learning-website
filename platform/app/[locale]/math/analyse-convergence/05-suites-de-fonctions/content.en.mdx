# Ensuring a small gap everywhere, not just at each point

<LessonMeta id="math-an-c05" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

A family of curves can approach a limiting curve at each of its points without ever approaching it as a whole: there is always some place, which moves, where the gap is large.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="The temperature profile of a pulse of heat along a rod, recorded at index n">

**Situation:** the end of a rod is heated briefly. At reading number $n$, the excess temperature at distance $x$ (in centimetres) from the end is $T_n(x)=n\,x\,e^{-n x}$, in kelvins.
**Question:** is there a reading number after which the entire rod has returned below $0{,}01$ K? And if we allow ourselves to ignore the first centimetre, from which reading onward?
**The obstacle:** at each fixed point, the recorded temperature eventually falls to zero—and yet the maximum of the profile does not change. The point-by-point reasoning learned in L1 does not distinguish between the two situations.
**The contract:** this chapter provides 3 tools (the pointwise limit, the measure of the maximal gap, and refutation using a moving point). By the end, we will answer both questions, with numbers to support the answers.

</Mission>

*Feeling confident? [Go straight to the TD (tutorial sheet)](../td-3/)—the theory will still be here when you get stuck.*

## The battle plan

To decide, we need three tools.

1. **Fix a point and examine the sequence of numbers**: obtain the curve towards which everything tends.
2. **Measure the maximal gap over the entire domain**: a single number for each index that summarises the worst case.
3. **Refute, then restrict**: construct a moving point that keeps the gap large, and see on which part of the domain the guarantee returns.

## 1. One point at a time: the pointwise limit

**Tool 1—obtain the limiting curve.** Before comparing two curves, we need to know which one is the target curve.

Problem: a sequence of functions is not a sequence of numbers, and “tending to” does not yet have a meaning for curves.
We therefore need a term for the most modest situation: the one in which we examine only one point at a time.

**Definition 1 (pointwise convergence).** Let $f_n:D\to\mathbb R$ be a sequence of functions. We say that it has <Def id="convergence-simple">**pointwise convergence**</Def> (each point taken separately behaves well) to $f$ on $D$ if, for each fixed $x$ in $D$, the sequence of numbers $f_n(x)$ tends to $f(x)$.

More rigorously:

$$
\forall x\in D,\ \forall\varepsilon>0,\ \exists N(x,\varepsilon),\ \forall n\ge N(x,\varepsilon),\quad |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon .
$$

The order of the symbols is everything: $N$ is written **after** $x$, so it is allowed to depend on it.

**Example (physics) 1.** For the heat profile $T_n(x)=n\,x\,e^{-nx}$:

- at $x=0$, we have $T_n(0)=0$ for every $n$;
- for fixed $x>0$, the exponential dominates the factor $n$, so $T_n(x)\to0$.

The limiting function is therefore the zero function: $T(x)=0$ on $[0,+\infty)$. Every point of the rod does eventually cool down.

**Remark (laboratory notation).** In a TP (laboratory session), you would instead write $T(x,t)$ with continuous time $t$, and $n$ would be the acquisition number. Same object, different clothing: what follows does not depend on the name of the index.

**Example 2.** On $[0,1]$, the sequence $f_n(x)=x^n$ converges pointwise to

$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}0,&0\le x<1,\\ 1,&x=1.\end{cases}
$$

Continuous functions can therefore have a discontinuous pointwise limit: pointwise convergence does not preserve continuity. Remember this pair; it serves as a counterexample everywhere.

## 2. A single number for each index: the maximal gap

**Tool 2—measure the worst case.** We want a guarantee that holds everywhere at once, hence a number that no longer depends on the point.

Problem: in Definition 1, the index $N$ depends on the point; a promise made point by point says nothing about the entire rod.
We therefore need a term for the situation in which the same index works for every point.

**Definition 2 (uniform convergence).** The sequence $f_n$ has <Def id="convergence-uniforme">**uniform convergence**</Def> (a single index works for the entire domain) to $f$ on $D$ if

$$
\forall\varepsilon>0,\ \exists N(\varepsilon),\ \forall n\ge N(\varepsilon),\ \forall x\in D,\quad |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon .
$$

Compare this with Definition 1: the symbols are the same; only their order changes. Here $N$ is written **before** $x$, so it can no longer depend on it.

Problem: this statement with four quantifiers is cumbersome to check directly.
We therefore need a number we can calculate that summarises it.

**Definition 3 (uniform norm).** For a bounded function $g$ on $D$, the <Def id="norme-uniforme">**uniform norm**</Def> (the largest possible gap over the domain) is

$$
\|g\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in D}|g(x)| .
$$

**Theorem 1 (norm criterion).** $f_n$ converges uniformly to $f$ on $D$ if and only if

$$
\|f_n-f\|_\infty\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0 .
$$

<Collapsible title="Proof—why the supremum summarises the four quantifiers">

Suppose that $\|f_n-f\|_\infty\to0$ and let $\varepsilon>0$. There exists $N$ such that $\|f_n-f\|_\infty<\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$. Then, for this same $N$ and for **every** $x$ in $D$,

$$
|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le\|f_n-f\|_\infty<\varepsilon,
$$

which is exactly Definition 2.

Conversely, if Definition 2 holds, fix $\varepsilon>0$ and take the $N$ it provides. For $n\ge N$, the number $\varepsilon$ is an upper bound for $|f_n(x)-f(x)|$ for every $x$: it is therefore an upper bound of the set of these values, and the supremum, the least upper bound, satisfies $\|f_n-f\|_\infty\le\varepsilon$. Thus $\|f_n-f\|_\infty\to0$. $\blacksquare$

Note the technical point: we obtain $\le\varepsilon$ and not $<\varepsilon$, which is sufficient for a limit.

</Collapsible>

**Method 1 (calculating a maximal gap).**

1. write the gap function $E_n(x)=|f_n(x)-f(x)|$;
2. differentiate it with respect to $x$, with $n$ fixed, to find its maximum point;
3. evaluate $E_n$ at that point: this is $\|f_n-f\|_\infty$;
4. if the exact calculation is too difficult, look for an upper bound $E_n(x)\le a_n$ where **$a_n$ does not depend on $x$**, and see whether $a_n\to0$.

**Example (physics) 3.** For the heat profile, $E_n(x)=T_n(x)=n x e^{-nx}$ because the limit is zero. Differentiate:

$$
T_n'(x)=n e^{-nx}(1-n x),
$$

which vanishes at $x=1/n$. The maximum is therefore

$$
\|T_n\|_\infty=T_n\!\left(\frac1n\right)=n\cdot\frac1n\cdot e^{-1}=\frac1e\approx0{,}368\ \text{K}.
$$

This number does not depend on $n$: it does not tend to $0$. By Theorem 1, there is no uniform convergence on $[0,+\infty)$, even though each point cools down (Example (physics) 1).

**Remark (inconclusive case).** An upper bound gives a conclusion only if it is independent of $x$. Writing $T_n(x)\le n x$ is true and completely useless: the upper bound depends on $x$ and grows with $n$. When you produce a bound, mentally put a box around it: does it still contain an $x$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Since $|\sin(nx)|\le1$ for every $x$, we have $|f_n(x)|\le 1/n$, and the value $1/n$ is attained at $x=\pi/(2n)$. Therefore $\|f_n\|_\infty=1/n\to0$: convergence is uniform over the whole of $\mathbb R$, by Theorem 1. Here the upper bound no longer contained $x$: that is what makes it useful.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. Refute with a moving point, then restrict the domain

**Tool 3—the point that follows the problem.** When the supremum is difficult to calculate, it is often enough to exhibit a single point, chosen as a function of the index, where the gap remains large.

Problem: showing that a supremum does not tend to $0$ requires us, in principle, to calculate it, which is not always possible.
We therefore need a procedure that gives a lower bound without a complete calculation.

**Definition 4 (witness sequence).** A <Def id="suite-temoin">**witness sequence**</Def> (a measuring point that moves with the index) is a sequence of points $x_n$ in $D$ such that $|f_n(x_n)-f(x_n)|$ does not tend to $0$.

**Proposition 1 (refutation by a witness).** If a witness sequence exists, convergence is not uniform on $D$.

**Justification.** By definition of the supremum, $\|f_n-f\|_\infty\ge|f_n(x_n)-f(x_n)|$. If the right-hand side does not tend to $0$, neither does the left-hand side, and Theorem 1 gives the conclusion. $\blacksquare$

**Example (physics) 4.** For the heat profile, take $x_n=1/n$, that is, the point that follows the maximum from reading to reading. Then $T_n(x_n)=1/e$ for every $n$: this is a witness sequence, and Proposition 1 recovers the verdict of Example (physics) 3 without calculating a derivative.

**Remark.** The witness is a **moving** point. No fixed point could work: convergence does occur at each fixed point (Example (physics) 1). The entire difficulty of the chapter lies in this difference between “for each point” and “for all points at once”.

Problem: these families whose maximum does not fall all have the same shape; we might as well give them a name and a framework for interpreting them.

**Definition 5 (travelling bump).** A <Def id="bosse-glissante">**travelling bump**</Def> (a peak that moves or narrows instead of flattening) is a sequence of functions whose graph has a peak whose position or width depends on $n$.

For a bump, three quantities behave independently, and each answers a different question:

| Quantity | What it determines | For the profile $T_n$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Value at a fixed point | pointwise convergence | tends to $0$ |
| Height of the peak | uniform convergence | equals $1/e$, constant |
| Area under the curve | limit of the integrals | equals $1/n$, tends to $0$ |

<Collapsible title="Calculating the area, to check the third row">

By integration by parts, with $u=x$ and $v'=n e^{-nx}$:

$$
\int_0^{+\infty} n x e^{-nx}\,dx=\Big[-x e^{-nx}\Big]_0^{+\infty}+\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-nx}\,dx=0+\frac1n=\frac1n .
$$

Constant height and collapsing area: the two do not measure the same thing. The chapter on exchange theorems will make use of precisely this difference.

</Collapsible>

**Method 2 (recovering the guarantee on part of the domain).**

1. locate the offending point, that is, the position of the peak;
2. remove a neighbourhood of that point, for example by working on $[a,+\infty)$ with $a>0$;
3. check that from some index onward, the peak has **left** the restricted domain;
4. on this domain, the function is then monotone: the supremum is attained at the boundary and can be calculated.

**Example (physics) 5.** On $[a,+\infty)$ with $a>0$: as soon as $1/n\le a$, that is, $n\ge1/a$, the function $T_n$ is decreasing on $[a,+\infty)$. Its supremum is therefore attained at $x=a$:

$$
\sup_{x\ge a}T_n(x)=n\,a\,e^{-na}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0 .
$$

Convergence is uniform on $[a,+\infty)$ for every $a>0$, but not on $[0,+\infty)$. The defect is entirely confined to a neighbourhood of the heated end.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** “Uniform on $[a,+\infty)$ for every $a>0$” does **not** imply “uniform on $(0,+\infty)$”: the required index depends on $a$ and blows up as $a$ tends to $0$. This is the chapter's most common error; we will encounter it again at the boundary of power series.

## Worked example—two families, two verdicts

**Example 6—refuting uniformity.** Let us study $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1]$.

**Step 1—obtain the limiting curve.** For $0\le x<1$, $x^n\to0$; for $x=1$, $x^n=1$. The pointwise limit $f$ is therefore $0$ on $[0,1)$ and $1$ at $1$.

*Why?* Definition 2 compares $f_n$ with $f$: without the limiting function, there is nothing to measure.

**Step 2—locate the sensitive area.** The gap $E_n(x)=x^n$ is small away from $1$ and equals $1$ at $1$: everything happens just to the left of $1$.

*Why?* Method 2 asks us to locate the offending point before any calculation, because it will determine the verdict.

**Step 3—construct a witness.** Set $x_n=2^{-1/n}\in[0,1)$. Then

$$
E_n(x_n)=\left(2^{-1/n}\right)^n=\frac12 .
$$

*Why?* Proposition 1 gives a conclusion as soon as a single moving point keeps the gap away from zero—there is no need to calculate the exact supremum.

**Step 4—conclude and localise.** There is no uniform convergence on $[0,1]$. On the other hand, on $[0,b]$ with $b<1$, we have $E_n(x)\le b^n$, an upper bound independent of $x$ that tends to $0$: convergence is uniform there.

*Why?* Point 4 of Method 1 allows us to conclude by an upper bound as soon as the bound no longer contains $x$; Method 2 explains why removing the neighbourhood of $1$ is sufficient.

**Example 7—proving uniformity.** Let us study $g_n(x)=\sqrt{x^2+\tfrac1{n^2}}$ on $\mathbb R$.

**Step 1—obtain the limiting curve.** For fixed $x$, $g_n(x)\to\sqrt{x^2}=|x|$.

*Why?* The same reason as in Example 6: Definition 1 provides the object of comparison.

**Step 2—write the gap in a usable form.** Multiply by the conjugate quantity:

$$
0\le g_n(x)-|x|=\frac{\left(x^2+\frac1{n^2}\right)-x^2}{\sqrt{x^2+\frac1{n^2}}+|x|}=\frac{1/n^2}{\sqrt{x^2+\frac1{n^2}}+|x|}.
$$

*Why?* A difference of two square roots cannot be bounded directly; the conjugate turns the gap into a quotient whose denominator we know how to bound below.

**Step 3—bound independently of $x$.** The denominator is at least $1/n$, so

$$
0\le g_n(x)-|x|\le\frac{1/n^2}{1/n}=\frac1n .
$$

*Why?* This is exactly point 4 of Method 1: a bound without $x$, hence usable over the entire domain at once.

**Step 4—conclude using the criterion.** The maximal gap is bounded above by $1/n$, which tends to $0$: convergence is uniform on $\mathbb R$ by Theorem 1. The maximum is in fact attained at $x=0$, where the gap is exactly $1/n$.

*Why?* Theorem 1 turns the upper bound into a verdict, and knowing the maximum point confirms that the bound $1/n$ is optimal.

**Example 8—your turn, with less help.** Let $h_n(x)=n^2 x e^{-n x}$ on $[0,+\infty)$, a bump whose height changes.

**Your turn:** calculate the pointwise limit, then the height of the peak, and then draw a conclusion.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

For fixed $x>0$, the exponential still dominates: $h_n(x)\to0$, and $h_n(0)=0$. The pointwise limit is zero.

The calculation from Example (physics) 3 carries over: the maximum is at $x=1/n$ and equals

$$
h_n\!\left(\frac1n\right)=n^2\cdot\frac1n\cdot e^{-1}=\frac ne\longrightarrow+\infty .
$$

Not only is there no uniform convergence, but the maximal gap explodes. The area is $1$ at every index, by the integration-by-parts calculation already carried out. The three rows of the bump table really are independent.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Understanding question—answer">

**Why must the witness sequence in Example 6 move with $n$, whereas an ordinary counterexample is a fixed object?**

Because the property to be refuted is itself moving. At each fixed point $x<1$, convergence does occur: no fixed point can therefore serve as a counterexample. What is false is the existence of an index that works simultaneously for every point; its negation reads “for every $n$, there is a point where the gap remains large”, and that point is allowed to depend on $n$. The witness sequence is nothing more than this point written as a function of the index.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**“For every $x$, the gap tends to $0$, so convergence is uniform.”** No: this is Definition 1, not Definition 2. The heat profile cools at each point yet retains a maximal gap of $1/e$. The question to ask yourself is: **does my index $N$ depend on $x$?**

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Using an upper bound that still contains $x$.** The inequality $T_n(x)\le n x$ is true and worthless. An upper bound is useful for Theorem 1 only if it is independent of the variable—that is the meaning of the word “uniform”.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Looking for a fixed witness.** A fixed point can never refute uniformity, since pointwise convergence occurs at each fixed point. The witness must depend on $n$: here $x_n=1/n$, there $x_n=2^{-1/n}$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Patching the pieces together.** “Uniform on $[a,+\infty)$ for every $a>0$” does not imply “uniform on $(0,+\infty)$”. Each $a$ comes with its own index, and these indices have no common bound. Always treat the interior and the boundary separately.

</Pitfall>

<MissionSolved>

**1. Find the target curve.** By Example (physics) 1, at every point $x$ of the rod, $T_n(x)\to0$: Definition 1 gives the zero limiting function. Every point does indeed cool down.

**2. Measure the worst case.** Example (physics) 3 gives the maximum of the profile:

$$
\|T_n\|_\infty=T_n\!\left(\frac1n\right)=\frac1e\approx0{,}368\ \text{K}\quad\text{pour tout }n .
$$

This number does not tend to $0$: by Theorem 1, **there is no reading after which the entire rod is below $0{,}01$ K**. The answer to the first question is no, and the witness $x_n=1/n$ from Example (physics) 4 tells us where the problem is hiding: at $1/n$ centimetres from the end, that is, closer and closer to the heated point.

**3. Ignore the first centimetre.** On $[1,+\infty)$, Method 2 applies with $a=1$: as soon as $n\ge1$, the peak lies outside the domain, the function is decreasing there, and

$$
\sup_{x\ge1}T_n(x)=n\,e^{-n}.
$$

We seek the first $n$ such that $n e^{-n}<0{,}01$:

$$
6\,e^{-6}\approx0{,}0149>0{,}01,\qquad 7\,e^{-7}\approx0{,}0064<0{,}01 .
$$

**From reading $n=7$ onward, the whole rod beyond $1$ cm is below $0{,}01$ K**, and remains so. The contract is fulfilled: a global guarantee is impossible near the heated end and is achieved from the seventh reading onward if we move one centimetre away from it.

</MissionSolved>

## Lesson summary

You now have the three capabilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Pointwise limit:** fix $x$, calculate the limit of $f_n(x)$, and handle exceptional points separately—this is Definition 1, where the index depends on $x$.
- **Maximal gap:** $\|f_n-f\|_\infty=\sup_x|f_n(x)-f(x)|$; convergence is uniform if and only if this number tends to $0$ (Theorem 1).
- **Calculate this supremum:** differentiate the gap function, or bound it above by an $a_n$ **without $x$**; a bound that still contains $x$ proves nothing.
- **Refute:** exhibit a witness sequence $x_n$ such that the gap at $x_n$ does not tend to $0$ (Proposition 1).
- **Localise:** removing a neighbourhood of the offending point often restores uniformity; but “uniform on every $[a,b]$” is not “uniform on the open interval”.
- **Interpret a bump:** the value at a fixed point, the height of the peak, and the area under the curve answer three different questions.

</KeyResults>
