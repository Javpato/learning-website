<GuidingQuestion>

On $[0,1]$, every function $f_n(x)=x^n$ is continuous, and at each point the sequence of numbers $f_n(x)$ converges. Yet the limit function jumps at $1$. **Where did the convergence fail, given that it holds at every single point?**

</GuidingQuestion>

## 1. Looking at one point at a time

A sequence of functions is not a sequence of numbers. Before comparing two curves, we must say what "tends towards" means for curves — and the most modest answer is to look at only one point at a time.

Problem: we know how to make numbers converge, not graphs. So we have to reduce the question to numerical sequences, one per point of the domain.

**Definition 1 (pointwise convergence).** Let $f_n:D\to\mathbb R$ be a sequence of functions and $f:D\to\mathbb R$. We say that $(f_n)$ admits <Def id="convergence-simple">**pointwise convergence**</Def> towards $f$ on $D$ when, for each fixed $x$ in $D$, the numerical sequence $\bigl(f_n(x)\bigr)_n$ converges to $f(x)$. In other words:

$$
\forall x\in D,\ \forall\varepsilon>0,\ \exists N(x,\varepsilon)\in\mathbb N,\ \forall n\ge N(x,\varepsilon),\quad |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon .
$$

The decisive point is the order of writing: the index $N$ appears **after** $x$, so it is allowed to depend on it. Each point receives its own promise, and nothing forces those promises to resemble one another.

**Example 1.** On $[0,1]$, set $f_n(x)=x^n$. For $0\le x<1$ we have $x^n\to0$; for $x=1$, $x^n=1$ for every $n$. The pointwise limit is therefore

$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}0,&0\le x<1,\\ 1,&x=1.\end{cases}
$$

Continuous functions thus have a discontinuous pointwise limit. Remember this pair: it will serve as a counterexample throughout the whole arc.

**Example 2.** On $[0,+\infty)$, set $g_n(x)=n\,x\,e^{-nx}$. At $0$, $g_n(0)=0$ for every $n$. For fixed $x>0$, the exponential beats the factor $n$, so $g_n(x)\to0$. The pointwise limit is the zero function — and yet, as we shall see, the graph of $g_n$ keeps a peak whose height never drops.

**Remark (a physical reading).** If you read $g_n(x)$ as the temperature profile of a bar at reading number $n$, Definition 1 says that every point of the bar eventually cools down. It says nothing about the bar as a whole. This reading helps intuition; it replaces no argument.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Pointwise convergence transmits essentially nothing: neither continuity (Example 1), nor the value of integrals, nor differentiability. Establishing pointwise convergence is establishing the bare minimum; it is not a usable result.

### Mini-exercises

1. What is the pointwise limit of $h_n(x)=\dfrac{nx}{1+nx}$ on $[0,+\infty)$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   At $x=0$, $h_n(0)=0$ for every $n$. For $x>0$, divide top and bottom by $nx$:

   $$
   h_n(x)=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{nx}+1}\longrightarrow1 .
   $$

   The limit equals $0$ at $0$ and $1$ elsewhere: another discontinuous limit of continuous functions.

   </Collapsible>

2. In Definition 1, what would happen if we required $N$ not to depend on $x$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   We would obtain a strictly stronger statement — precisely the one in the next section. The order of the quantifiers is not a matter of style: it changes the property being defined.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Demanding one index for every point

Problem: in Definition 1, the index $N$ depends on the point. A promise kept point by point says nothing about the domain taken as a whole, since nothing prevents the indices $N(x,\varepsilon)$ from being arbitrarily large as $x$ runs through $D$.

So we need a word for the situation where **one single index works everywhere**.

**Definition 2 (uniform convergence).** The sequence $(f_n)$ admits <Def id="convergence-uniforme">**uniform convergence**</Def> towards $f$ on $D$ when

$$
\forall\varepsilon>0,\ \exists N(\varepsilon)\in\mathbb N,\ \forall n\ge N(\varepsilon),\ \forall x\in D,\quad |f_n(x)-f(x)|<\varepsilon .
$$

Compare the two definitions symbol by symbol: they use exactly the same quantifiers. Only their position changes. Here $N$ is written **before** $x$, so it can no longer depend on it. That is the whole of the difference, and the whole of the chapter's difficulty.

**Proposition 1.** Uniform convergence on $D$ implies pointwise convergence on $D$.

<Proof title="weakening one quantifier">

Assume Definition 2 holds, and fix $x_0\in D$ together with $\varepsilon>0$. The index $N(\varepsilon)$ provided works for every point, so in particular for $x_0$: for $n\ge N(\varepsilon)$ we have $|f_n(x_0)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$. This is Definition 1 at the point $x_0$, with $N(x_0,\varepsilon)=N(\varepsilon)$.

**What the proof teaches.** An index that works for all points works for each one; the converse implication would require choosing an index common to an infinite family of promises, which nothing guarantees. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** The converse is false, and that is the subject of the following sections. Never write "the convergence holds at each point, therefore it is uniform": that confuses Definitions 1 and 2.

Problem: Definition 2 is a sentence with four quantifiers, painful to check as it stands. We need a **number** to compute that summarises it.

**Definition 3 (uniform norm).** For a function $g$ bounded on $D$, the <Def id="norme-uniforme">**uniform norm**</Def> of $g$ on $D$ is

$$
\|g\|_\infty=\sup_{x\in D}|g(x)| .
$$

It is the largest gap that $g$ attains or approaches on $D$: a single number, no longer depending on the point.

**Theorem 1 (uniform norm criterion).** The sequence $(f_n)$ converges uniformly towards $f$ on $D$ if and only if $f_n-f$ is bounded from some index on and

$$
\|f_n-f\|_\infty\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0 .
$$

<Proof title="why a supremum summarises four quantifiers">

**Idea.** Definition 2 demands that one same $\varepsilon$ bound all the gaps at once. But "bounding all the gaps" and "bounding their supremum" are the same requirement: that is the definition of the supremum as the least upper bound.

**Direct implication.** Assume $\|f_n-f\|_\infty\to0$ and fix $\varepsilon>0$. There exists $N$ such that $\|f_n-f\|_\infty<\varepsilon$ as soon as $n\ge N$. For that **same** $N$ and for every $x\in D$,

$$
|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le\sup_{y\in D}|f_n(y)-f(y)|=\|f_n-f\|_\infty<\varepsilon,
$$

which is exactly Definition 2, the index $N$ having never consulted $x$.

**Converse implication.** Assume Definition 2 holds and fix $\varepsilon>0$. It provides $N$ such that, for $n\ge N$, the number $\varepsilon$ bounds $|f_n(x)-f(x)|$ for every $x$. The real number $\varepsilon$ is therefore an upper bound of the set of those gaps, and the supremum, being the least upper bound, satisfies

$$
\|f_n-f\|_\infty\le\varepsilon\qquad\text{for every }n\ge N .
$$

In particular $\|f_n-f\|_\infty$ is finite, and it tends to $0$.

**What the proof teaches.** We obtain $\le\varepsilon$ and not $<\varepsilon$: that is normal, a supremum may sit exactly on its upper bounds, and it is enough for a limit. The reusable move is this one — *for every $x$* becomes *for the sup*, and a logical sentence becomes a computation. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Method 1 (computing a largest gap).**

1. write the gap function $E_n(x)=|f_n(x)-f(x)|$;
2. differentiate it **in $x$, with $n$ fixed**, to locate its maximum;
3. evaluate $E_n$ at that point: this is $\|f_n-f\|_\infty$;
4. if the exact computation is too costly, look for a bound $E_n(x)\le a_n$ where **$a_n$ no longer contains $x$**, then check whether $a_n\to0$.

**Example 3.** Take again $g_n(x)=nxe^{-nx}$ on $[0,+\infty)$, whose pointwise limit is zero, so $E_n=g_n$. Differentiate with $n$ fixed:

$$
g_n'(x)=n e^{-nx}(1-nx),
$$

which vanishes at $x=1/n$ and changes sign from positive to negative: this is a maximum. Hence

$$
\|g_n\|_\infty=g_n\!\left(\tfrac1n\right)=n\cdot\tfrac1n\cdot e^{-1}=\frac1e\approx0{,}368 .
$$

This number does not depend on $n$: it does not tend to $0$. By Theorem 1, **there is no uniform convergence on $[0,+\infty)$**, even though every point converges (Example 2).

**Example 4.** On $\mathbb R$, set $u_n(x)=\dfrac{\sin(nx)}{n}$, whose pointwise limit is zero. Since $|\sin|\le1$ everywhere,

$$
|u_n(x)|\le\frac1n\qquad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb R,
$$

and this value is attained at $x=\pi/(2n)$. Hence $\|u_n\|_\infty=1/n\to0$: the convergence is uniform on the whole of $\mathbb R$.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** A bound concludes something only if it is independent of $x$. Writing $g_n(x)\le nx$ is true and completely useless: the bound still contains $x$ and grows with $n$. Every time you produce a bound, ask yourself — *is there still an $x$ inside?* If so, it says nothing about uniformity.

### Mini-exercises

1. We establish $|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le\dfrac{3}{n}$ for every $x$ in the domain. What can be concluded, and what remains undetermined?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The bound no longer contains $x$ and tends to $0$: Theorem 1 gives uniform convergence, without computing the sup. On the other hand the exact value of $\|f_n-f\|_\infty$ remains unknown; we only know it is at most $3/n$.

   </Collapsible>

2. On $[0,1]$, compute $\|f_n\|_\infty$ for $f_n(x)=x^n$, then conclude.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The gap to the limit equals $x^n$ for $x<1$ and $0$ at $x=1$, so the sup equals $\sup_{0\le x<1}x^n=1$, not attained. This sup equals $1$ at every index: no uniform convergence on $[0,1]$.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. Disproving uniformity with a moving point

Computing a sup exactly requires a derivative, sometimes a painful study. To **disprove** uniformity, however, we do not need the value of the sup: a lower bound suffices, and a single well-chosen point provides one.

Problem: we must bound a supremum from below without computing it. So we need a procedure that exhibits a point where the gap stays large — even if that point changes at every index.

**Definition 4 (witness sequence).** A <Def id="suite-temoin">**witness sequence**</Def> for $(f_n)$ and $f$ on $D$ is a sequence of points $(x_n)$ of $D$ such that $|f_n(x_n)-f(x_n)|$ does not tend to $0$.

**Proposition 2 (disproof by a witness).** If a witness sequence exists, the convergence is not uniform on $D$.

<Proof title="one single point bounds the supremum from below">

**Idea.** The supremum bounds each value from above, so in particular it bounds the value taken at the point $x_n$ — even if that point changes with the index.

By definition of the supremum, for every $n$,

$$
\|f_n-f\|_\infty\ge|f_n(x_n)-f(x_n)| .
$$

If the right-hand side does not tend to $0$, neither does the left-hand side, and Theorem 1 forbids uniform convergence.

**What the proof teaches.** The point $x_n$ is allowed to depend on $n$ because the inequality is written with $n$ fixed. That is what makes the method more flexible than a maximum computation. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Example 5.** For $g_n(x)=nxe^{-nx}$, take $x_n=1/n$, that is, the point where the peak sits at index $n$. Then $g_n(x_n)=1/e$ for every $n$: this is a witness sequence, and Proposition 2 recovers the verdict of Example 3 without differentiating anything.

**Example 6.** For $f_n(x)=x^n$ on $[0,1)$, take $x_n=2^{-1/n}$, which does belong to $[0,1)$. Then

$$
f_n(x_n)=\left(2^{-1/n}\right)^n=\frac12,
$$

whereas the pointwise limit equals $0$ at every point of $[0,1)$. No uniform convergence on $[0,1)$.

**Remark (why the witness must move).** No **fixed** point can disprove uniformity, since pointwise convergence holds at each of them. The reason is logical: the negation of Definition 2 reads

$$
\exists\varepsilon_0>0,\ \forall N,\ \exists n\ge N,\ \exists x\in D,\quad |f_n(x)-f(x)|\ge\varepsilon_0 ,
$$

and in this sentence the point $x$ is quantified **after** the index $n$: it is therefore allowed to depend on it. A witness sequence is nothing other than that point, written as a function of the index.

Problem: these families whose maximum refuses to come down all look alike. We may as well give them a name and a reading grid.

**Definition 5 (travelling bump).** A <Def id="bosse-glissante">**travelling bump**</Def> is a sequence of functions whose graph shows a peak whose position, width or height depends on $n$, without the peak flattening out.

For a bump, three quantities behave **independently**, and each answers a different question:

| Quantity measured | What it decides | For $g_n(x)=nxe^{-nx}$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| value at a fixed point | pointwise convergence | tends to $0$ |
| height of the peak | uniform convergence | equals $1/e$, constant |
| area under the curve | limit of the integrals | equals $1/n$, tends to $0$ |

<Collapsible title="The area computation, to check the third row">

Integrate by parts, with $u=x$ and $v'=ne^{-nx}$:

$$
\int_0^{+\infty}nxe^{-nx}\,dx=\Bigl[-xe^{-nx}\Bigr]_0^{+\infty}+\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-nx}\,dx=0+\frac1n=\frac1n .
$$

Constant height and collapsing area: the two do not measure the same thing. The lesson on interchange theorems will exploit precisely this discrepancy.

</Collapsible>

**Method 2 (recovering uniformity on part of the domain).**

1. locate the guilty point, that is, the position of the peak;
2. remove a neighbourhood of that point, for instance work on $[a,+\infty)$ with $a>0$;
3. check that from some index on the peak has **left** the restricted domain;
4. on that domain the function is then monotone: the sup is attained at the boundary and can be computed.

**Example 7.** On $[a,+\infty)$ with $a>0$: as soon as $1/n\le a$, that is $n\ge1/a$, the function $g_n$ is decreasing on $[a,+\infty)$. The sup there is therefore attained at $x=a$:

$$
\sup_{x\ge a}g_n(x)=n\,a\,e^{-na}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0 .
$$

The convergence is uniform on $[a,+\infty)$ for **every** $a>0$, but not on $(0,+\infty)$.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** "Uniform on $[a,+\infty)$ for every $a>0$" does **not** imply "uniform on $(0,+\infty)$". The required index is $n\ge1/a$: it depends on $a$ and blows up as $a$ tends to $0$, so no common index exists. This is the most frequent error of the arc, and we shall meet it again at the boundary of power series.

### Mini-exercises

1. Propose a witness sequence for $f_n(x)=\sin(x/n)$ on $[0,+\infty)$, whose pointwise limit is zero.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Take $x_n=n\pi/2$, which does belong to the domain. Then $f_n(x_n)=\sin(\pi/2)=1$, which does not tend to $0$: no uniform convergence on $[0,+\infty)$. On a segment $[0,b]$, however, $|\sin(x/n)|\le b/n\to0$ and the convergence is uniform there.

   </Collapsible>

2. A bump has constant height equal to $1$ and width $1/n$. Which of the three quantities in the table tends to $0$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The area alone, which is of the order of the width, hence of $1/n$. The height — and therefore the largest gap — equals $1$ at every index: no uniform convergence, although pointwise convergence holds.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. What uniformity buys

A definition is worth only what it lets you conclude. Here is the first benefit, and it explains Example 1 in retrospect.

Problem: the $f_n$ are continuous and we would like $f$ to be continuous too. We must relate three gaps that live in different places — at $x$, at $x_0$, and between the two functions.

**Theorem 2 (continuity of a uniform limit).** If every $f_n$ is continuous on $D$ and if $f_n\to f$ **uniformly** on $D$, then $f$ is continuous on $D$.

<Proof title="cutting the gap into three pieces">

**Idea.** We know nothing about $f$, but we know two things: $f$ is close to $f_n$ **everywhere at once**, and $f_n$ is continuous. So we travel from $f$ to $f_n$, move along $f_n$, then come back to $f$ — three small gaps instead of one unknown.

Fix $x_0\in D$ and $\varepsilon>0$. The triangle inequality gives, for every $x\in D$ and every $n$,

$$
|f(x)-f(x_0)|\le|f(x)-f_n(x)|+|f_n(x)-f_n(x_0)|+|f_n(x_0)-f(x_0)| .
$$

- **Uniform** convergence provides an index $N$ such that $\|f_N-f\|_\infty<\varepsilon/3$. This single choice bounds the first term **and** the third by $\varepsilon/3$, because the bound does not depend on the point: this is exactly where uniformity is indispensable.
- The function $f_N$, now fixed, is continuous at $x_0$: there exists $\delta>0$ such that $|x-x_0|<\delta$ implies $|f_N(x)-f_N(x_0)|<\varepsilon/3$.

For $|x-x_0|<\delta$, adding the three bounds gives $|f(x)-f(x_0)|<\varepsilon$. So $f$ is continuous at $x_0$, and $x_0$ was arbitrary.

**What the proof teaches.** With pointwise convergence alone, the first term would be bounded at the point $x$ by an index $N(x)$ and the third at the point $x_0$ by an index $N(x_0)$: nothing would allow us to choose **one** $N$ working for both while $x$ varies. The $\varepsilon/3$ cut is the standard pattern for every interchange of limits; we shall reuse it. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Corollary (disproof by discontinuity).** If the $f_n$ are continuous and the pointwise limit $f$ is discontinuous, then the convergence is not uniform. This is the contrapositive of Theorem 2.

**Example 8.** Example 1 is settled without the slightest computation: the functions $x^n$ are continuous on $[0,1]$ and their pointwise limit jumps at $1$, so the convergence is not uniform on $[0,1]$.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** The converse of Theorem 2 is false: a continuous limit **does not imply** uniformity. The functions $g_n(x)=nxe^{-nx}$ have the zero function as their limit, perfectly continuous, and yet the convergence is not uniform (Example 3). The Corollary therefore serves only to disprove, never to confirm.

Problem: Theorem 1 and Proposition 2 assume that $f$ is already known. Yet we often want to prove that a limit **exists** — for a series of functions, for instance, the sum is not given in advance.

**Theorem 3 (uniform Cauchy criterion).** The sequence $(f_n)$ converges uniformly on $D$ towards some function if and only if

$$
\forall\varepsilon>0,\ \exists N,\ \forall p,q\ge N,\ \forall x\in D,\quad |f_p(x)-f_q(x)|\le\varepsilon .
$$

<Proof title="completeness builds the limit, uniformity makes it uniform">

**Idea.** The criterion does not mention $f$. We obtain it in two stages: the completeness of $\mathbb R$ constructs $f$ point by point, then we let one index tend to infinity **inside the inequality**, which carries uniformity over to the limit.

**Direct implication.** If $f_n\to f$ uniformly, take $N$ such that $\|f_n-f\|_\infty\le\varepsilon/2$ for $n\ge N$. For $p,q\ge N$ and every $x$,

$$
|f_p(x)-f_q(x)|\le|f_p(x)-f(x)|+|f(x)-f_q(x)|\le\varepsilon .
$$

**Converse implication.** Assume the criterion holds. With $x$ fixed, the numerical sequence $\bigl(f_n(x)\bigr)_n$ is a Cauchy sequence of $\mathbb R$. Since $\mathbb R$ is **complete** — every real Cauchy sequence converges — it has a limit, which we denote $f(x)$. This defines a function $f$ on $D$, but only in the pointwise sense.

Now take the criterion's inequality with $p\ge N$ and $x$ fixed, and let $q$ tend to $+\infty$. The left-hand side tends to $|f_p(x)-f(x)|$ and the weak inequality passes to the limit:

$$
|f_p(x)-f(x)|\le\varepsilon\qquad\text{for every }p\ge N\text{ and every }x\in D .
$$

The index $N$ came from the criterion and never consulted $x$: this is Definition 2.

**What the proof teaches.** The only fact taken for granted is the completeness of $\mathbb R$, and we see exactly where it enters: to build $f$, not to make it uniform. The move "let an index tend to infinity inside a weak inequality" will return every time a limit must inherit a uniform bound. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

### Mini-exercises

1. The $f_n$ are continuous and the pointwise limit $f$ is continuous. Can we conclude uniformity?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No. Theorem 2 reads in one direction only; its converse is false, as $g_n(x)=nxe^{-nx}$ shows — a zero, hence continuous, limit without uniform convergence.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why is Theorem 3 indispensable for series of functions?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because it involves only the $f_n$ among themselves. For a series, the $f_n$ are the partial sums and the sum is not known in advance: a criterion requiring $f$ would not be usable.

   </Collapsible>

## Worked example — proving, then disproving

**Case A — proving uniformity.** Let us study $\varphi_n(x)=\sqrt{x^2+\tfrac1{n^2}}$ on $\mathbb R$.

**Step 1 — obtain the limit function.** With $x$ fixed, $\tfrac1{n^2}\to0$ so $\varphi_n(x)\to\sqrt{x^2}=|x|$.

*Why?* Theorem 1 compares $\varphi_n$ to $\varphi$: without the pointwise limit there is nothing to measure.

**Step 2 — write the gap in a boundable form.** A difference of two square roots cannot be bounded as it stands; multiply by the conjugate quantity:

$$
0\le\varphi_n(x)-|x|=\frac{\left(x^2+\frac1{n^2}\right)-x^2}{\sqrt{x^2+\frac1{n^2}}+|x|}=\frac{1/n^2}{\sqrt{x^2+\frac1{n^2}}+|x|} .
$$

*Why?* The conjugate turns a difference into a quotient whose denominator we can bound from below — and bounding a denominator from below means bounding the quotient from above.

**Step 3 — bound independently of $x$.** The denominator is at least $\sqrt{1/n^2}=1/n$, so

$$
0\le\varphi_n(x)-|x|\le\frac{1/n^2}{1/n}=\frac1n .
$$

*Why?* This is point 4 of Method 1: the bound $1/n$ no longer contains $x$, so it is usable for the whole domain at once.

**Step 4 — conclude by the criterion.** Since $1/n\to0$, Theorem 1 gives uniform convergence on $\mathbb R$. The maximum is moreover attained at $x=0$, where the gap equals exactly $1/n$: the bound was optimal.

*Why?* Checking that the bound is attained confirms that nothing was lost in bounding, and gives the exact value of the uniform norm.

**Remark.** The limit $|x|$ is not differentiable at $0$, whereas every $\varphi_n$ is of class $C^\infty$. Uniformity transmits continuity (Theorem 2); it does not transmit differentiability. The lesson on interchange theorems will say which hypothesis does.

**Case B — disproving uniformity.** Let us study $\psi_n(x)=\dfrac{x^n}{1+x^n}$ on $[0,1]$.

**Step 1 — obtain the limit function.** For $0\le x<1$, $x^n\to0$ so $\psi_n(x)\to0$; at $x=1$, $\psi_n(1)=1/2$ at every index. The pointwise limit equals $0$ on $[0,1)$ and $1/2$ at $1$.

*Why?* As in Step 1 of Case A: the pointwise limit is the compulsory object of comparison.

**Step 2 — recognise the situation.** Every $\psi_n$ is continuous on $[0,1]$ and the pointwise limit is discontinuous there.

*Why?* The Corollary of Theorem 2 concludes immediately in this configuration, without any sup computation.

**Step 3 — conclude and locate the defect.** There is no uniform convergence on $[0,1]$. The witness $x_n=2^{-1/n}$ confirms it: $\psi_n(x_n)=\tfrac{1/2}{1+1/2}=\tfrac13$, which does not tend to $0$.

*Why?* Proposition 2 provides an independent proof, and the position of $x_n$ shows that the defect is concentrated near $x=1$.

**Step 4 — restrict.** On $[0,b]$ with $b<1$, we have $0\le\psi_n(x)\le x^n\le b^n$, a bound without $x$ tending to $0$: the convergence is uniform there.

*Why?* This is Method 2: removing a neighbourhood of the guilty point makes the sup computable and restores the global guarantee.

<Collapsible title="Your turn — answer">

Study $\theta_n(x)=nx(1-x)^n$ on $[0,1]$: pointwise limit, largest gap, verdict.

At $x=0$ and $x=1$ the term is zero; for $0<x<1$, $(1-x)^n$ decreases geometrically and beats $n$, so $\theta_n(x)\to0$. The pointwise limit is zero.

Differentiating, $\theta_n'(x)=n(1-x)^{n-1}\bigl(1-(n+1)x\bigr)$, which vanishes at $x=1/(n+1)$. There,

$$
\theta_n\!\left(\frac1{n+1}\right)=\frac{n}{n+1}\left(1-\frac1{n+1}\right)^{n}\longrightarrow\frac1e ,
$$

since $\bigl(1-\tfrac1{n+1}\bigr)^{n}\to e^{-1}$. The largest gap tends to $1/e\ne0$: no uniform convergence on $[0,1]$. This is again a travelling bump, this time of only asymptotically constant height.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**"For each $x$ the gap tends to $0$, therefore the convergence is uniform."** That is Definition 1, not Definition 2. The test to ask yourself: **does my index $N$ depend on $x$?** If it does, you have proved only pointwise convergence.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Bounding with a bound that still contains $x$.** The inequality $nxe^{-nx}\le nx$ is true and worthless. A bound serves Theorem 1 only if it is independent of the variable — that is the very meaning of the word "uniform".

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Looking for a fixed witness.** A fixed point can never disprove uniformity, since pointwise convergence holds at every fixed point. The negation quantifies $x$ after $n$: the witness must depend on the index.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Gluing the pieces together.** From "uniform on $[a,b]$ for every segment" one does not deduce "uniform on the open interval". Each segment comes with its own index, and those indices have no common bound. Always treat the interior and the boundary separately.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believing that Theorem 2 reverses.** A continuous limit proves nothing: the contrapositive disproves, it never confirms.

</Pitfall>

<GuidingAnswer>

The convergence failed **nowhere in particular** — and that is precisely the answer. At every point of $[0,1]$ the sequence $x^n$ converges; what is missing is not a point, it is a **common index**. The indices $N(x,\varepsilon)$ all exist, but they blow up as $x$ approaches $1$, so none of them works for the whole domain at once.

The defect can be read in three equivalent ways: the largest gap $\|f_n-f\|_\infty$ equals $1$ at every index (Theorem 1); the witness sequence $x_n=2^{-1/n}$ keeps a gap of $1/2$ (Proposition 2); and the limit is discontinuous although the $f_n$ are not (Theorem 2). Moving a single quantifier changed the property — and it is that displaced quantifier which will decide, in the coming lessons, whether we are allowed to interchange a limit with a sum, an integral or a derivative.

</GuidingAnswer>

## Summary — decide, disprove, localise

<KeyResults title="The decision sheet">

| Situation | Hypotheses to write | Conclusion | Inconclusive case |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x$ fixed, $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ | none | <Terme id="convergence-simple">pointwise convergence</Terme> | transmits neither continuity nor integrals |
| largest gap $\to0$ | $f_n-f$ bounded | <Terme id="convergence-uniforme">uniform convergence</Terme> | none: this is an equivalence |
| bound $E_n(x)\le a_n$ | $a_n$ **without** $x$, $a_n\to0$ | uniform convergence | bound containing $x$: nothing |
| <Terme id="suite-temoin">witness sequence</Terme> $x_n$ | gap at $x_n$ does not tend to $0$ | no uniform convergence | fixed witness: impossible |
| $f_n$ continuous, $f$ discontinuous | continuity of each $f_n$ | no uniform convergence | $f$ continuous: no verdict |
| uniform Cauchy criterion | one $\varepsilon$ common to all $x$ | uniform convergence, limit not known in advance | — |
| uniform on every $[a,b]$ | — | nothing on the open interval | the index depends on $a$ |

**Proofs worth being able to reconstruct.** The sup summarises the four quantifiers (Theorem 1); a moving point bounds the sup from below (Proposition 2); the $\varepsilon/3$ cut transmits continuity (Theorem 2); the completeness of $\mathbb R$ builds the limit, then one lets an index tend to infinity inside a weak inequality (Theorem 3).

**The idea to take away.** A <Terme id="bosse-glissante">travelling bump</Terme> reads in three independent columns — value at a fixed point, height of the peak, area under the curve — and only the second decides uniformity. The <Terme id="norme-uniforme">uniform norm</Terme> is the tool that turns that reading into a computation.

</KeyResults>
