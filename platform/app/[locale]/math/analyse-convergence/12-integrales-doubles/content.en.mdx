<GuidingQuestion>

Lagrange boasted of needing no drawing at all. For double integrals the instruction is exactly the opposite: **a picture of the domain decides the bounds, and almost every error in this chapter comes from bounds written before the domain was described.** It remains to understand why the order of integration may be exchanged — and when it may not.

</GuidingQuestion>

## 1. Integrating over a region of the plane

**Definition 1 (double integral).** For $f$ continuous on a bounded domain $D\subset\mathbb R^2$, the <Def id="integrale-double">**double integral**</Def>

$$
\iint_D f(x,y)\,dx\,dy
$$

is the limit of Riemann sums built on ever finer tilings of $D$. When $f\ge0$ it is the volume between $D$ and the graph of $f$; when $f=1$ it is the area of $D$.

Problem: this definition is useless for computing. A single integral, by contrast, is computed with an antiderivative. So we must reduce the planar computation to **two** successive one-variable computations — and that is where the description of the domain comes in.

**Definition 2 (domain described by slices).** A domain is described **by vertical slices** when it is written

$$
D=\bigl\{(x,y)\ :\ a\le x\le b,\ \varphi_1(x)\le y\le\varphi_2(x)\bigr\},
$$

with $\varphi_1\le\varphi_2$ continuous. The description **by horizontal slices** exchanges the roles of $x$ and $y$.

**Method 1 (writing the bounds without error).**

1. **Draw** the domain, including the intersection points of the curves bounding it.
2. Choose a slicing direction and **project** the domain onto the corresponding axis: this gives the **outer** bounds, which are always **constants**.
3. At the current value of the outer variable, read off where the slice enters and leaves the domain: this gives the **inner** bounds, which may depend on the outer variable.
4. Check consistency: the outer bound must **never** contain the inner variable.

That point 4 is the most profitable test in the chapter. An outer bound still containing $y$ signals a description error for certain.

### Mini-exercises

1. Describe by vertical slices the triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The projection onto the $x$-axis is $[0,1]$. With $x$ fixed, the slice runs from $y=0$ to the line $y=x$. So $0\le x\le1$ and $0\le y\le x$.

   </Collapsible>

2. What should we make of the expression $\displaystyle\int_0^{y}\!\!\int_0^{1}f\,dx\,dy$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   It is wrong: the **outer** bound contains the inner variable $y$, which makes no sense. This is the test of point 4 of Method 1.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Fubini: when may we split into two successive computations?

**Definition 3 (Fubini's theorem).** <Def id="theoreme-de-fubini">**Fubini's theorem**</Def> authorises replacing a double integral by two nested single integrals, in either order.

**Theorem 1 (Fubini on a simple domain).** If $f$ is continuous on the domain $D$ described by vertical slices as in Definition 2, then

$$
\iint_D f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=\int_a^{b}\left(\int_{\varphi_1(x)}^{\varphi_2(x)}f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx .
$$

<Proof title="on a rectangle, two groupings of the same Riemann sums">

**Idea.** On a rectangle, a double Riemann sum is a finite sum over a grid. But a **finite** sum may be grouped by columns or by rows without changing anything: that is the commutativity of addition. All the work consists in carrying that elementary fact over to the limit.

Take first the rectangle $P=[a,b]\times[c,d]$. Subdivide $[a,b]$ into $m$ pieces and $[c,d]$ into $n$ pieces, and write $\Delta_{ij}$ for the resulting rectangles, of sides $\Delta x_i$ and $\Delta y_j$. The Riemann sum reads

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(x_i,y_j)\,\Delta x_i\,\Delta y_j
=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}f(x_i,y_j)\,\Delta y_j\right)\Delta x_i .
$$

The regrouping is legitimate because the sum is **finite** — the same move as in lesson 07, where the finite identity always preceded the passage to the limit.

Since $f$ is continuous on the compact set $P$, it is **uniformly continuous** there. The inner sum therefore converges to $\int_c^d f(x_i,y)\,dy$ with a control independent of $x_i$, which authorises passing to the limit in the outer sum. We obtain the stated equality on $P$.

For a domain described by slices, extend $f$ by $0$ outside $D$ on a rectangle containing $D$. The extended function remains integrable, and the inner integral equals $\int_{\varphi_1(x)}^{\varphi_2(x)}f(x,y)\,dy$, the other values of $y$ contributing nothing.

**What the proof teaches.** Fubini is not a mysterious computation rule: it is the commutativity of a finite sum, plus uniform continuity on a compact set to allow the passage to the limit. Remove compactness or continuity and the argument collapses — which the next section illustrates. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Theorem 2 (Fubini–Tonelli, positive version).** If $f\ge0$ is measurable, the two iterated integrals are **always** equal, possibly to $+\infty$. They may therefore be computed in either order with no prior hypothesis — and that computation is exactly what establishes integrability.

**Remark (the standard strategy).** Hence the usual order of work on an unbounded domain:

1. apply Tonelli to $|f|$ to check that $\iint|f|<+\infty$;
2. only then apply Fubini to $f$ and exchange freely.

**Counterexample 1 (without positivity or integrability, the order matters).** On $D=[0,1]\times[0,1]$, take

$$
f(x,y)=\frac{x^{2}-y^{2}}{(x^{2}+y^{2})^{2}},\qquad f(0,0)=0 .
$$

A direct computation — the antiderivative in $y$ being $-y/(x^2+y^2)$ — gives

$$
\int_0^1\!\left(\int_0^1 f\,dy\right)dx=\frac\pi4,
\qquad
\int_0^1\!\left(\int_0^1 f\,dx\right)dy=-\frac\pi4 ,
$$

the second following from the first by the antisymmetry $f(y,x)=-f(x,y)$.

**The implication that fails is "both iterated integrals exist $\Rightarrow$ they are equal".** The missing hypothesis is integrability: here $\iint|f|=+\infty$, since $|f|$ behaves like $1/(x^2+y^2)$ near the origin, whose double integral diverges. Without it, the order of integration becomes a choice that changes the answer — exactly like the two limits of lesson 07.

### Mini-exercises

1. Why may Tonelli be applied with no prior check?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because it requires only positivity, and a sum of positive terms always has a value in $[0,+\infty]$: no cancellation is possible, hence no ambiguity. This is the same role positivity played in lesson 02.

   </Collapsible>

2. Does Counterexample 1 contradict Theorem 1?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No. Theorem 1 assumes $f$ **continuous on the closed domain**, and this $f$ is not continuous at $(0,0)$ — it is not even bounded there. The hypothesis is violated, and the counterexample shows that the conclusion is too.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. Changing variables: the Jacobian as an area factor

Problem: describing a disc by vertical slices forces bounds involving $\sqrt{1-x^2}$, painful to integrate. It would be better to change coordinate system — provided we know how the area element transforms.

**Definition 4 (Jacobian).** For a map $(u,v)\mapsto\bigl(x(u,v),y(u,v)\bigr)$ of class $C^1$, the <Def id="jacobien">**Jacobian**</Def> is the determinant

$$
J=\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}
=\begin{vmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v}\\[8pt]
\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v}
\end{vmatrix}.
$$

**Theorem 3 (change of variables).** The <Def id="changement-de-variables">**change of variables**</Def> $(u,v)\mapsto(x,y)$, assumed $C^1$ and bijective from $D'$ onto $D$ except on a set of zero area, gives

$$
\iint_D f(x,y)\,dx\,dy=\iint_{D'}f\bigl(x(u,v),y(u,v)\bigr)\,|J|\,du\,dv .
$$

<Proof title="why a determinant, and why its absolute value">

**Idea.** Locally, a $C^1$ map looks like its differential, that is, like a **linear** map. So it suffices to understand how a linear map transforms areas, and then to glue.

Take the linear case, where everything is exact. A linear map $L$ with matrix $A$ sends the unit square to the parallelogram spanned by the columns of $A$, whose area equals $|\det A|$. It therefore multiplies **all** areas by that same factor: a fine tiling of the plane is sent to a tiling each of whose cells has had its area multiplied by $|\det A|$.

The absolute value appears because an area is positive whereas a determinant may be negative: its sign only indicates whether the map preserves or reverses orientation, which the area does not care about.

In the general case, the affine approximation near a point $(u_0,v_0)$ has the Jacobian matrix, so a small cell of area $\Delta u\,\Delta v$ is sent to a region of area approximately $|J(u_0,v_0)|\,\Delta u\,\Delta v$. Summing over a tiling and passing to the limit — the approximation error being controlled by the continuity of the partial derivatives on a compact set — gives the formula.

**What the proof teaches.** The Jacobian is not an arbitrary corrective factor: it is the local rate of dilation of areas, and it is a determinant because areas are read off determinants. Remembering this saves memorising where the $|J|$ goes in the formula. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Example 1 (polar coordinates).** With $x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$,

$$
J=\begin{vmatrix}\cos\theta & -r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta\end{vmatrix}
=r\cos^2\theta+r\sin^2\theta=r,
$$

whence the formula to know by heart:

$$
dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta .
$$

The factor $r$ is not a convention: at distance $r$ from the origin, a sector of angle $d\theta$ is the wider the further out you go.

**Example 2 (dilation and the area of an ellipse).** For $x=au$, $y=bv$, we have $J=ab$. The ellipse $x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2\le1$ becomes the unit disc, so its area equals $ab\times\pi=\pi ab$.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If bijectivity fails on more than a set of zero area, the formula counts certain regions several times. In polar coordinates we therefore take $\theta$ in an interval of length $2\pi$ and $r\ge0$: the origin and a half-line, of zero area, are the only tolerated exceptions.

### Mini-exercises

1. Compute the area of the disc of radius $R$ in polar coordinates.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $$
   \iint_D dx\,dy=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{R}r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\cdot\frac{R^{2}}2=\pi R^{2}.
   $$

   Without the factor $r$ we would have found $2\pi R$, which is a perimeter: a Jacobian error is often spotted from the dimension of the result.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why the absolute value in Theorem 3?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because the sign of the determinant indicates orientation, whereas an area is positive by nature. Swapping two variables changes the sign of $J$ without changing the area of the domain.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. Two classic payoffs

**Example 3 (the Gaussian integral).** Let us show that $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}e^{-t^{2}}dt=\sqrt\pi$, a result already used in lesson 10.

Write $I$ for that integral. Since the integrand is positive, Tonelli applies with no check and

$$
I^{2}=\left(\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-x^{2}}dx\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-y^{2}}dy\right)
=\iint_{\mathbb R^{2}}e^{-(x^{2}+y^{2})}\,dx\,dy .
$$

Go over to polar coordinates, where $x^2+y^2=r^2$ and $dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta$:

$$
I^{2}=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{+\infty}e^{-r^{2}}\,r\,dr\,d\theta
=2\pi\left[-\tfrac12e^{-r^{2}}\right]_0^{+\infty}=\pi .
$$

Since $I>0$, we conclude $I=\sqrt\pi$. **It is the factor $r$ from the Jacobian that makes the integral computable**: without it $e^{-r^2}$ has no elementary antiderivative, with it the antiderivative is immediate. Passing through the plane solved a one-variable problem.

**Example 4 (swapping the order in order to compute at all).** Let us compute

$$
\int_0^1\!\!\int_x^1 e^{-y^{2}}\,dy\,dx .
$$

The inner integral is impossible as it stands. Describe the domain: $0\le x\le1$ and $x\le y\le1$, that is, the triangle $\{0\le x\le y\le1\}$. Described by horizontal slices it becomes $0\le y\le1$ and $0\le x\le y$. The integrand is continuous on that compact set, so Theorem 1 applies in both directions and

$$
\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{y}e^{-y^{2}}\,dx\,dy=\int_0^1 y\,e^{-y^{2}}\,dy=\left[-\tfrac12e^{-y^{2}}\right]_0^1=\frac{1-e^{-1}}2 .
$$

The factor $y$ produced by the inner integration is exactly what was missing in order to find an antiderivative.

### Mini-exercises

1. In Example 4, which hypothesis authorises the exchange?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The continuity of $e^{-y^2}$ on the closed triangle, which is compact: Theorem 1 then applies in both descriptions. One could also invoke Tonelli, the integrand being positive.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why can the Gaussian integral not be computed in one variable?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because $e^{-t^2}$ has no elementary antiderivative. Squaring produces a double integral, and the polar Jacobian supplies the factor $r$ that makes the integrand antidifferentiable.

   </Collapsible>

## Worked example — describe, choose, compute

Let us compute $\displaystyle\iint_D x\,dx\,dy$ where $D$ is the region bounded by the parabola $y=x^{2}$ and the line $y=x+2$.

**Step 1 — draw and find the intersections.** Solve $x^{2}=x+2$, that is $x^{2}-x-2=0$, so $x=-1$ and $x=2$. Between those two abscissas the line lies above the parabola.

*Why?* Without the intersection points we know neither the outer bounds nor which of the two curves bounds the top of the slice.

**Step 2 — choose the slicing direction.** By vertical slices, the slice enters at $y=x^{2}$ and leaves at $y=x+2$:

$$
D=\bigl\{-1\le x\le2,\ x^{2}\le y\le x+2\bigr\}.
$$

*Why?* Horizontal slices would require cutting the domain in two — below and above $y=1$ — because the description of the left boundary changes there. The direction that avoids a split is always preferable.

**Step 3 — check consistency, then integrate in $y$.** The outer bounds $-1$ and $2$ are constants: the test of point 4 of Method 1 is passed. Then

$$
\iint_D x\,dx\,dy=\int_{-1}^{2}x\Bigl[(x+2)-x^{2}\Bigr]dx=\int_{-1}^{2}\bigl(x^{2}+2x-x^{3}\bigr)dx .
$$

*Why?* The integrand $x$ does not depend on $y$, so the inner integration reduces to multiplying by the height of the slice.

**Step 4 — compute and check the result.**

$$
\int_{-1}^{2}\bigl(x^{2}+2x-x^{3}\bigr)dx=\left[\frac{x^{3}}3+x^{2}-\frac{x^{4}}4\right]_{-1}^{2}=\Bigl(\tfrac83+4-4\Bigr)-\Bigl(-\tfrac13+1-\tfrac14\Bigr)=\frac94 .
$$

*Why?* A final check is called for: the domain lies mostly on the $x>0$ side, so a positive integral of $x$ was to be expected. Checking sign and order of magnitude catches most bound errors.

<Collapsible title="Your turn — answer">

Compute $\displaystyle\iint_D (x^{2}+y^{2})\,dx\,dy$ where $D$ is the annulus $1\le x^{2}+y^{2}\le4$.

The rotational symmetry of the domain **and** of the integrand calls for polar coordinates. The annulus becomes $1\le r\le2$ and $0\le\theta\le2\pi$, a rectangle in $(r,\theta)$ — which is the whole point of the change. With $x^2+y^2=r^2$ and $dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta$:

$$
\iint_D (x^{2}+y^{2})\,dx\,dy=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_1^{2}r^{2}\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\left[\frac{r^{4}}4\right]_1^{2}=2\pi\cdot\frac{15}4=\frac{15\pi}2 .
$$

Beware the trap: the integrand becomes $r^2$, **and then** we multiply again by the $r$ of the Jacobian, which gives $r^3$ and not $r^2$.

</Collapsible>

## Guided visualisation

No widget here: it is the **description of the domain** that must be seen, and the table below makes it explicit for the triangle $T=\{0\le x\le y\le1\}$ of Example 4.

| Description | Outer bound | Inner bound | Contains a variable? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| vertical slices | $0\le x\le1$ | $x\le y\le1$ | inner only — correct |
| horizontal slices | $0\le y\le1$ | $0\le x\le y$ | inner only — correct |
| faulty expression | $0\le x\le y$ | $0\le y\le1$ | outer contains $y$ — wrong |

**Cycle 1 — two descriptions, one domain.**

- **Predict.** Do the first two rows describe the same region of the plane?
- **Act.** Take the point $(0{,}3\,;0{,}7)$ and then $(0{,}7\,;0{,}3)$, and test them in each system of inequalities.
- **Observe.** The first point satisfies both descriptions, the second satisfies neither: it really is the same triangle, described in two ways.
- **Connect.** This is Theorem 1: both orders compute the same integral, which gives the right to choose whichever one can be computed — Example 4 was feasible in one direction only.

**Cycle 2 — spotting an impossible expression.**

- **Predict.** The third row resembles the second, up to the order of the lines. Can it define anything?
- **Act.** Try to evaluate the outer integral: it would run over $x$ between $0$ and $y$, while $y$ has not yet been fixed.
- **Observe.** The result would depend on $y$, whereas a double integral is a number.
- **Connect.** This is the test of point 4 of Method 1: the outer bound must be constant. That mechanical check catches the most frequent error of the chapter, before any computation.

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Writing the bounds before drawing.** The correct order is: picture, intersections, projection, then bounds. Almost every error in this chapter comes from the reverse order.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**An outer bound containing the inner variable.** The result would be a function, not a number. Always re-read your bounds starting from the outside.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the Jacobian, or forgetting half of it.** In polar coordinates the integrand becomes $f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)$ **and** the area element becomes $r\,dr\,d\theta$. Both transformations, not just one.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Swapping the order without positivity or integrability.** Counterexample 1 gives $\pi/4$ one way and $-\pi/4$ the other. Check $\iint|f|<+\infty$ by Tonelli before invoking Fubini.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Not checking the result.** A negative area, a volume whose dimension does not match, an absurd order of magnitude: those three checks cost ten seconds and catch the essentials.

</Pitfall>

<GuidingAnswer>

The order of integration may be exchanged because, on a rectangle, a double Riemann sum is a **finite** sum that may be grouped by columns or by rows indifferently. That is the commutativity of addition, nothing more. What costs something is the passage to the limit: it requires uniform continuity on a compact set, or — off a compact set — positivity (Tonelli) or absolute integrability (Fubini).

When none of those hypotheses is available, the exchange really is false: Counterexample 1 gives $\pi/4$ one way and $-\pi/4$ the other. This is the same phenomenon as in lesson 07, where two limits gave $1$ and $0$ depending on the order, and as in lesson 10, where mass could escape. This whole track repeats a single lesson in different clothes: **exchanging two infinite processes is a right that has to be bought.**

As for the drawing, it is not a matter of comfort. The outer bounds must be constants and the inner ones must describe where the slice enters and leaves; only a picture of the domain and its intersections lets you write them. Lagrange could do without figures because his mechanics was already set up in equations; here, the figure **is** the setting-up of the equations.

</GuidingAnswer>

## Summary — describe, justify, compute

<KeyResults title="The decision sheet">

| Situation | What we write | Conclusion | Inconclusive case |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| bounded domain, $f$ continuous | description by slices, Theorem 1 | both orders are legitimate | $f$ unbounded: hypothesis violated |
| $f\ge0$ | Tonelli | both orders agree in $[0,+\infty]$ | none: this is the hypothesis-free case |
| $f$ of variable sign, unbounded domain | Tonelli on $\lvert f\rvert $, then Fubini | exchange legitimate if $\iint\lvert f\rvert <+\infty$ | without integrability, the order changes the answer |
| domain with rotational symmetry | polar, $dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta$ | rectangular domain in $(r,\theta)$ | bijectivity to be checked |
| general change of variables | Theorem 3, factor $\lvert J\rvert $ | transport formula | multiple covering: wrong |
| inner integral impossible | change description | often computable the other way | check the exchange hypotheses first |

**Proofs worth being able to reconstruct.** The regrouping of a finite sum by columns or rows, plus uniform continuity on a compact set (Theorem 1); the Jacobian as an area dilation factor, exact for a linear map where it equals $|\det A|$ (Theorem 3); and the square of the Gaussian integral turned into a double integral, which the factor $r$ makes antidifferentiable.

**The idea to take away.** A double integral is computed in two stages that have nothing to do with each other: first **describe** the domain, which is a piece of geometry and is done on a picture; then **justify** the exchange, which is a piece of analysis and is done with Tonelli or Fubini. Confusing the two is the source of almost every error.

</KeyResults>
