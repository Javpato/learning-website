# Describe the region to choose the right calculation

<LessonMeta id="math-an-c12" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

A surface is rarely calculated by attacking antiderivatives at random: you begin by choosing how to slice it.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="A non-uniform plate, then the elliptical cross-section of a beam">

**Situation:** a triangular plate occupies the region with vertices $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$, with lengths measured in metres; its surface mass density is $2e^{x^2}$ kg·m⁻². A beam then occupies an ellipse with semi-axes $2$ cm and $1$ cm, with intensity $100(1-x^2/4-y^2)$ W·cm⁻².
**Question:** what are the mass of the plate, its resistance to rotation about the vertical axis, and the total power of the beam?
**The obstacle:** one-variable calculus follows only a line; choosing the wrong slicing direction can even produce an antiderivative that cannot be written using elementary functions.
**The contract:** this chapter provides 4 tools (accumulating over a surface, choosing your slices, straightening an ellipse, correcting the distortion of areas). At the end, we obtain the three requested numbers.

</Mission>

*Feeling confident? [Go straight to the TD (tutorial sheet)](../td-7/) — the theory will still be here when you get stuck.*

## The battle plan

To go from the diagram to the number, we need four capabilities.

1. **Accumulate over a plane region**: turn a density or intensity into a total quantity.
2. **Slice in the right direction**: describe the same domain using vertical or horizontal slices.
3. **Straighten the domain**: replace an ellipse or a disc with suitable coordinates.
4. **Correct the areas**: measure exactly how much the transformation stretches or contracts them.

## 1. Accumulate a quantity distributed over a surface

**Tool 1 — go from local to total.** A value per unit area must be added over the entire plate, not merely along a segment.

You already know the <Terme id="limite">limit (in two variables)</Terme> and <Terme id="continuite">continuity</Terme>; we use them here without redefining them.

Problem: an ordinary integral adds along an interval, whereas our material occupies a region of the plane.
We therefore need a tool that adds small contributions over areas.

**Definition 1 (integral over a plane region).** For a continuous function $f$ on a bounded region $D$, the <Def id="integrale-double">**double integral**</Def> (the limiting sum of local values multiplied by small areas) is denoted

$$
\iint_D f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.
$$

If $f\ge0$, it represents the volume under the graph of $f$. If $f$ is a surface mass density $\rho$, it gives the mass; if $f=x^2\rho$, it gives the moment of inertia about the vertical axis.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous layer — from small cells to the limit">

We divide $D$ into small cells $D_k$ of area $\Delta A_k$, then choose a point $P_k$ in each one. When the maximum diameter of the cells tends to zero, the sums

$$
\sum_k f(P_k)\,\Delta A_k
$$

have a limit independent of the partition when $f$ is continuous and the boundary of $D$ is sufficiently regular. This limit is the double integral. Continuity is therefore not decorative: it guarantees that small changes of position have almost no effect on the local contributions.

</Collapsible>

**Example (physics) 1.** Let

$$
D=\bigl\{(x,y):0\le y\le x\le1\bigr\},
\qquad \rho(x,y)=2e^{x^2}.
$$

The mass and the moment about the vertical axis are respectively

$$
M=\iint_D\rho(x,y)\,dx\,dy,
\qquad I_y=\iint_Dx^2\rho(x,y)\,dx\,dy.
$$

The factor $x^2$ in $I_y$ gives more weight to material farther from the axis: two pieces with the same mass do not resist rotation equally if they are not at the same distance.

**Remark (laboratory notation).** In a TP (practical session), you would often denote the surface mass density by $\sigma(x,y)$ rather than $\rho(x,y)$, and the detector would use $I(x,y)$ for the beam intensity. Same object, different clothing: a local density multiplied by a small area.

## 2. Slice the domain in the useful order

**Tool 2 — turn the surface into two ordinary integrals.** The diagram of the domain determines the bounds; the integrand then determines the most economical direction.

Problem: the notation in Definition 1 says what to add, but not yet how to perform the calculation.
We therefore need a result that permits successive integrations.

**Theorem 1 (Fubini on a simple region).** Let $f$ be continuous on the compact region

$$
D=\bigl\{(x,y):a\le x\le b,\ \alpha(x)\le y\le\beta(x)\bigr\}.
$$

The result known as <Def id="theoreme-de-fubini">**Fubini's theorem**</Def> (calculating a sum over a surface through two ordinary integrations) gives

$$
\iint_Df(x,y)\,dx\,dy
=\int_a^b\left(\int_{\alpha(x)}^{\beta(x)}f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx.
$$

If $D$ is described horizontally by $c\le y\le d$ and $\gamma(y)\le x\le\delta(y)$, the same theorem gives the other order.

<Collapsible title="Proof — why two successive sums are enough">

On a rectangle, a sum over small cells can be grouped first row by row, then column by column: these are two finite sums whose order can be exchanged. On passing to the limit, the continuity of $f$ gives the two successive integrals. A region between two graphs reduces to the same argument by retaining, for each $x$, only the cells lying between $\alpha(x)$ and $\beta(x)$.

</Collapsible>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Theorem 1, in this version, does not apply if the domain is unbounded or if the integrand has a singularity. You must then prove integrability with a suitable theorem before interchanging anything.

**Method 1 (domain first, calculation second).** 

1. draw the boundaries and their intersections;
2. fix $x$ to read the vertical slice, then fix $y$ to read the horizontal one;
3. write both pairs of bounds without integrating yet;
4. choose the order that simplifies the inner antiderivative.

**Example (physics) 2.** For the plate, the descriptions

$$
0\le x\le1,\quad 0\le y\le x
$$

and

$$
0\le y\le1,\quad y\le x\le1
$$

cover the same triangle. Here is the checklist before calculating the mass.

1. **Domain:** $D$ is the compact triangle described by the two pairs of bounds above.
2. **Integrand:** $\rho(x,y)=2e^{x^2}$ is continuous on $D$.
3. **Theorem:** Theorem 1 permits both orders of integration.
4. **Conclusion:** we choose to integrate with respect to $y$ first because $\rho$ does not depend on $y$.

Thus

$$
M=\int_0^1\left(\int_0^x2e^{x^2}\,dy\right)dx
=\int_0^12x e^{x^2}\,dx
=e-1\approx1{,}718\ \text{kg}.
$$

In the other order, the inner integral would require an antiderivative of $e^{x^2}$, which is not elementary. Fubini does not change the value; it changes the difficulty.

**Example 3.** The signal “an impossible antiderivative appears in the given order” should prompt you to redraw the domain. For

$$
\int_0^1\left(\int_y^1e^{x^2}\,dx\right)dy,
$$

the triangle is still $0\le y\le x\le1$. The same checklist applies: compact domain, continuous integrand, Theorem 1, then vertical order. We obtain $\int_0^1x e^{x^2}\,dx=(e-1)/2$. This is exactly the mechanism of exercise `math-an-td7-02`.

## 3. Straighten an ellipse before calculating

**Tool 3 — adapt the coordinates to the shape.** Cartesian axes suit the triangle; an elliptical cross-section calls for a map built on its semi-axes.

Problem: describing an ellipse using slices produces square roots in the bounds.
We therefore need a transformation that maps a simple region onto the physical region.

**Definition 2 (new coordinates).** A <Def id="changement-de-variables">**change of variables**</Def> (a new map of the same domain) is a mapping $\Phi$ that associates with parameters $(u,v)$ a point $(x,y)=\Phi(u,v)$.

**Method 2 (transform without losing the domain).** Always write, in this order:

1. the parameter domain $Q$;
2. the formula for $\Phi$;
3. its exact image $\Phi(Q)=D$;
4. only then, the transformed integral.

**Example (physics) 4.** The cross-section of the beam is

$$
E=\left\{(x,y):\frac{x^2}{4}+y^2\le1\right\}.
$$

Take $Q=[0,1]\times[0,2\pi]$ and

$$
\Phi(r,\theta)=\bigl(2r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta\bigr).
$$

Then $\Phi(Q)=E$. The parameter $r$ measures the fraction of the semi-axis reached and $\theta$ rotates around the centre. These are <Terme id="coordonnees-polaires">polar coordinates</Terme>, stretched horizontally by a factor of $2$.

**Remark.** Merely saying “let $x=2r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$” is not enough. Without $0\le r\le1$ and $0\le\theta\le2\pi$, we know neither which region is covered nor how many times it is covered.

## 4. Correct the distortion of small areas

**Tool 4 — pay the area factor.** A map that stretches lengths cannot automatically preserve areas.

Problem: replacing only $x$ and $y$ transforms the integrand but ignores the local stretching of the small cells.
We therefore need a number that measures this stretching.

**Definition 3 (Jacobian).** For $\Phi(u,v)=(x(u,v),y(u,v))$, the <Def id="jacobien">**Jacobian**</Def> (the oriented factor of local area stretching) is the determinant

$$
J_\Phi(u,v)
=\det\begin{pmatrix}
\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u}&\dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v}\\
\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u}&\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Example (physics) 5.** For the elliptical map in Example (physics) 4,

$$
D\Phi(r,\theta)=
\begin{pmatrix}
2\cos\theta&-2r\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&r\cos\theta
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad J_\Phi(r,\theta)=2r.
$$

When the radius is doubled, a small polar cell covers twice as much area; near the centre, it narrows in proportion to $r$.

**Theorem 2 (change-of-variables formula).** If $\Phi$ is regular, bijective in the interiors of the domains and its Jacobian does not vanish there, then, for $f$ continuous on $D=\Phi(Q)$,

$$
\iint_Df(x,y)\,dx\,dy
=\iint_Qf\bigl(\Phi(u,v)\bigr)\,\left|J_\Phi(u,v)\right|\,du\,dv.
$$

The absolute value is mandatory: a map that reverses orientation does not create a negative area.

<Collapsible title="Why does the determinant measure an area?">

At a very small scale, $\Phi$ resembles the linear map given by its matrix $D\Phi$. The two sides of a small cell become the two columns of this matrix; the area of the resulting parallelogram is the absolute value of their determinant. Adding these small areas and then passing to the limit gives Theorem 2.

</Collapsible>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If $\Phi$ covers the interior of $D$ several times or if $J_\Phi$ vanishes there, Theorem 2 does not apply in this form. You must divide the domain or choose another map.

**Counterexample — the injectivity hypothesis.** The statement without injectivity is false. Take $\Phi(u,v)=(u^2,v)$ on $Q=[-1,1]\times[0,1]$. The continuity hypothesis is indeed satisfied because $\Phi$ is polynomial. But injectivity fails: almost every point in the image square has two preimages. Yet the naive conclusion gives $\iint_Q2|u|\,du\,dv=2$ instead of the square's area $1$.

For the beam, the checklist for Theorem 2 is as follows.

1. **Domain:** $Q=[0,1]\times[0,2\pi]$ and its image is the ellipse $E$; identifications on the boundary have no effect on the area.
2. **Regularity:** $\Phi$ is regular and bijective on the interiors, and $|J_\Phi|=2r$ is positive there.
3. **Theorem:** Theorem 2 applies to the continuous intensity of the beam.
4. **Conclusion:** the power is written

$$
P=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 100(1-r^2)\,2r\,dr\,d\theta.
$$

## Worked example — obtain the physical quantities without losing a factor

**Example 6 — moment of inertia of the plate.** Let us calculate $I_y$ with a complete solution.

**Step 1 — choose the vertical slices.** We keep $0\le x\le1$ and $0\le y\le x$.

*Why?* Method 1 requires us to choose the order before calculating; here the integrand does not depend on $y$ and the first antiderivative will be immediate.

**Step 2 — translate the physical quantity.** To the local mass $\rho(x,y)\,dx\,dy$, we apply the weight $x^2$, the square of the distance to the vertical axis:

$$
I_y=\iint_Dx^2\rho(x,y)\,dx\,dy.
$$

*Why?* Definition 1 adds a density; choosing $x^2\rho$ rather than $\rho$ distinguishes the moment of inertia from the mass.

**Step 3 — present the Fubini checklist.** The domain $D$ is compact; $x^2\rho(x,y)=2x^2e^{x^2}$ is continuous on it; Theorem 1 applies; therefore

$$
I_y=\int_0^1\left(\int_0^x2x^2e^{x^2}\,dy\right)dx
=\int_0^12x^3e^{x^2}\,dx.
$$

*Why?* Naming the hypotheses before the theorem avoids using Fubini as a mere permutation of symbols.

**Step 4 — finish with a substitution.** With $u=x^2$ and $du=2x\,dx$,

$$
I_y=\int_0^1u e^u\,du
=\left[(u-1)e^u\right]_0^1
=1\ \text{kg·m}^2.
$$

*Why?* The factor $x$ created by the length of the slice makes the substitution available; this is the reward for the right order.

**Example 7 — your turn, with less help.** Return to the power integral obtained after the checklist for Theorem 2.

**Your turn:** first calculate the integral with respect to $r$, then the one with respect to $\theta$, and check the unit.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

The four lines of the checklist have already checked the domain, regularity, Theorem 2 and the transformed expression. It remains to calculate

$$
P=200\int_0^{2\pi}\left[\frac{r^2}{2}-\frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^1d\theta
=50\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta
=100\pi\ \text{W}\approx314{,}2\ \text{W}.
$$

The factor $2r$ carries square centimetres; multiplied by watts per square centimetre, it does indeed leave watts.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Understanding question — answer">

**Why do Fubini and change of variables not solve the same problem?**

Fubini preserves the coordinates and only chooses the order of the slices of the same domain. A change of variables replaces the map of the domain; it must therefore correct the areas by $|J_\Phi|$. For the plate, changing the order is enough. For the ellipse, the new map directly reveals the reduced radius $r$.

</Collapsible>

## Guided visualisation

Here the visualisation is numerical: you describe the same plate using vertical and then horizontal slices, without a widget.

| Slice direction | Fixed variable | value $0$ | value $0{,}5$ | value $1$ |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| vertical | $x$ | length $0$ | length $0{,}5$ | length $1$ |
| horizontal | $y$ | length $1$ | length $0{,}5$ | length $0$ |

**Cycle 1 — reconstruct the same triangle.**

- **Predict.** Do the two rows of the table cover different regions or the same triangle?
- **Act.** For $x=0$, $0{,}5$, $1$, calculate the length of $0\le y\le x$; then, for the same values of $y$, calculate that of $y\le x\le1$.
- **Observe.** The lengths swap: one increases from $0$ to $1$, the other decreases from $1$ to $0$, but their endpoints remain on the diagonal $y=x$.
- **Connect.** These are the two descriptions in Example (physics) 2; Method 1 requires you to obtain them before any interchange.

**Cycle 2 — compare the cost of the calculation.**

- **Predict.** With $\rho(x,y)=2e^{x^2}$, which of the two inner integrals immediately provides the factor needed for a substitution?
- **Act.** Vertically, calculate $\int_0^x2e^{x^2}\,dy=2x e^{x^2}$. Horizontally, write only $\int_y^12e^{x^2}\,dx$ and look for an elementary antiderivative.
- **Observe.** The first order ends with the substitution $u=x^2$; the second gets stuck at the inner antiderivative.
- **Connect.** Theorem 1 guarantees the same value in both orders, but Tool 2 explains why only one is efficient here.

Choose other levels between $0$ and $1$ yourself: check the lengths, then try to predict the best order for a density that depends only on $y$.

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Writing the bounds before drawing.** The inequalities $0\le y\le x\le1$ do not become $0\le x\le y\le1$ when the order is reversed. The region does not change: horizontally, the correct bounds are $0\le y\le1$ followed by $y\le x\le1$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Interchanging only $dx\,dy$.** Reversing the order requires the Fubini checklist and a new reading of the domain. All four bounds must be reconstructed from the diagram.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the absolute value of the Jacobian.** The determinant may be negative if the map reverses orientation; an area remains positive. The formula in Theorem 2 therefore contains $|J_\Phi|$, never merely $J_\Phi$ by reflex.

</Pitfall>

**1. Mass of the plate.** The domain is $D=\{(x,y):0\le y\le x\le1\}$. The checklist in Example (physics) 2 verifies that $D$ is compact and that $\rho$ is continuous, then names Theorem 1. The right order gives

$$
M=\int_0^1\int_0^x2e^{x^2}\,dy\,dx=e-1\approx1{,}718\ \text{kg}.

P=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1100(1-r^2)\,2r\,dr\,d\theta

The plate therefore weighs approximately $1{,}718$ kg, its moment about the vertical axis is $1$ kg·m², and the elliptical cross-section transmits approximately $314{,}2$ W: the three questions in the mission have been answered, including units.

## Lesson summary

You now have the four capabilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Accumulate:** $\iint_Df(x,y)\,dx\,dy$ adds a local density over a plane region (Definition 1).
- **Slice:** describe $D$ vertically and horizontally, then choose the order whose inner antiderivative is simplest (Theorem 1 and Method 1).
- **Write up Fubini:** domain, continuity of the integrand, named theorem, conclusion — in that order.
- **Straighten:** state the parameter domain, the map and its image before transforming the integral (Definition 2 and Method 2).
- **Correct the areas:** multiply by $|J_\Phi|$ and check regularity, bijectivity and non-vanishing before applying Theorem 2.

</KeyResults>
