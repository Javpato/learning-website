# Deciding when sign cancellation is enough

<LessonMeta id="math-an-c04" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

Corrections that become smaller and smaller can stabilise thanks to their opposite signs—until a nonlinear response turns their tiny asymmetry into cumulative drift.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="Two paths of an interferometer in phase opposition, followed by a detector with a saturating response">

**Situation:** from index $n=2$ onwards, two paths of an interferometer produce the normalised correction $u_n=(-1)^n/\sqrt n$; instead, the saturating detector records $w_n=u_n/(1+u_n)$.
**Precise question:** does the cumulative reading of the $u_n$ settle, and after which index does the guaranteed error fall below $0{,}01$? Does the cumulative reading of the $w_n$ stay near a fixed value or drift without bound?
**The obstacle:** removing the signs gives an infinite total quantity, while saturation seems negligible because each correction becomes small.
**The contract:** this chapter provides 4 tools (testing without the signs, exploiting their regular opposition, separating a perturbation, summing by parts). By the end, we will decide the behaviour of both detector readings, with numbers to support the conclusion.

</Mission>

*Feeling confident? [Go straight to the TD (tutorial sheet)](../td-2/)—the theory will still be here when you get stuck.*

## The battle plan

1. **Remove the signs without losing the logical information**: know what the resulting test proves—and does not prove.
2. **Exploit a regular opposition**: check three hypotheses and turn the first omitted term into an error guarantee.
3. **Dissect a nonlinear response**: separate the main signal from the small term that may accumulate.
4. **Replace perfect alternation with bounded partial sums**: handle an arbitrary phase through discrete summation.

## 1. Removing the signs: a strong but one-way test

**Tool 1—test the series without its signs.** For a <Terme id="serie-numerique">numerical series</Terme>, removing the signs produces a positive series that the tests from the previous chapter can often classify.

Problem: terms with opposite signs can cancel, but a proof that ignores this cancellation must be stated precisely.
We therefore need a term for the case where the series remains finite even after all the signs have been removed.

**Definition 1 (test without the signs).** The series $\sum u_n$ has <Def id="convergence-absolue">**absolute convergence**</Def> (it still converges when cancellation is ignored) when the series $\sum |u_n|$ is a <Terme id="serie-convergente">convergent series</Terme>.

**Example 1.** The series

$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}$$

converges absolutely, because the series of absolute values is the <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> with exponent $2>1$.

**Theorem 1 (absolute convergence is sufficient).** If $\sum |u_n|$ converges, then $\sum u_n$ converges.

<Collapsible title="Proof—controlling the gaps between partial sums">

Let $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_n$. For $q>p$,

$$|S_q-S_p|=\left|\sum_{n=p+1}^{q}u_n\right|\le\sum_{n=p+1}^{q}|u_n|.$$

Since the series $\sum |u_n|$ converges, its positive <Terme id="reste-d-une-serie">remainder</Terme> becomes arbitrarily small: the right-hand side tends to $0$ as $p$ tends to infinity, uniformly in $q>p$. The <Terme id="somme-partielle">partial sums</Terme> $(S_N)$ therefore satisfy the Cauchy criterion: any two sufficiently late partial sums are as close as desired. Every real Cauchy sequence converges, so $\sum u_n$ converges. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If $\sum |u_n|$ diverges, Theorem 1 says **nothing** about $\sum u_n$. The signs may still save the series; they must then be exploited instead of drawing a hasty conclusion.

**Counterexample 1.** The statement “if $\sum |u_n|$ diverges, then $\sum u_n$ diverges” is false. Take $u_{2k-1}=1/k$ and $u_{2k}=-1/k$. The hypothesis that $\sum |u_n|$ diverges does hold because its even partial sums equal $2\sum_{k=1}^{K}1/k$ and the <Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme> diverges. Yet the conclusion that $\sum u_n$ diverges fails because $S_{2K}=0$ and $S_{2K-1}=1/K$ both tend to $0$.

**Example (physics) 2.** For the interferometer,

$$|u_n|=\frac1{\sqrt n}=\frac1{n^{1/2}}.$$

The associated positive series diverges, because it is a Riemann series with exponent $1/2\le1$. The absolute test therefore fails, but the previous Remark prevents us from declaring the raw reading divergent: the paths are precisely in phase opposition.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Exploiting a regular opposition and bounding the error

**Tool 2—bound partial sums that zigzag.** When the signs oppose each other at every index and the amplitudes decrease, the even and odd partial sums close in around the same number.

Problem: saying “the signs alternate” remains an intuition until the exact form of the term has been specified.
We therefore need a name for this pattern that can be recognised at a glance.

**Definition 2 (alternating signs).** An <Def id="serie-alternee">**alternating series**</Def> (the signs follow $+,-,+,-,\ldots$) is a series whose term can be written $u_n=(-1)^n a_n$ or $u_n=(-1)^{n+1}a_n$, with $a_n\ge0$.

**Example (physics) 3.** The raw signal satisfies $u_n=(-1)^n a_n$ with $a_n=1/\sqrt n$. One path contributes a positive correction, the other a negative correction, then the pattern begins again with a smaller amplitude.

**Remark (laboratory notation).** In a TP (lab practical), you would instead write $u_n=A_n\cos(n\pi)$ with $A_n=1/\sqrt n$ and a phase opposition of $\pi$ radians. It is the same object, expressed in amplitude–phase form rather than sign–modulus form.

Problem: alternation alone is not enough; we need to know which properties of the envelope $a_n$ actually force the two zigzags to meet.
We therefore need a test that turns three checks into both a verdict and error control.

**Theorem 2 (decreasing cancellation).** The <Def id="critere-de-leibniz">**Leibniz test**</Def> states that if, from some index onwards,

1. $a_n\ge0$;
2. $(a_n)$ is decreasing;
3. $a_n\to0$;

then the series $\sum (-1)^n a_n$ converges. Moreover, its remainder $R_N$ has the sign of the first omitted term and satisfies

$$|R_N|\le a_{N+1}.$$

<Collapsible title="Proof—two staircases towards the same limit">

After multiplying the whole series by $-1$ if necessary and ignoring finitely many terms, consider $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}(-1)^{n+1}a_n$ with $(a_n)$ decreasing to $0$.

The even partial sums can be written

$$S_{2p}=(a_1-a_2)+(a_3-a_4)+\cdots+(a_{2p-1}-a_{2p}).$$

They increase because each parenthesis is positive. The odd partial sums satisfy $S_{2p+1}=S_{2p}+a_{2p+1}$ and decrease. Furthermore,

$$S_{2p}\le S_{2p+1},\qquad S_{2p+1}-S_{2p}=a_{2p+1}\longrightarrow0.$$

The two adjacent sequences therefore have the same limit, and $(S_N)$ converges. Repeating the argument from index $N+1$, the sum of the tail remains between $0$ and its first term in absolute value: it has the same sign, and its modulus is at most $a_{N+1}$. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If the decrease of $a_n$ has not been established, Theorem 2 gives no verdict: look for a decrease from some index onwards, group terms cautiously, or move on to summation by parts in Tool 4. If $a_n$ does not tend to $0$, another tool—the necessary condition on the general term—directly yields divergence.

**Example (physics) 4.** Let us check the three hypotheses for $a_n=1/\sqrt n$ before stating the conclusion.

| Hypothesis of Theorem 2 | Check for the interferometer | Consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| positivity | $a_n=1/\sqrt n>0$ | the modulus really is an envelope |
| decrease | $a_{n+1}<a_n$ | the two zigzags close in |
| zero limit | $a_n\to0$ | the gap between them tends to $0$ |

Theorem 2 therefore gives convergence of $\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}u_n$ and

$$|R_N|\le\frac1{\sqrt{N+1}}.$$

To guarantee $|R_N|<0{,}01$, it is enough to have $1/\sqrt{N+1}<0{,}01$, that is, $N+1>10\,000$. The first integer guaranteed by this estimate is $N=10\,000$.

Problem: the raw signal converges while the series of its moduli diverges; this intermediate situation deserves a name.
We therefore need to distinguish convergence caused by cancellation from convergence that would survive the removal of the signs.

**Definition 3 (convergence due to the signs).** A series has <Def id="semi-convergence">**conditional convergence**</Def> (its convergence relies on cancellation) when it converges but does not converge absolutely.

**Example 5.** The interferometer series is conditionally convergent: Theorem 2 proves its convergence, while Example (physics) 2 proves the divergence of $\sum |u_n|$.

| Nature | Signed series | Series of moduli | Reference example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| absolute convergence | converges | converges | $\sum (-1)^n/n^2$ |
| conditional convergence | converges | diverges | $\sum (-1)^n/\sqrt n$ |
| divergence | diverges | necessarily diverges | $\sum 1/n$ |

## 3. Separating a perturbation that appears small

**Tool 3—isolate the term that can accumulate.** A transformation that is almost the identity can preserve the first-order term yet add a second-order term whose series diverges.

Problem: the saturating response $w_n=u_n/(1+u_n)$ resembles $u_n$ when $u_n$ is small, but a simple <Terme id="equivalent">asymptotic equivalent</Terme> $w_n\sim u_n$ does not classify a series whose signs change.
We therefore need an exact identity that separates what we already know from the new term.

**Method 1 (dissecting a nonlinear perturbation).** When $u_n\to0$ and $1+u_n\ne0$ from some index onwards:

1. subtract the main term $u_n$;
2. simplify the difference exactly;
3. study the series of the main term and that of the correction separately;
4. recombine the partial sums only after classifying both parts.

The central identity is

$$\frac{u_n}{1+u_n} =u_n-\frac{u_n^2}{1+u_n}.$$

**Example (physics) 6.** For $n\ge2$, $|u_n|\le1/\sqrt2<1$, so $1+u_n>0$ and the saturating detector is well defined. Set

$$v_n=\frac{u_n^2}{1+u_n}.$$

Then $v_n>0$ and, since $u_n\to0$,

$$v_n\sim u_n^2=\frac1n.$$

The symbol $\sim$ denotes the asymptotic equivalent learned in the toolbox: here it compares two positive terms. The series $\sum v_n$ diverges like the harmonic series. Thus, even though each $v_n$ tends to $0$, their cumulative sum becomes arbitrarily large.

**Remark (recognition signal).** Whenever a conditionally convergent series undergoes a transformation $\phi(u)=u+c\,u^2+o(u^2)$, immediately examine $\sum u_n^2$. If it diverges and the correction keeps one sign, the second-order term can change the nature of the transformed series.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** The equivalence $w_n\sim u_n$ does not preserve the nature of a series with variable signs. The equivalence test used for positive series requires eventual positivity; here $w_n$ and $u_n$ continue to change sign.

### Mini-exercises—tool 3

1. **The detector.** What positive term is responsible for the drift after separating $w_n$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   It is $v_n=u_n^2/(1+u_n)$. It is equivalent to $1/n$, so its series diverges.

   </Collapsible>

2. **Changing the scale.** If $u_n=(-1)^n/n$, what happens to the series of the correction $u_n^2/(1+u_n)$ from index $2$ onwards?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The correction is positive and equivalent to $1/n^2$. Its series therefore converges by comparison with the Riemann series with exponent $2$. This time, subtracting the correction does not destroy the convergence of $\sum u_n$.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. Summing by parts when the signs are no longer regular

**Tool 4—replace alternation with control of the partial sums.** An arbitrary phase no longer gives the pattern $+,-,+,-$, but its oscillations can keep the partial sums bounded.

Problem: in $\sum a_n b_n$, the oscillating factor $a_n$ does not necessarily alternate, while the factor $b_n$ decreases slowly.
We therefore need the discrete analogue of integration by parts: sum the oscillation and move the difference onto the envelope.

**Definition 4 (summation by parts).** <Def id="transformation-d-abel">**Abel summation**</Def> (integration by parts for sums) consists, for $A_k=\sum_{n=p}^{k}a_n$, in writing

$$\sum_{n=p}^{N}a_n b_n =A_Nb_N+\sum_{n=p}^{N-1}A_n(b_n-b_{n+1}).$$

<Collapsible title="Proof—revealing the difference of the envelope">

Since $a_n=A_n-A_{n-1}$, with $A_{p-1}=0$,

$$\sum_{n=p}^{N}a_n b_n =\sum_{n=p}^{N}(A_n-A_{n-1})b_n.$$

Shift the index in the second part:

$$\sum_{n=p}^{N}A_nb_n-\sum_{n=p}^{N}A_{n-1}b_n =A_Nb_N+\sum_{n=p}^{N-1}A_n(b_n-b_{n+1}).$$

This is exactly the stated formula. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Proposition 1 (bounded cumulative oscillation).** If the sums $B_N=\sum_{n=n_0}^{N}a_n$ are bounded and $(b_n)$ is positive, decreasing, and tends to $0$, then $\sum a_n b_n$ converges.

<Collapsible title="Proof—bounding every tail of the series">

Suppose $|B_N|\le M$. For a tail starting at index $p$, the partial sums $C_n=\sum_{k=p}^{n}a_k=B_n-B_{p-1}$ satisfy $|C_n|\le2M$. Abel summation gives, for $q\ge p$,

$$\left|\sum_{n=p}^{q}a_n b_n\right| \le2M b_q+2M\sum_{n=p}^{q-1}(b_n-b_{n+1}) =2M b_p.$$

Since $b_p\to0$, every tail becomes arbitrarily small. The Cauchy criterion gives convergence. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If the partial sums of $a_n$ are not bounded, or if $b_n$ does not decrease to $0$, Proposition 1 is inconclusive. This does not prove divergence: return to a comparison, a decomposition, or a better-suited estimate.

**Example (physics) 7.** If the paths of the interferometer have a phase shift $\theta$ that is not a multiple of $2\pi$, the signal becomes

$$u_n(\theta)=\frac{\cos(n\theta)}{\sqrt n}.$$

The signs no longer follow a regular alternation. Yet the identity

$$2\sin\!\left(\frac\theta2\right)\sum_{n=1}^{N}\cos(n\theta) =\sin\!\left(\left(N+\frac12\right)\theta\right)-\sin\!\left(\frac\theta2\right)$$

shows that the partial sums of $a_n=\cos(n\theta)$ are bounded. Since $b_n=1/\sqrt n$ is positive, decreasing, and tends to $0$, Proposition 1 gives convergence of $\sum u_n(\theta)$. This is the mechanism in exercise math-an-td2-06.

### Mini-exercises—tool 4

1. **Recovering perfect opposition.** For $\theta=\pi$, what values do the partial sums of $a_n=\cos(n\pi)$ take?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Since $a_n=(-1)^n$, the partial sums alternate between $-1$ and $0$. They are bounded, and Proposition 1 recovers the Leibniz verdict for $b_n=1/\sqrt n$.

   </Collapsible>

2. **Checking the passport.** For $\sum \sin(n\theta)/n$, with $\theta$ not a multiple of $2\pi$, what are the two decisive checks?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The partial sums of $\sin(n\theta)$ are bounded, while $1/n$ is positive, decreases, and tends to $0$. Proposition 1 applies. There is no need to look for an alternation that does not exist.

   </Collapsible>

## Worked example—the saturation reveals a harmonic term

**Example 8—handling the signature question from past papers.** For $n\ge2$, set

$$u_n=\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt n}, \qquad w_n=\frac{u_n}{1+u_n}.$$

Let us study $\sum u_n$, $\sum |u_n|$, and $\sum w_n$ in turn.

**Step 1—classify the raw series.** With $a_n=1/\sqrt n$, all three hypotheses of Theorem 2 hold; therefore $\sum u_n$ converges.

*Why?* An alternating sign does not constitute a proof: citing Theorem 2 forces us to check positivity, decrease, and the zero limit.

**Step 2—test convergence without the signs.** We have $|u_n|=1/n^{1/2}$, whose series diverges. By Definition 3, $\sum u_n$ is conditionally convergent.

*Why?* The nature of the raw series determines the main term, but the transformed question also depends on the cumulative size of its moduli.

**Step 3—secure the denominator.** For $n\ge2$, $|u_n|\le1/\sqrt2<1$, so $1+u_n>0$.

*Why?* Before any decomposition, we must check that the mathematical detector is defined and that the correction will indeed have a constant sign.

**Step 4—separate the perturbation exactly.** Method 1 gives

$$w_n=\frac{u_n}{1+u_n} =u_n-\frac{u_n^2}{1+u_n}.$$

*Why?* An asymptotic equivalent between signed terms does not classify their series; the exact identity lets us compare two sequences of partial sums separately.

**Step 5—classify the correction.** Set $v_n=u_n^2/(1+u_n)$. It is positive and

$$v_n\sim u_n^2=\frac1n.$$

The series $\sum v_n$ therefore diverges to $+\infty$ like the harmonic series.

*Why?* The first-order term $u_n$ cancels; the first term that can accumulate without cancellation is precisely the square.

**Step 6—recombine at the level of partial sums.** If

$$U_N=\sum_{n=2}^{N}u_n, \qquad V_N=\sum_{n=2}^{N}v_n, \qquad W_N=\sum_{n=2}^{N}w_n,$$

then $W_N=U_N-V_N$. Now $U_N$ tends to a finite number and $V_N\to+\infty$, so $W_N\to-\infty$: the transformed series diverges.

*Why?* The Definition of convergence concerns partial sums; it is at their level that “finite minus infinity” becomes a rigorous conclusion.

**Example 9—your turn, with less help.** Now set $u_n=(-1)^n/(n+1)$ for $n\ge1$ and $w_n=u_n/(1+u_n)$.

**Your turn:** repeat the exact decomposition, then decide whether the correction still destroys convergence.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

The Leibniz test gives convergence of $\sum u_n$, while $\sum |u_n|$ diverges like the harmonic series: the series is conditionally convergent.

Again, write

$$w_n=u_n-\frac{u_n^2}{1+u_n}.$$

This time,

$$\frac{u_n^2}{1+u_n}\sim u_n^2=\frac1{(n+1)^2}.$$

The series of the correction converges absolutely. As the difference of two convergent series, $\sum w_n$ converges. Since $|w_n|\sim1/(n+1)$, it does not converge absolutely: it remains conditionally convergent. This is the same method as in exercise math-an-td2-05, but the square is now summable.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Understanding question—answer">

**Why is the information $w_n\sim u_n$ insufficient, when equivalence classified positive series so well?**

Because the comparison theorem by asymptotic equivalents relies on the absence of cancellation: its terms must be positive from some index onwards. Here, $u_n$ and $w_n$ change sign. Their first-order terms cancel, while the difference $u_n-w_n$, which is always positive, accumulates. The exact identity reveals this second-order information that the equivalent erases.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**“The series of absolute values diverges, so the series diverges.”** No: Theorem 1 is one-way. The interferometer series is precisely a counterexample; when the absolute test fails, examine the signs.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Applying Leibniz because the signs alternate.** Two checks remain: the amplitude must decrease and tend to $0$. For $w_n=u_n/(1+u_n)$, the moduli move in a sawtooth pattern; citing Leibniz without checking decrease hides the divergent quadratic term.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Using $w_n\sim u_n$ on a signed series.** Equivalence preserves the nature of positive series under its hypotheses, not that of series in which cancellation decides everything. Decompose the perturbation exactly.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Treating Abel as a converse.** Bounded oscillating partial sums and an envelope decreasing to $0$ are sufficient; if a hypothesis is missing, the method is merely inconclusive. It does not automatically imply divergence.

</Pitfall>

<MissionSolved>

**1. Classify the raw reading.** For $u_n=(-1)^n/\sqrt n$, the amplitude $a_n=1/\sqrt n$ is positive, decreasing, and tends to $0$. Theorem 2 therefore gives convergence of $\sum_{n=2}^{+\infty}u_n$. On the other hand, $\sum |u_n|=\sum1/\sqrt n$ diverges: by Definition 3, the raw signal is conditionally convergent.

**2. Guarantee the requested error.** Theorem 2 gives

$$|R_N|\le\frac1{\sqrt{N+1}}.$$

At index $N=9\,999$, this bound is exactly $0{,}01$ and does not guarantee the strict inequality. At index $N=10\,000$,

$$|R_{10\,000}|\le\frac1{\sqrt{10\,001}}<0{,}01.$$

The first certified index is therefore **$N=10\,000$**.

**3. Classify the saturated reading.** For $n\ge2$, Method 1 applies and gives

$$w_n=u_n-\frac{u_n^2}{1+u_n}.$$

The series of the first part converges, while the second part is positive and equivalent to $1/n$; its partial sums tend to $+\infty$. Consequently, the partial sums $W_N$ tend to $-\infty$.

The raw reading settles and its error is guaranteed to be below **$0{,}01$ after index $10\,000$**; the saturating detector reading, by contrast, **drifts without bound towards negative values**. The small nonlinearity does not disappear: its quadratic term accumulates.

</MissionSolved>

## Lesson summary

You now have the four capabilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Test without the signs:** if $\sum |u_n|$ converges, then $\sum u_n$ converges; if $\sum |u_n|$ diverges, no verdict follows.
- **Recognise conditional convergence:** the signed series converges, but the series of its moduli diverges; cancellation is essential.
- **Apply Leibniz:** write $u_n=(-1)^n a_n$, then check $a_n\ge0$, that it is decreasing, and that its limit is zero.
- **Control the error:** for an alternating series satisfying these hypotheses, $|R_N|\le a_{N+1}$ and the remainder has the sign of the first omitted term.
- **Handle saturation:** use the identity $u_n/(1+u_n)=u_n-u_n^2/(1+u_n)$, then classify the two series separately.
- **Move to an irregular phase:** sum the oscillating factor and apply Abel summation when these partial sums are bounded and the envelope decreases to $0$.
- **Write the solution:** identify the signal, verify the hypotheses, name the theorem, rule out the inconclusive case, then give an explicit verdict on the partial sums.

</KeyResults>
