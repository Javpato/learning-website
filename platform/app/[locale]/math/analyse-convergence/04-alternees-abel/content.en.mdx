<GuidingQuestion>

The series $\sum(-1)^{n+1}/n$ converges although the sum of the distances $\sum1/n$ diverges. **How can signs create a convergence, how is that cancellation measured, and how far does it survive a perturbation?**

</GuidingQuestion>

## 1. Removing the signs: a stronger test

With signed terms, two phenomena are superimposed: their size and their cancellation. The first reflex, facing a <Terme id="serie-numerique">numerical series</Terme>, is to separate those questions.

Problem: we must name the case where convergence does not depend on the signs.

**Definition 1 (absolute convergence).** The series $\sum u_n$ has <Def id="convergence-absolue">**absolute convergence**</Def> when $\sum|u_n|$ is a <Terme id="serie-convergente">convergent series</Terme>.

**Theorem 1.** Every absolutely convergent series converges.

<Proof title="controlling every signed tail by the tail of the moduli">

Write $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_n$. For $q>p$, the triangle inequality gives

$$
|S_q-S_p|
=\left|\sum_{n=p+1}^{q}u_n\right|
\le\sum_{n=p+1}^{q}|u_n|.
$$

Since $\sum|u_n|$ converges, its <Terme id="reste-d-une-serie">remainder</Terme> becomes arbitrarily small. The right-hand side is therefore below any $\varepsilon>0$ for $p$ large enough, uniformly in $q>p$.

The <Terme id="somme-partielle">partial sums</Terme> $(S_N)$ satisfy the Cauchy criterion proved in L01; the series $\sum u_n$ converges.

**What the proof teaches.** The absolute value destroys the cancellations but supplies a control strong enough for every signed series. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If $\sum|u_n|$ diverges, Theorem 1 says nothing about $\sum u_n$. The signs may still produce a convergence.

**Example 1.** The series

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}
$$

converges absolutely, since the series of moduli is a <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> of exponent $2>1$.

### Mini-exercises

1. Does the series $\sum(-1)^n/n^{3/2}$ converge absolutely?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Yes: its series of moduli is a Riemann series of exponent $3/2>1$.

   </Collapsible>

2. If $\sum|u_n|$ diverges, may we assert that $\sum u_n$ diverges?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No. That is precisely the inconclusive case of Theorem 1.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Leibniz: making the zigzag rigorous

A regular alternation makes the partial sums oscillate. If each step is shorter than the previous one, the two sides of the zigzag can meet.

Problem: we must distinguish "the signs change" from the hypotheses that really force convergence.

**Definition 2 (alternating series).** An <Def id="serie-alternee">**alternating series**</Def> has the form

$$
\sum(-1)^n a_n
\quad\text{or}\quad
\sum(-1)^{n+1}a_n,\qquad a_n\ge0.
$$

**Theorem 2 (Leibniz criterion).** The <Def id="critere-de-leibniz">**Leibniz criterion**</Def> asserts that, if

1. $a_n\ge0$;
2. $(a_n)$ is decreasing from some index on;
3. $a_n\to0$;

then $\sum(-1)^{n+1}a_n$ converges. Moreover its remainder satisfies

$$
|R_N|\le a_{N+1}
$$

and has the sign of the first omitted term.

<Proof title="making the even and odd staircases converge">

Discarding a finite beginning if necessary, assume $(a_n)$ decreasing from $n=1$.

The even partial sums read

$$
S_{2p}=(a_1-a_2)+(a_3-a_4)+\cdots +(a_{2p-1}-a_{2p}).
$$

They increase, since each bracket is positive. Moreover,

$$
S_{2p+2}-S_{2p}=a_{2p+1}-a_{2p+2}\ge0.
$$

The odd sums satisfy $S_{2p+1}=S_{2p}+a_{2p+1}$ and decrease. We always have

$$
S_{2p}\le S_{2p+1},
\qquad
S_{2p+1}-S_{2p}=a_{2p+1}\to0.
$$

The two <Terme id="suites-adjacentes">adjacent sequences</Terme> therefore have the same limit; the whole sequence $(S_N)$ converges. Repeating the same bracketing from the first omitted term, the sum of the tail stays between $0$ and that term in absolute value: $|R_N|\le a_{N+1}$.

**What the proof teaches.** The three hypotheses have distinct roles: the sign creates two subsequences, the decrease orders them, the zero limit closes their gap. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If the decrease does not hold, Leibniz permits no verdict. Look for a decrease from some index on, a decomposition, or the Abel transformation; do not declare divergence.

**Definition 3 (conditional convergence).** A series has <Def id="semi-convergence">**conditional convergence**</Def> when it converges but does not converge absolutely.

**Example 2.** The alternating harmonic series

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}n
$$

converges by Leibniz, since $1/n$ decreases to $0$. It does not converge absolutely, since $\sum1/n$ is the <Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme>. It is therefore conditionally convergent.

For its approximation $S_N$,

$$
|R_N|\le\frac1{N+1}.
$$

So $N=100$ guarantees an error strictly below $0{,}01$.

### Mini-exercises

1. For which $\alpha>0$ does the series $\sum(-1)^n/n^\alpha$ converge? And absolutely?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   It converges for every $\alpha>0$ by Leibniz. It converges absolutely exactly for $\alpha>1$. For $0<\alpha\le1$ it is conditionally convergent.

   </Collapsible>

2. The sequence $a_n=2+1/n$ is positive and decreasing. Does Leibniz apply to $\sum(-1)^n a_n$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No: $a_n$ does not tend to $0$. The series even diverges by the necessary condition on the general term.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. A perturbation small term by term can accumulate

This section settles the difficulty flagged in the past papers: an asymptotic expansion classifies a series only after classifying **each** piece and justifying its remainder.

Let us study

$$
u_n=\frac{(-1)^n}{n+(-1)^n\sqrt n},\qquad n\ge2.
$$

At first sight the signs alternate. Yet

$$
|u_n|=\frac1{n+(-1)^n\sqrt n}
$$

is not decreasing: the denominators jump with the parity. Leibniz is therefore unavailable.

**Method 1 (separate until you reach known series).** Set $x_n=(-1)^n/\sqrt n$. The exact identity

$$
\frac1{1+x}=1-x+\frac{x^2}{1+x}
$$

gives

$$
u_n
=\frac{(-1)^n}{n}
-\frac1{n^{3/2}}
+r_n,
$$

with

$$
r_n=\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2\left(1+(-1)^n/\sqrt n\right)}.
$$

For $n$ large enough the denominator is at least $1/2$, so

$$
|r_n|\le\frac2{n^2}.
$$

In other words $r_n=O(1/n^2)$ in the sense defined in L03, and $\sum r_n$ converges absolutely by comparison.

The three pieces are now classified:

1. $\sum(-1)^n/n$ converges by Leibniz;
2. $\sum1/n^{3/2}$ converges by Riemann;
3. $\sum r_n$ converges absolutely by the bound above.

So $\sum u_n$ converges. On the other hand $|u_n|\sim1/n$; the series does not converge absolutely.

**Remark.** The sentence "the term $O(1/n^2)$ converges" is improper. We defined a sequence $r_n$, proved $|r_n|\le2/n^2$, then applied a comparison to the series $\sum|r_n|$.

**Counterexample 1 (signed equivalents do not transmit behaviour).** Set

$$
a_n=\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt n},
\qquad
b_n=a_n+\frac1n.
$$

We have $b_n/a_n\to1$, so $a_n$ and $b_n$ are <Terme id="equivalent">equivalent</Terme>. Yet $\sum a_n$ converges by Leibniz while $\sum b_n$ diverges: its partial sums are the sum of a convergent sequence and of harmonic sums tending to $+\infty$.

### Mini-exercises

1. Why would $O(1/n)$ not suffice in the decomposition above?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because a sequence $r_n=1/n$ is indeed $O(1/n)$ but its series diverges. The exponent strictly greater than $1$ is decisive.

   </Collapsible>

2. True or false: if $\sum u_n$ and $\sum v_n$ converge, then $\sum u_nv_n$ converges.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   False. Take $u_n=v_n=(-1)^n/\sqrt n$. Both series converge by Leibniz, but $u_nv_n=1/n$ produces the divergent harmonic series.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. Abel: replacing alternation by bounded running totals

Leibniz handles the regular pattern $+,-,+,-$. For oscillations such as $\cos(n\theta)$ the signs do not always alternate, but their running totals may stay confined to an interval.

Problem: "bounded partial sums" must concern the oscillating factor. If $A_N=\sum_{n=p}^{N}a_n$, this means that there exists $M$ with $|A_N|\le M$ for every $N\ge p$.

**Definition 4 (Abel transformation).** The <Def id="transformation-d-abel">**Abel transformation**</Def> is the finite identity

$$
\sum_{n=p}^{N}a_nb_n
=A_Nb_N+\sum_{n=p}^{N-1}A_n(b_n-b_{n+1}),
\qquad
A_n=\sum_{k=p}^{n}a_k.
$$

<Proof title="an integration by parts for sums">

Since $a_n=A_n-A_{n-1}$ and $A_{p-1}=0$,

$$
\sum_{n=p}^{N}a_nb_n
=\sum_{n=p}^{N}(A_n-A_{n-1})b_n.
$$

Separate the two sums and shift the index in the second. The interior terms regroup into $A_n(b_n-b_{n+1})$; the right boundary gives $A_Nb_N$. We obtain exactly the announced identity.

**What the proof teaches.** We accumulate the oscillating factor and move the variation onto the regular factor. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Theorem 3 (Abel–Dirichlet criterion).** If the partial sums of $\sum a_n$ are bounded and if $(b_n)$ is positive, decreasing and tends to $0$, then $\sum a_nb_n$ converges.

<Proof title="making every tail small with Abel">

Suppose the global partial sums of $a_n$ are bounded by $M$. For a tail starting at index $p$, its running totals

$$
C_n=\sum_{k=p}^{n}a_k
$$

are differences of two global partial sums, so $|C_n|\le2M$.

The Abel transformation applied between $p$ and $q$ gives

$$
\left|\sum_{n=p}^{q}a_nb_n\right|
\le2M b_q+2M\sum_{n=p}^{q-1}(b_n-b_{n+1})
=2M b_p.
$$

Since $b_p\to0$, every tail becomes arbitrarily small. The Cauchy criterion gives convergence.

**What the proof teaches.** "Bounded" means here controlled by one common constant; that constant becomes directly a bound on the tail. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If the running totals of $a_n$ are not bounded, or if $b_n$ does not decrease to $0$, this theorem concludes nothing. The failure of a hypothesis is not a proof of divergence.

**Example 3.** For $\theta\notin2\pi\mathbb Z$, the partial sums of $a_n=e^{in\theta}$ are geometric:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{in\theta}
=e^{i\theta}\frac{1-e^{iN\theta}}{1-e^{i\theta}}.
$$

Their modulus is bounded independently of $N$. Since $b_n=1/n$ decreases to $0$, Theorem 3 gives the convergence of $\sum e^{in\theta}/n$, hence of its real and imaginary parts. This is the mechanism of math-an-td2-06.

## Worked example — a complete write-up for an alternating series

Let us study

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}(-1)^{n+1}\sin\left(\frac1{\sqrt n}\right).
$$

**Step 1 — set the amplitude.** $a_n=\sin(1/\sqrt n)>0$.

*Why?* Leibniz requires a series written as an alternating sign times a positive amplitude.

**Step 2 — check the limit.** Since $1/\sqrt n\to0$ and $\sin$ is continuous, $a_n\to0$.

*Why?* Alternation alone does not make a series converge.

**Step 3 — check the decrease.** The sequence $1/\sqrt n$ decreases and $\sin$ increases on $[0,1]$; their composition decreases.

*Why?* This is the hypothesis the past papers trap most often.

**Step 4 — conclude convergence.** The three hypotheses of Theorem 2 hold; the series converges.

*Why?* An exam conclusion cites the theorem only after its passport of hypotheses.

**Step 5 — study absolute convergence.** Since $\sin x\sim x$ at $0$,

$$
|u_n|\sim\frac1{\sqrt n}.
$$

The series of moduli diverges by comparison with Riemann of exponent $1/2$. The series is conditionally convergent.

*Why?* "Converges" and "converges absolutely" are two distinct questions.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**Why does the decrease intervene in the proof of Leibniz?**

It makes the even sums increasing and the odd sums decreasing. Without that order, the zigzag no longer supplies two adjacent sequences.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Concluding "divergent" because absolute convergence fails.** That only proves that Theorem 1 no longer concludes.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Seeing $(-1)^n$ and citing Leibniz immediately.** A positive amplitude, decreasing and with zero limit, is still required.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Using an equivalent for a signed series.** Comparison by an equivalent requires positivity; separate the terms instead, with an identity or a controlled expansion.

</Pitfall>

<GuidingAnswer>

Signs can make a series converge by organising its partial sums around one same limit. Absolute convergence is robust; conditional convergence depends on the cancellations. Leibniz quantifies the regular zigzag, Abel replaces alternation by bounded running totals, and every perturbation must be decomposed down to series whose behaviour is genuinely proved.

</GuidingAnswer>

## Summary — robustness and cancellation

<KeyResults title="The decisions worth being able to write">

| Situation | Hypotheses | Conclusion | If they fail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\sum\lvert u_n\rvert $ converges | none other | $\sum u_n$ converges | divergence of the moduli: no verdict |
| $(-1)^n a_n$ | $a_n\ge0$, decreasing, $a_n\to0$ | convergence and $\lvert R_N\rvert \le a_{N+1}$ | change tool |
| expansion with $r_n$ | explicit bound on $\lvert r_n\rvert $ | compare the series of the remainder | an O symbol alone is not a proof |
| $a_nb_n$ | running totals of $a_n$ bounded; $b_n\downarrow0$ | convergence by Abel–Dirichlet | no automatic conclusion |

**Proofs worth being able to reconstruct:** bounding a signed tail by the moduli; separating even and odd sums; turning a big O into an inequality; deriving Abel summation and then bounding its tails.

</KeyResults>
