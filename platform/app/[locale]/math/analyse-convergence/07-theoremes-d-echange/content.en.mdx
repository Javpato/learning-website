<GuidingQuestion>

Integrating a **finite** sum means integrating each term. Differentiating a finite sum means differentiating each term. These two obvious facts cost nothing and require no hypothesis. **What must be added for them to remain true when the sum no longer has a last term?**

</GuidingQuestion>

## 1. Two limit processes do not commute

Before looking for theorems, we must see that the problem is real: nothing guarantees a priori that two limits can be interchanged.

Problem: an integral is a limit, a derivative is a limit, an infinite sum is a limit. Every "interchange" in this lesson is therefore, in reality, the interchange of **two** limit processes — and there is nothing automatic about that.

**Example 1 (two limits that do not commute).** For $p,q\ge1$, set

$$
a_{p,q}=\frac{p}{p+q}.
$$

With $q$ fixed, $a_{p,q}\to1$ as $p\to+\infty$; the limit in $q$ of those values is therefore $1$. With $p$ fixed, $a_{p,q}\to0$ as $q\to+\infty$; the limit in $p$ of those values is therefore $0$. Hence

$$
\lim_{q\to\infty}\lim_{p\to\infty}a_{p,q}=1
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\lim_{p\to\infty}\lim_{q\to\infty}a_{p,q}=0 .
$$

Both computations concern the same array of numbers and give two different results. No mistake has been made: it is the order of the two limits that decides.

**Remark.** Remember the shape of the obstacle. In $a_{p,q}$, everything depends on the **competition** between $p$ and $q$; depending on which one wins, the answer changes. Every counterexample in this lesson will have that shape: a quantity concentrated on a region whose size depends on the index.

This lesson therefore does not give computation rules, but **licences**: each theorem authorises one precise interchange, under precise hypotheses, and stops saying anything at all as soon as one hypothesis is missing.

### Mini-exercises

1. Why does Example 1 contradict no theorem?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because no theorem asserts that two limits commute without hypotheses. The example shows that the general statement is false, which justifies the existence of the hypotheses we are about to add.

   </Collapsible>

2. In what sense is $\displaystyle\int_a^b f$ a limit?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   It is the limit of Riemann sums as the mesh of the subdivision tends to $0$. Interchanging a limit and an integral is therefore indeed interchanging two limits — and Example 1 says that this is never free.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Carrying continuity across

The first interchange has already been proved: it is Theorem 2 of lesson 05, which says that a **uniform** limit of continuous functions is continuous. We shall not reprove it, but adjust it to the form in which it is really used.

Problem: uniform convergence on a whole open interval is rare, whereas continuity is only read in a neighbourhood of a point. Demanding a global control for a local property would be asking too much.

**Theorem 1 (continuity, local version).** Let $I$ be an interval and $f_n$ functions continuous on $I$. If $f_n\to f$ uniformly **on every segment of $I$**, then $f$ is continuous on $I$.

<Proof title="continuity is a local property">

**Idea.** Being continuous at $x_0$ concerns only what happens near $x_0$. It therefore suffices to find a segment of $I$ containing a neighbourhood of $x_0$, and to apply the result already proved on it.

Let $x_0\in I$. Choose $\eta>0$ such that $J=[x_0-\eta,x_0+\eta]\cap I$ is a segment containing a neighbourhood of $x_0$ in $I$ — this is possible even if $x_0$ is an endpoint of $I$, the segment then lying on one side only.

By hypothesis $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $J$, and each $f_n$ is continuous there. Theorem 2 of lesson 05 gives the continuity of $f$ on $J$, hence in particular at $x_0$. Since $x_0$ was arbitrary, $f$ is continuous on $I$.

**What the proof teaches.** We trade one global hypothesis for a family of local ones, losing nothing, **because the intended conclusion is itself local**. That trade would not be possible for a global conclusion: this is exactly why it does not give uniform convergence on $I$. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** "Uniform on every segment of $I$" does not imply "uniform on $I$": that is the warning of lesson 05, and it remains true here. We gain the continuity of the sum, not a global control of the gap.

**Example 2.** The <Terme id="serie-geometrique">geometric series</Terme> $\sum_{n\ge0}x^n$ converges normally on every segment $[-r,r]$ with $r<1$, since $\|x^n\|_\infty=r^n$ on that segment and $\sum r^n$ converges. Theorem 1 therefore gives the continuity of its sum $1/(1-x)$ on $(-1,1)$ — without the convergence being uniform on the whole of $(-1,1)$.

### Mini-exercises

1. The convergence is uniform on $[a,b]$ for every $[a,b]\subset(0,1)$. What can be asserted on $(0,1)$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   That the limit is continuous on $(0,1)$, by Theorem 1. Nothing can be asserted about uniformity of the convergence on $(0,1)$, nor about the behaviour at $0$ and at $1$.

   </Collapsible>

2. A pointwise limit of continuous functions is continuous. Can uniform convergence on segments be deduced?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No. The converse of Theorem 1 is false, exactly like that of Theorem 2 of lesson 05: the <Terme id="bosse-glissante">travelling bump</Terme> $nxe^{-nx}$ has a zero, hence continuous, limit without uniform convergence near $0$.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. Passing the limit under the integral sign

Problem: we know the areas $\int f_n$ and would like the area of $f$. But an area can hide in a shrinking region — Example 1 warned us.

**Definition 1 (interchange of limit and integral).** The <Def id="interversion-limite-integrale">**interchange of limit and integral**</Def> is the equality

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_a^b f_n(x)\,dx=\int_a^b\Bigl(\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n(x)\Bigr)dx,
$$

when a theorem guarantees its hypotheses. It is never a computation rule acquired in advance.

**Theorem 2 (passing to the limit under the integral, on a segment).** Let $f_n$ be continuous on a **segment** $[a,b]$, converging uniformly to $f$ on $[a,b]$. Then $f$ is continuous, it is integrable, and

$$
\int_a^b f_n(x)\,dx\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx .
$$

<Proof title="an area is bounded by a height times a length">

**Idea.** The gap between two areas is bounded by the area of the gap. But the gap is bounded **everywhere at once** by the <Terme id="norme-uniforme">uniform norm</Terme>: so we replace an unknown function by a rectangle.

The continuity of $f$ comes from Theorem 1. Then, by linearity and the triangle inequality for integrals,

$$
\left|\int_a^b f_n-\int_a^b f\right|=\left|\int_a^b (f_n-f)\right|\le\int_a^b|f_n-f| .
$$

On $[a,b]$ the function $|f_n-f|$ is bounded by the constant $\|f_n-f\|_\infty$, so its integral is bounded by the area of the corresponding rectangle:

$$
\left|\int_a^b f_n-\int_a^b f\right|\le(b-a)\,\|f_n-f\|_\infty .
$$

The factor $(b-a)$ is **finite** and the second factor tends to $0$: the product tends to $0$.

**What the proof teaches.** The whole demonstration sits in the product "length × height". It is that product which explains why the segment hypothesis is not decoration: on an unbounded interval the length factor is infinite, and a height tending to $0$ is no longer enough. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Counterexample 1 (pointwise convergence is not enough, even on a segment).** On $[0,1]$, take the triangular bump of height $n$ centred at $1/n$ with base $2/n$:

$$
h_n(x)=n\max\Bigl(0,\;1-|nx-1|\Bigr).
$$

For fixed $x>0$ the bump eventually passes to the left of $x$, so $h_n(x)\to0$; and $h_n(0)=0$. The pointwise limit is zero. Yet the area of a triangle of base $2/n$ and height $n$ equals

$$
\int_0^1h_n(x)\,dx=\frac12\cdot\frac2n\cdot n=1\qquad\text{for every }n .
$$

So $\lim\int h_n=1$ whereas $\int\lim h_n=0$. **The implication that fails is "pointwise convergence $\Rightarrow$ interchange".** And this is consistent with Theorem 2: here $\|h_n\|_\infty=n$, so the convergence is not uniform.

**Counterexample 2 (on an unbounded interval, uniformity is not enough).** On $[0,+\infty)$, take

$$
g_n(x)=\frac1n\,e^{-x/n}.
$$

We have $\|g_n\|_\infty=1/n\to0$, so $g_n\to0$ **uniformly** on $[0,+\infty)$. Yet

$$
\int_0^{+\infty}g_n(x)\,dx=\frac1n\cdot n=1\qquad\text{for every }n .
$$

**The implication that fails is "uniform convergence $\Rightarrow$ interchange on an arbitrary interval".** The mass does not disappear: it spreads out towards infinity, exactly where the factor $(b-a)$ of the proof becomes infinite.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Off a segment, Theorem 2 says nothing — in either direction. The tool that survives relies on a <Terme id="hypothese-de-domination">domination hypothesis</Terme>: one bounds $|f_n(t)|$ by a fixed integrable function, independent of $n$, which forbids the mass to escape. That is the dominated convergence theorem, named here and worked in lesson 10.

### Mini-exercises

1. On $[0,1]$ we know $\|f_n-f\|_\infty\le5/n$ and the $f_n$ are continuous. What is $\lim\int_0^1f_n$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The convergence is uniform on a segment, so Theorem 2 applies: the limit equals $\int_0^1f$. We even have the rate, since the gap between the areas is bounded by $1\cdot5/n$.

   </Collapsible>

2. In Counterexample 2, where exactly does the proof of Theorem 2 break down?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   At the last line: the bound $(b-a)\|f_n-f\|_\infty$ becomes $+\infty\times0$, which is not a bound. The triangle inequality itself remains true.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. Integrating a series term by term

Problem: a series is a sequence of partial sums, and each partial sum can be integrated term by term without difficulty since it is **finite**. It remains to know whether we may pass to the limit in that identity.

**Definition 2 (term-by-term integration).** <Def id="integration-terme-a-terme">**Term-by-term integration**</Def> is the equality

$$
\int_a^b\sum_{n\ge0}u_n(x)\,dx=\sum_{n\ge0}\int_a^b u_n(x)\,dx .
$$

**Theorem 3 (term-by-term integration on a segment).** Let $u_n$ be continuous on $[a,b]$. If $\sum u_n$ converges uniformly on $[a,b]$ — in particular if it converges <Terme id="convergence-normale">normally</Terme> there — then its sum $S$ is continuous and the equality of Definition 2 holds.

<Proof title="prove the finite identity, and only then pass to the limit">

**Idea.** The order of the two moves is the whole proof: we first write an **exact** identity between finite objects, then apply Theorem 2 to it.

For every $N$, the linearity of the integral over a **finite** sum gives

$$
\int_a^b S_N(x)\,dx=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\int_a^b u_n(x)\,dx .
$$

This equality requires no hypothesis: it concerns $N+1$ terms.

By assumption $S_N\to S$ uniformly on the segment $[a,b]$, and each $S_N$ is continuous as a finite sum of continuous functions. Theorem 2 therefore applies to the sequence $(S_N)$:

$$
\int_a^b S_N\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{}\int_a^b S .
$$

The left-hand side is the partial sum of the numerical series $\sum\int u_n$. That series therefore converges, and its sum equals $\int_a^b S$.

**What the proof teaches.** The pattern is reusable as it stands: *write a finite identity, check a uniform convergence, pass to the limit*. We never manipulate an infinite sum directly; we build it as a limit of finite sums. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Example 3 (manufacturing an identity).** For $|x|<1$, the series $\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^nx^{2n}$ is geometric with ratio $-x^2$ and sum $1/(1+x^2)$. Fix $t$ with $0\le t<1$. On $[0,t]$ the convergence is normal, since $\|(-1)^nx^{2n}\|_\infty=t^{2n}$ and $\sum t^{2n}$ converges. Theorem 3 therefore authorises

$$
\arctan t=\int_0^t\frac{dx}{1+x^2}=\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^n\int_0^t x^{2n}\,dx=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n t^{2n+1}}{2n+1}.
$$

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Example 3 says nothing at $t=1$: the segment $[0,1]$ is not contained in the interval where normal convergence was proved. Obtaining Leibniz's formula $\pi/4=1-\tfrac13+\tfrac15-\cdots$ requires an extra argument at the boundary, which lesson 08 will put in place.

### Mini-exercises

1. Why does Theorem 3 require uniform convergence of the **series**, and not of the sequence $(u_n)$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because it is applied to the sequence of partial sums $(S_N)$, not to $(u_n)$. Having $u_n\to0$ uniformly is a necessary condition of convergence, very far from sufficient — the same distinction as between $u_n\to0$ and the convergence of a numerical series.

   </Collapsible>

2. Deduce from Example 3 the expansion of $-\ln(1-t)$ for $0\le t<1$.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   On $[0,t]$, $\sum_{n\ge0}x^n$ converges normally to $1/(1-x)$, so

   $$
   -\ln(1-t)=\int_0^t\frac{dx}{1-x}=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{t^{n+1}}{n+1}.
   $$

   </Collapsible>

## 5. Differentiating a series term by term

This is the hardest point of the lesson, and the one most often written badly in exams.

**Counterexample 3 (uniform convergence transmits nothing to the derivatives).** On $\mathbb R$, set $\varphi_n(x)=\sqrt{x^2+1/n^2}$. Each $\varphi_n$ is of class $C^\infty$, and lesson 05 showed that $\varphi_n\to|x|$ **uniformly** on $\mathbb R$, with $\|\varphi_n-|\cdot|\,\|_\infty=1/n$. Yet the limit is not differentiable at $0$.

**The implication that fails is "uniform convergence of the functions $\Rightarrow$ differentiability of the limit".** The reason is visible: the derivative $\varphi_n'(x)=x/\sqrt{x^2+1/n^2}$ goes from $-1$ to $1$ over an interval of width of order $1/n$. The derivatives themselves do not converge uniformly.

Problem: we must therefore control the **derivatives**, not the functions. But then, how do we recover the function itself?

**Definition 3 (term-by-term differentiation).** <Def id="derivation-terme-a-terme">**Term-by-term differentiation**</Def> is the equality

$$
\Bigl(\sum_{n\ge0}u_n\Bigr)'=\sum_{n\ge0}u_n' .
$$

**Theorem 4 (term-by-term differentiation).** Let $I$ be an interval and $u_n$ functions of class $C^1$ on $I$. Assume:

1. the series $\sum u_n(x_0)$ converges **at at least one point** $x_0$ of $I$;
2. the series of derivatives $\sum u_n'$ converges uniformly on every segment of $I$.

Then $\sum u_n$ converges on $I$, its sum $S$ is of class $C^1$, and $S'=\sum u_n'$.

<Proof title="control the slopes, then come back up through an integral">

**Idea.** We control only the series of derivatives. So we rebuild the function by integrating its slopes from an anchor point, where a single numerical value suffices to fix the constant.

Write $T=\sum u_n'$. Each $u_n'$ is continuous and the convergence is uniform on every segment, so Theorem 1 gives that $T$ is **continuous** on $I$.

Fix $x\in I$. On the segment with endpoints $x_0$ and $x$, the convergence of $\sum u_n'$ is uniform, so Theorem 3 applies:

$$
\int_{x_0}^{x}T(t)\,dt=\sum_{n\ge0}\int_{x_0}^{x}u_n'(t)\,dt=\sum_{n\ge0}\bigl(u_n(x)-u_n(x_0)\bigr),
$$

the last equality being the fundamental theorem applied to each $u_n$, which is $C^1$.

The left-hand side is a well-defined number, and $\sum u_n(x_0)$ converges by hypothesis 1. By difference, the series $\sum u_n(x)$ therefore also converges — this is **where** the one-point hypothesis is used — and its sum equals

$$
S(x)=S(x_0)+\int_{x_0}^{x}T(t)\,dt .
$$

Finally $T$ is continuous, so the fundamental theorem applies a second time: $S$ is of class $C^1$ and $S'=T=\sum u_n'$.

**What the proof teaches.** Differentiation is never proved directly: it is reduced to integration, which is the stable operation. The pointwise hypothesis is not a technical convenience, it is the constant of integration — without it we would know the slope of $S$ without knowing $S$. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If all we know is "$\sum u_n$ converges uniformly", nothing can be concluded about $S'$: Counterexample 3 shows this. Conversely, if $\sum u_n'$ converges uniformly but $\sum u_n$ converges at **no** point, the sum $S$ does not exist. The two hypotheses are independent and both necessary.

**Example 4.** For $S(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}\dfrac{\sin(nx)}{n^3}$, the series of derivatives is $\sum\cos(nx)/n^2$, whose terms satisfy $\|\cos(nx)/n^2\|_\infty\le1/n^2$ with $\sum1/n^2$ convergent: normal, hence uniform, convergence on the whole of $\mathbb R$. The original series converges at $x_0=0$, where every term vanishes. Theorem 4 gives: $S$ is of class $C^1$ on $\mathbb R$ and $S'(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}\cos(nx)/n^2$.

### Mini-exercises

1. We know that $\sum u_n$ converges normally on $\mathbb R$. May we differentiate term by term?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No, not with that information alone: normality concerns the $u_n$, whereas Theorem 4 requires a control on the $u_n'$. Counterexample 3 shows that a control on the functions says nothing about the derivatives.

   </Collapsible>

2. In the proof of Theorem 4, what exactly is the hypothesis "$\sum u_n(x_0)$ converges" used for?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   To fix the constant of integration. The integral gives $\sum(u_n(x)-u_n(x_0))$; without convergence at $x_0$ we cannot split that difference into two convergent series, so we do not obtain $S(x)$.

   </Collapsible>

## 6. Writing it up: the hypothesis passport

An interchange is always written in **four lines**, in this order. Examiners look for those four lines; skipping them costs more marks than a computational slip.

**Method 1 (the passport).**

1. **Hypotheses listed** — those the theorem requires, stated as such.
2. **Hypotheses checked** — why they hold here, with the computation.
3. **Theorem named** — its name, and the segment or interval on which it is applied.
4. **Conclusion** — the equality obtained, written out in full.

The three passports of this chapter, ready to copy:

> **Continuity.** Each $u_n$ is continuous on $I$. The series $\sum u_n$ converges uniformly on every segment of $I$. By the theorem on continuity of the sum, $S$ is continuous on $I$.

> **Integration.** Each $u_n$ is continuous on the segment $[a,b]$. The series $\sum u_n$ converges normally there, hence uniformly. By the term-by-term integration theorem we may permute, and $\int_a^b S=\sum\int_a^b u_n$.

> **Differentiation.** Each $u_n$ is of class $C^1$ on $I$. The series $\sum u_n$ converges at the point $x_0$. The series $\sum u_n'$ converges normally on every segment of $I$. By the term-by-term differentiation theorem, $S$ is of class $C^1$ and $S'=\sum u_n'$.

**The boundary.** A result valid on every segment **contained** in $I$ does not extend automatically to an endpoint of $I$. Example 3 shows this: the formula for $\arctan t$ is proved for $t<1$, and nothing in the argument speaks of $t=1$. An endpoint is always studied separately.

## Worked example — certifying a numerical sum

Let us show that $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n\,2^n}=\ln 2$, justifying each authorisation.

**Step 1 — recognise a series of functions.** Start from the geometric identity, valid for $|x|<1$:

$$
\sum_{n\ge0}x^{n}=\frac1{1-x}.
$$

*Why?* We are looking for a family whose integration will produce the factor $1/n$; integrating $x^{n-1}$ gives $x^n/n$, so we start from the geometric series.

**Step 2 — check the hypothesis on the right domain.** Fix $t=1/2$ and work on the segment $[0,1/2]$. On that segment $\|x^{n}\|_\infty=(1/2)^{n}$ and $\sum(1/2)^{n}$ converges: the convergence is normal, hence uniform.

*Why?* Theorem 3 requires uniform convergence **on the segment of integration**, and normality is the fastest way to obtain it (lesson 06).

**Step 3 — apply the theorem and permute.** Theorem 3 authorises the interchange:

$$
\int_0^{1/2}\frac{dx}{1-x}=\sum_{n\ge0}\int_0^{1/2}x^{n}\,dx=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(1/2)^{n+1}}{n+1}=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n\,2^{n}} .
$$

*Why?* We write the permutation only after naming the theorem and checking its hypotheses; that is the order of the passport.

**Step 4 — compute the remaining side.** On the left,

$$
\int_0^{1/2}\frac{dx}{1-x}=\Bigl[-\ln(1-x)\Bigr]_0^{1/2}=\ln 2 .
$$

*Why?* An identity between series is worth something only if one of the two sides is computable; here it is the integral side that supplies the closed number.

<Collapsible title="Your turn — answer">

Justify in the same way that $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)3^{n}\sqrt3}=\frac{\pi}{6}$.

Start from $\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^nx^{2n}=1/(1+x^2)$ and integrate on $[0,t]$ with $t=1/\sqrt3<1$. On that segment $\|(-1)^nx^{2n}\|_\infty=t^{2n}=(1/3)^n$ and $\sum(1/3)^n$ converges: normal, hence uniform, convergence. Theorem 3 gives

$$
\arctan\frac1{\sqrt3}=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1}\left(\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)^{2n+1}=\frac1{\sqrt3}\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n}{(2n+1)3^{n}} ,
$$

and $\arctan(1/\sqrt3)=\pi/6$. Note that the choice $t=1/\sqrt3$ is what keeps the segment strictly inside the domain: at $t=1$ the argument would break down.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Interchanging without naming the theorem.** Writing $\int\sum=\sum\int$ without the four lines of the passport is claiming a licence without applying for it. This is the most frequent loss of marks in the chapter.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting that the segment is a hypothesis.** Counterexample 2 converges uniformly on $[0,+\infty)$ and the interchange is false there. Off a segment you need a domination, not a uniform norm.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Controlling $\sum u_n$ instead of $\sum u_n'$.** To differentiate, it is the series of **derivatives** that must converge uniformly. Counterexample 3 shows that controlling the functions transmits nothing to the slopes.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the anchor point.** Without a point where $\sum u_n$ converges, Theorem 4 gives the derivative of a function that does not exist. One value suffices, but one is needed.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Extending an interior result to the boundary.** "True on every $[-r,r]$ with $r<1$" says nothing at $\pm1$. The endpoint is studied separately, always.

</Pitfall>

<GuidingAnswer>

What must be added is a **uniform control, and on the right object**.

For continuity and for the integral, uniform convergence is needed: it turns an unknown gap into a rectangle of height $\|f_n-f\|_\infty$ and length $b-a$. The length factor alone explains the segment hypothesis — as soon as it becomes infinite, the example $g_n=e^{-x/n}/n$ shows that uniformity no longer suffices and a domination is required.

For the derivative, the object to control is not the series but **the series of derivatives**; the function itself is rebuilt by integrating those slopes from a single anchor point. That is why Theorem 4 has two hypotheses of different natures: one global, on the slopes, and one pointwise, on the value.

Finally, in all three cases the proof follows the same order: write a **finite** identity, which costs nothing, then pay a hypothesis for the right to pass to the limit in it. This is exactly Cauchy's warning in the epigraph: a formula holds only under its conditions.

</GuidingAnswer>

## Summary — which licence, at what price

<KeyResults title="The three interchanges and their hypotheses">

| Interchange wanted | Hypotheses to write | Conclusion | Inconclusive case |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| continuous limit | $f_n$ continuous, uniform on every segment of $I$ | $f$ continuous on $I$ | continuous limit without uniformity: no verdict |
| limit under the integral | $f_n$ continuous on a **segment**, uniform | the areas converge to the area | off a segment: a domination is needed |
| series integrated term by term | $u_n$ continuous, series uniform or <Terme id="convergence-normale">normal</Terme> on $[a,b]$ | permutation legitimate | nothing at the boundary of the interval |
| series differentiated term by term | $u_n$ of class $C^1$, $\sum u_n(x_0)$ convergent, $\sum u_n'$ uniform on every segment | sum is $C^1$, differentiated term by term | controlling $\sum u_n$ alone: no verdict |

**The three counterexamples worth being able to reconstruct.** The area-one bump on $[0,1]$: pointwise, not uniform, integrals equal to $1$. The spread-out bump $e^{-x/n}/n$ on $[0,+\infty)$: uniform, integrals equal to $1$ — the segment is what is missing. The root $\sqrt{x^2+1/n^2}$: uniform, limit not differentiable — the derivatives are what is missing.

**Proof ideas to take away.** An area is bounded by "length times height", and it is the length that forces the segment. A finite identity is proved without hypotheses, then one pays to pass to the limit. A differentiation always reduces to an integration, the constant being fixed by a single point.
