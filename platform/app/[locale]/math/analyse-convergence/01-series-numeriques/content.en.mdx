<GuidingQuestion>

The symbol $1+\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\cdots$ looks like an addition, but nobody can carry out its last step. **Which finite object must we study in order to give that expression a rigorous meaning, and how do we control the error when we stop?**

</GuidingQuestion>

## 1. Starting from what we know how to compute

A series will never be defined as a "completed addition". We start from a sequence of terms, then look at the finite additions it produces.

Problem: the expression $u_1+u_2+\cdots$ describes a plan for a computation, not yet a number. We must distinguish the terms, the finite running totals, and their possible limit.

**Definition 1 (numerical series).** To a sequence $(u_n)_{n\ge1}$ we associate the <Def id="serie-numerique">**numerical series**</Def>

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}u_n.
$$

This symbol denotes the study of the running totals of the terms $u_n$ in that order. It does not assert that the result exists.

**Definition 2 (partial sum).** The <Def id="somme-partielle">**partial sum**</Def> of index $N$ is the number that really can be computed:

$$
S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_n=u_1+\cdots+u_N.
$$

The series is therefore studied through the sequence $(S_N)$.

| Expression | Object | Legitimate question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $u_N$ | the term of index $N$ | does it become small? |
| $S_N$ | the sum of the first $N$ terms | does it settle down? |
| $\sum u_n$ | the series | does the sequence $(S_N)$ converge? |

**Example 1.** For $u_n=2^{-n}$,

$$
S_1=\frac12,\qquad S_2=\frac34,\qquad S_3=\frac78.
$$

The terms $u_n$ tend to $0$ while the running totals $S_N$ seem to tend to $1$. Those two observations do not say the same thing.

### Mini-exercises

1. How many terms does $\sum_{n=0}^{N}u_n$ contain?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   There are $N+1$ of them: the indices are $0,1,\ldots,N$.

   </Collapsible>

2. For $u_n=1/[n(n+1)]$, compute $S_3$ without looking for a general formula yet.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $S_3=1/2+1/6+1/12=3/4$.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Defining convergence through the partial sums

Problem: a few numerical values prove no settling down. We must express "all sufficiently late running totals are close to one same number" by a limit.

**Definition 3 (convergent series).** The series $\sum u_n$ is a <Def id="serie-convergente">**convergent series**</Def> when the sequence $(S_N)$ is a <Terme id="suite-convergente">convergent sequence</Terme>. If $S_N\to S$, we write

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}u_n=S.
$$

The number $S$ is the sum of the series. If $(S_N)$ has no real limit, the series diverges.

**Proposition 1 (necessary condition).** If $\sum u_n$ converges, then $u_n\to0$.

<Proof title="a term is the difference of two running totals">

**Idea.** Go back to the only sequence whose convergence is known: $(S_N)$.

For $N\ge2$,

$$
u_N=S_N-S_{N-1}.
$$

If the series converges to $S$, then $S_N\to S$ and $S_{N-1}\to S$. Passing to the limit in a difference,

$$
u_N\longrightarrow S-S=0.
$$

**What the proof teaches.** When a statement concerns a series, rewrite it first with its partial sums. Here a finite identity suffices. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** The converse is false. The limit $u_n\to0$ only allows the study to continue; it never proves convergence.

Problem: to prove convergence without knowing $S$, we would have to recognise that all the late running totals come close to one another.

**Theorem 1 (Cauchy criterion for a series).** The series $\sum u_n$ converges if and only if, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $N_0$ such that, for all $q>p\ge N_0$,

$$
\left|\sum_{n=p+1}^{q}u_n\right|<\varepsilon.
$$

The sum inside the absolute value is a **finite tail**: $S_q-S_p$.

<Proof title="translating the Cauchy criterion for sequences">

**Direct implication.** If $S_N\to S$, two sufficiently late terms $S_p$ and $S_q$ are close to $S$, hence close to each other:

$$
|S_q-S_p|\le |S_q-S|+|S_p-S|<\varepsilon.
$$

**Converse implication.** The hypothesis says exactly that $(S_N)$ is a Cauchy sequence. The completeness of $\mathbb R$ — every real Cauchy sequence converges — gives $(S_N)$ a limit $S$; that is Definition 3.

**What the proof teaches.** To establish absolute convergence or an Abel transformation later, we shall control not the unknown sum, but all the finite tails. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

### Mini-exercises

1. The sequence $u_n=1/n$ tends to $0$. Can we conclude anything about $\sum1/n$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No. Proposition 1 works only in the direction "convergence of the series $\Rightarrow u_n\to0$".

   </Collapsible>

2. If a finite tail is always at least $1/2$, can the Cauchy criterion be satisfied?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No: take $\varepsilon=1/4$, for instance. No tail of size at least $1/2$ can become smaller than that $\varepsilon$.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. Two patterns whose sum can be computed

### The geometric pattern

Problem: in $1+\frac12+\frac14+\cdots$, each term is obtained by the same multiplication. We must exploit that repetition before passing to the limit.

**Definition 4 (geometric series).** A <Def id="serie-geometrique">**geometric series**</Def> has term $a q^n$, where $q$ is a fixed ratio.

**Theorem 2 (geometric sum).** For $q\ne1$,

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{N}q^n=\frac{1-q^{N+1}}{1-q}.
$$

The series $\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}q^n$ converges exactly when $|q|<1$; its sum is then $1/(1-q)$.

<Proof title="shift, then subtract">

Set $S_N=1+q+\cdots+q^N$. Multiplying by $q$ shifts all the powers:

$$
qS_N=q+q^2+\cdots+q^{N+1}.
$$

Subtracting the two equalities, the intermediate terms cancel:

$$
(1-q)S_N=1-q^{N+1}.
$$

If $|q|<1$, then $q^{N+1}\to0$ and $S_N\to1/(1-q)$. If $|q|\ge1$, the term $q^n$ does not tend to $0$; Proposition 1 forces divergence.

**What the proof teaches.** The infinite formula comes after an identity on $S_N$, never before. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** A quotient $u_{n+1}/u_n$ that depends on $n$ does not define a geometric series. Variable quotients will be studied in the lesson on d'Alembert.

### The telescoping pattern

Problem: other terms do not multiply regularly, but are already differences of two neighbouring indices.

**Definition 5 (telescoping series).** A <Def id="serie-telescopique">**telescoping series**</Def> has a term of the form $u_n=a_n-a_{n+1}$.

**Proposition 2.** For every $N$,

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{N}(a_n-a_{n+1})=a_1-a_{N+1}.
$$

<Proof title="write out the first terms to see the cancellations">

Expand the finite sum:

$$
(a_1-a_2)+(a_2-a_3)+\cdots +(a_N-a_{N+1}).
$$

Each interior term appears once with a $+$ sign and once with a $-$ sign. Only $a_1-a_{N+1}$ remains. If $a_n\to\ell$, the series converges to $a_1-\ell$. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Example 2.** Since

$$
\frac1{n(n+1)}=\frac1n-\frac1{n+1},
$$

we obtain $S_N=1-1/(N+1)$, hence

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n(n+1)}=1.
$$

This is the central mechanism of exercise math-an-td1-01.

### Mini-exercises

1. Compute $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}3/4^n$.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   It equals $3\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}(1/4)^n=3(1/4)/(1-1/4)=1$.

   </Collapsible>

2. Decompose $1/[(n+1)(n+2)]$ and then compute its sum from $n=1$.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $1/[(n+1)(n+2)]=1/(n+1)-1/(n+2)$. The sum equals $1/2$.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. The counterexample that forbids a false rule

Problem: the condition $u_n\to0$ looks convincing as long as one has not met a very slow accumulation. We need an example we can prove, not merely observe.

**Definition 6 (harmonic series).** The <Def id="serie-harmonique">**harmonic series**</Def> is

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac1n.
$$

**Theorem 3.** The harmonic series diverges, even though $1/n\to0$.

<Proof title="group into blocks each costing at least one half">

Group the terms between two powers of $2$:

$$
1+\frac12+\left(\frac13+\frac14\right)+\left(\frac15+\cdots+\frac18\right)+\cdots
$$

In the block running from $2^{k-1}+1$ to $2^k$ there are $2^{k-1}$ terms, each at least $1/2^k$. The block is therefore worth at least

$$
2^{k-1}\frac1{2^k}=\frac12.
$$

After $m$ blocks the partial sum has gained at least $m/2$. The partial sums are not bounded above and tend to $+\infty$.

**What the proof teaches.** Tiny terms may be too numerous. The speed at which $u_n$ tends to $0$ therefore becomes the central question of the criteria to come. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Counterexample 1.** The statement "$u_n\to0$ implies that $\sum u_n$ converges" is false: its hypothesis is satisfied by $u_n=1/n$, but its conclusion is destroyed by Theorem 3.

## 5. Measuring the error when we stop

Problem: a convergent series has a sum $S$, but a real computation produces only $S_N$. We must name what is missing.

**Definition 7 (remainder of a series).** For a convergent series, the <Def id="reste-d-une-serie">**remainder of a series**</Def> after index $N$ is

$$
R_N=S-S_N=\sum_{n=N+1}^{+\infty}u_n.
$$

For a geometric series starting at $n=0$,

$$
R_N=\frac{q^{N+1}}{1-q}\qquad (|q|<1).
$$

**Example 3.** For $\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}2^{-n}=2$, the remainder equals $R_N=2^{-N}$. To guarantee $R_N<10^{-3}$ we need $N\ge10$, since $2^{-10}<10^{-3}<2^{-9}$.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** This remainder formula requires an exactly geometric pattern. For any other series, a suitable bound must be produced.

## Worked example — writing it up without handling the infinite too early

Let us study $\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}1/[(n+1)(n+2)]$ and its error after $N$ terms.

**Step 1 — check the necessary condition.** The term tends to $0$.

*Why?* If it did not, Proposition 1 would end the study immediately with a divergence.

**Step 2 — identify the pattern.** We write

$$
\frac1{(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac1{n+1}-\frac1{n+2}.
$$

*Why?* A difference of neighbouring indices calls for Proposition 2.

**Step 3 — compute the finite sum.** We obtain

$$
S_N=\frac12-\frac1{N+2}.
$$

*Why?* Definition 3 asks for the limit of $(S_N)$; that is therefore the object to construct.

**Step 4 — pass to the limit.** Hence $S_N\to1/2$: the series converges and its sum is $1/2$.

*Why?* The verdict and the value come from the same limit, but both must be stated.

**Step 5 — read off the error.** The remainder is $R_N=1/(N+2)$.

*Why?* This expression allows $N$ to be chosen for any precision. It is the mechanism of math-an-td1-05.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**Why do we never begin a proof by writing directly "the infinite sum equals …"?**

Because that sum exists only after convergence has been proved. The safe identities concern $S_N$ first, a finite addition; the limit comes afterwards.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Confusing $u_N$ with $S_N$.** A series converges when $(S_N)$ converges. The limit $u_N\to0$ is only a necessary condition.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the starting index.** From $0$ to $N$ there are $N+1$ terms. Always write both endpoints before using a geometric formula.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Cancelling terms in an infinite sum not yet justified.** Carry out the cancellations in $S_N$, then take the limit.

</Pitfall>

<GuidingAnswer>

An infinite sum receives a meaning by studying the finite additions $S_N$. If they tend to $S$, then — and only then — do we write $\sum u_n=S$. To approximate $S$ we compute $S_N$ and control the remainder $R_N=S-S_N$. Rigour therefore consists in **constructing, proving convergence, and only then naming the sum**.

</GuidingAnswer>

## Summary — the mental map of the lesson

<KeyResults title="Decide, compute, control">

| If you see… | You construct… | You may conclude… | Watch out |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| an arbitrary series | $S_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}u_n$ | convergence if $S_N$ has a limit | $u_n\to0$ is not enough |
| a constant ratio $q$ | a finite geometric sum | exact sum if $\lvert q\rvert <1$ | check the starting index |
| $a_n-a_{n+1}$ | the cancellations in $S_N$ | sum from the boundary terms | do not cancel before going finite |
| a required precision | $R_N=S-S_N$ | a guaranteed index $N$ | a specific bound is needed |

**Proofs worth being able to reconstruct:** writing $u_N=S_N-S_{N-1}$; shifting a geometric sum; expanding a telescoping sum; grouping the harmonic series into blocks.

</KeyResults>
