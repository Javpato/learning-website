<ExerciseView id="math-an-td5-01">
<Statement>

For each of the following <Terme id="serie-entiere">power series</Terme>, determine the <Terme id="rayon-de-convergence">radius of convergence</Terme>. The parameter $\alpha$ is a fixed real number.

1. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}n!\,x^n$;
2. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!}$;
3. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}n^{\alpha}x^n$;
4. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{2^n}{n}x^n$;
5. $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{n^2}x^n$.

For each series, state the test used and calculate its limit before concluding.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>For the first four series, first try the ratio of two consecutive coefficients.</Hint>
<Hint>For the fifth, the $n$-th root of the coefficient immediately simplifies the exponent $n^2$.</Hint>
<Hint>Remember that $1/R$ is the limit of $|a_n|^{1/n}$ when it exists.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

The five radii are respectively $0$, $+\infty$, $1$, $1/2$, and $e^{-1}$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

We write each series in the form $\sum a_nx^n$ and choose the tool suited to the coefficients.

**1. Factorial coefficients.** Here $a_n=n!$. The ratio is

$$
\left|\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}\right|
=\frac{n!}{(n+1)!}
=\frac1{n+1}
\longrightarrow0.
$$

By the ratio formula, derived from the <Terme id="regle-de-d-alembert">ratio test</Terme>, the radius is therefore $R=0$. For every $x\ne0$, the ratio of the terms in absolute value is $(n+1)|x|$ and tends to $+\infty$: the general term does not tend to $0$.

**2. Reciprocals of factorial coefficients.** Here $a_n=1/n!$, so

$$
\left|\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}\right|
=n+1
\longrightarrow+\infty.
$$

The same formula gives $R=+\infty$. The series has <Terme id="convergence-absolue">absolute convergence</Terme> for every real $x$; it is the standard series for the exponential function.

**3. Polynomial coefficients.** For $a_n=n^{\alpha}$,

$$
\left|\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}\right|
=\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^{\alpha}
\longrightarrow1.
$$

The ratio formula gives $R=1$, whatever the fixed real value of $\alpha$. The power of $n$ may change the behavior at the endpoints, but not the radius.

**4. Adjusted exponential coefficients.** With $a_n=2^n/n$,

$$
\left|\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}\right|
=\frac{2^n/n}{2^{n+1}/(n+1)}
=\frac{n+1}{2n}
\longrightarrow\frac12.
$$

The ratio formula gives $R=1/2$.

**5. Hidden exponential growth.** Set

$$
a_n=\left(1+\frac1n\right)^{n^2}.
$$

The $n$-th root is

$$
|a_n|^{1/n}=\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n\longrightarrow e.
$$

By the Cauchy–Hadamard formula, or the <Terme id="regle-de-cauchy">root test</Terme> applied to $|a_nx^n|$, we have $1/R=e$. Thus $R=e^{-1}$.

The structure is the same in all five cases: identify the coefficients, calculate the limit of the ratio or root, name the test, then state the radius.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Confusing the limit of the coefficients with the radius.** It is $|a_n/a_{n+1}|$ or $|a_n|^{1/n}$ that determines $R$, not merely the limit of $a_n$.
- **Writing $R=e$ for the fifth series.** The root of the coefficients tends to $e$, but the formula gives $1/R=e$; you had to take the reciprocal.
- **Studying the endpoints before finding the radius.** This exercise first asks for the boundary; behavior at the endpoints is a second study, practiced in Exercise 3.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td5-02">
<Statement>

Study the two lacunary <Terme id="serie-entiere">power series</Terme>

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{2n}}{3^n}
\qquad\text{et}\qquad
\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}x^{n^2}.
$$

For each one, determine the <Terme id="rayon-de-convergence">radius of convergence</Terme> and the behavior at both real endpoints. Explain precisely why the <Terme id="regle-de-d-alembert">ratio test</Terme> on consecutive coefficients does not apply as stated.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>Rewrite the first series as a geometric series whose ratio depends on $x$.</Hint>
<Hint>For the second, if $|x|<1$, compare $|x|^{n^2}$ with $|x|^n$.</Hint>
<Hint>Written in the form $\sum_{k\ge0}a_kx^k$, both series have infinitely many zero coefficients.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

The first series has radius $\sqrt3$ and diverges at $x=\pm\sqrt3$; the second has radius $1$ and diverges at $x=\pm1$; the ratios of consecutive coefficients are undefined infinitely often.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. The series of even powers.** For fixed $x$,

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{2n}}{3^n}
=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{x^2}{3}\right)^n.
$$

This is the <Terme id="serie-geometrique">geometric series</Terme> with ratio $x^2/3$. It converges if and only if

$$
\left|\frac{x^2}{3}\right|<1,
$$

that is, if $|x|<\sqrt3$. Its radius is therefore $R=\sqrt3$. At $x=\sqrt3$ as well as at $x=-\sqrt3$, the general term equals $1$: it does not tend to $0$, so the series diverges at both endpoints.

Now write the series as $\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}a_kx^k$. We have

$$
a_{2n}=3^{-n},
\qquad
a_{2n+1}=0.
$$

The ratio $a_k/a_{k+1}$ therefore involves division by zero for infinitely many indices and a $0/0$ form for other lacunary families. The ratio formula for consecutive coefficients does not satisfy its hypotheses. By contrast, the ratio of the **terms that are present**, indexed by $n$, does allow us to recognize a geometric series here; this is not the same application.

**2. The series of squares.** If $|x|<1$, then $n^2\ge n$ for $n\ge1$, so

$$
0\le |x|^{n^2}\le |x|^n.
$$

The reference geometric series $\sum |x|^n$ converges. By the <Terme id="critere-de-comparaison">comparison test</Terme>, $\sum |x|^{n^2}$ converges, so the series under study has <Terme id="convergence-absolue">absolute convergence</Terme>. If $|x|>1$, the term $x^{n^2}$ does not tend to $0$; the series diverges. Thus $R=1$.

At the right endpoint, $x=1$ gives terms all equal to $1$. At the left endpoint,

$$
(-1)^{n^2}=(-1)^n,
$$

which oscillates between $-1$ and $1$. In both cases, the general term does not tend to $0$: both numerical series diverge.

In the form $\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}a_kx^k$, we have $a_k=1$ when $k$ is a square and $a_k=0$ otherwise. Once again, the consecutive coefficients are not all nonzero from some index onward: their ratio is not a valid tool. The direct study provides the radius without erasing the gaps.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Applying $R=\lim|a_k/a_{k+1}|$ despite the zeros.** The formula requires the ratios to be defined from some index onward; here this hypothesis fails infinitely often.
- **Concluding that $R=3$ for the first series.** The geometric ratio is $x^2/3$; the condition therefore concerns $|x|^2$, which gives $R=\sqrt3$.
- **Saying that $\sum(-1)^{n^2}$ converges because the signs alternate.** The <Terme id="critere-de-leibniz">Leibniz test</Terme> also requires the absolute value of the terms to tend to $0$; here it remains equal to $1$.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td5-03">
<Statement>

For each of the following series, first calculate the <Terme id="rayon-de-convergence">radius of convergence</Terme> $R$, then study $x=R$ and $x=-R$ separately:

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n},
\qquad
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n^2},
\qquad
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{\sqrt n}.
$$

At each endpoint, specify whether there is divergence, <Terme id="convergence-absolue">absolute convergence</Terme>, or <Terme id="semi-convergence">conditional convergence</Terme>.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>In all three cases, the ratio of two consecutive coefficients tends to $1$.</Hint>
<Hint>At the right endpoint, you obtain three Riemann series; at the left endpoint, the signs alternate.</Hint>
<Hint>At the left endpoint, check that $1/n^p$ decreases to $0$, but still examine the series of absolute values before classifying the convergence.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

All three radii equal $1$; at $x=1$, the conclusions are divergence, absolute convergence, divergence; at $x=-1$, they are conditional convergence, absolute convergence, conditional convergence.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

For $a_n=n^{-p}$ with $p>0$,

$$
\left|\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}\right|
=\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^p
\longrightarrow1.
$$

By the ratio formula associated with the <Terme id="regle-de-d-alembert">ratio test</Terme>, the three series therefore have the same radius $R=1$. This calculation says nothing about the endpoints: we repeat the study for each series and each endpoint.

**1. Series $\sum x^n/n$.** At $x=1$, we obtain the <Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme>

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac1n,
$$

which diverges. At $x=-1$, we obtain $\sum(-1)^n/n$. The absolute values $1/n$ decrease to $0$, so the <Terme id="critere-de-leibniz">Leibniz test</Terme> gives convergence. It is not absolute, because the series of absolute values is the divergent harmonic series: there is conditional convergence.

**2. Series $\sum x^n/n^2$.** At $x=1$, the <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> $\sum1/n^2$ converges because its exponent is $2>1$. At $x=-1$, the series of absolute values is again $\sum1/n^2$, so it also converges. There is absolute convergence at both endpoints.

**3. Series $\sum x^n/\sqrt n$.** At $x=1$, we obtain the Riemann series $\sum1/n^{1/2}$, which diverges because $1/2\le1$. At $x=-1$, the absolute values $1/\sqrt n$ decrease to $0$; Leibniz gives convergence of $\sum(-1)^n/\sqrt n$. The series of absolute values diverges: the convergence is only conditional.

The final table is therefore:

| Series | Radius | At $x=R$ | At $x=-R$ | Real domain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\sum x^n/n$ | $1$ | harmonic divergence | conditional convergence by Leibniz | $[-1,1[$ |
| $\sum x^n/n^2$ | $1$ | absolute convergence | absolute convergence | $[-1,1]$ |
| $\sum x^n/\sqrt n$ | $1$ | Riemann divergence | conditional convergence by Leibniz | $[-1,1[$ |

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Writing “$R=1$, so the series converges on $[-1,1]$.”** The radius gives no conclusion when $|x|=R$; you had to carry out two separate numerical-series studies.
- **Concluding absolute convergence as soon as a series alternates.** Leibniz gives ordinary convergence; the absolute value must be studied separately.
- **Copying the conclusion at $x=1$ to $x=-1$.** The factor $(-1)^n$ changes the nature of the first and third series.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td5-04">
<Statement>

Define the <Terme id="serie-entiere">power series</Terme>

$$
S(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n(n+1)}.
$$

1. Determine its <Terme id="rayon-de-convergence">radius of convergence</Terme>.
2. Using

   $$
   \frac1{n(n+1)}=\frac1n-\frac1{n+1},
   $$

   obtain a closed-form expression for $S(x)$ when $|x|<1$.
3. Calculate the limits of this expression as $x\to0$ and as $x\to1^-$, then compare them with the values of the series at $0$ and $1$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>The ratio of two consecutive coefficients tends to $1$.</Hint>
<Hint>Use $\sum_{n\ge1}x^n/n=-\ln(1-x)$, then change the index in the sum containing $1/(n+1)$.</Hint>
<Hint>At $0$, expand $\ln(1-x)$ to order $2$; at $1$, set $t=1-x$ and use $t\ln t\to0$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

$R=1$ and, for $|x|<1$ with $x\ne0$, $S(x)=1+(1-x)\ln(1-x)/x$; this expression extends to $S(0)=0$ and tends to $1=S(1)$ as $x\to1^-$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Radius.** Set $a_n=1/[n(n+1)]$. Then

$$
\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}
=\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{n(n+1)}
=\frac{n+2}{n}
\longrightarrow1.
$$

By the ratio formula and the <Terme id="regle-de-d-alembert">ratio test</Terme>, the radius is $R=1$.

**2. Closed-form expression.** For $|x|<1$, the two series obtained by decomposition converge. By linearity of convergent series,

$$
S(x)
=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n}
-\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n+1}.
$$

The standard <Terme id="developpement-en-serie-entiere">power series expansion</Terme> of the logarithm gives

$$
A(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n}=-\ln(1-x).
$$

If $x\ne0$, the change of index $m=n+1$ gives

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n+1}
=\frac1x\sum_{m=2}^{+\infty}\frac{x^m}{m}
=\frac{A(x)-x}{x}.
$$

Thus

$$
S(x)=A(x)-\frac{A(x)-x}{x}
=1+\frac{1-x}{x}\ln(1-x),
\qquad |x|<1,
\quad x\ne0.
$$

**3. Limit at $0$.** The <Terme id="formule-de-taylor-young">Taylor–Young formula</Terme> gives

$$
\ln(1-x)=-x-\frac{x^2}{2}+o(x^2).
$$

Consequently,

$$
S(x)
=1+(1-x)\left(-1-\frac x2+o(x)\right)
=\frac x2+o(x)
\longrightarrow0.
$$

The apparent singularity of the closed-form expression at $0$ is therefore removable, and the value obtained agrees with the series: $S(0)=0$.

**4. Limit at $1$.** Set $t=1-x$. Since $t\ln t\to0$ as $t\to0^+$,

$$
\frac{1-x}{x}\ln(1-x)
=\frac{t\ln t}{1-t}
\longrightarrow0.
$$

Therefore $S(x)\to1$ as $x\to1^-$. At the endpoint $x=1$, the numerical series is <Terme id="serie-telescopique">telescoping</Terme>:

$$
S(1)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+1}\right)=1.
$$

The interior limit and the sum at the endpoint do indeed agree.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Losing the factor $1/x$ after changing the index.** We have $x^n=x^{m-1}=x^m/x$; omitting it gives an incorrect closed-form expression.
- **Replacing $x$ directly with $0$ in a formula containing $1/x$.** You first had to remove the singularity with an asymptotic expansion or return to the series.
- **Declaring the limit at $1$ from the radius alone.** The radius says nothing about the endpoint; here $S(1)=1$ comes from telescoping the numerical series.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td5-05">
<Statement>

Solve the differential problem

$$
(1-x)y'=2y,
\qquad
y(0)=1,
$$

by seeking $y$ in the form of a <Terme id="serie-entiere">power series</Terme> $y(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}a_nx^n$.

1. Establish the recurrence relation for the coefficients.
2. Determine $a_n$, then the <Terme id="rayon-de-convergence">radius of convergence</Terme> of the resulting series.
3. Recognize its sum as $y(x)=1/(1-x)^2$.
4. Check the equation and initial condition directly.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>Write $y'$ with the index $n$ in front of $x^n$, then reindex before comparing coefficients.</Hint>
<Hint>The coefficient of $x^n$ in $(1-x)y'$ is $(n+1)a_{n+1}-na_n$.</Hint>
<Hint>The recurrence gives $a_n=n+1$; then differentiate the identity $\sum_{n\ge0}x^{n+1}=x/(1-x)$ to recognize the sum.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

$a_0=1$ and $(n+1)a_{n+1}=(n+2)a_n$, so $a_n=n+1$, $R=1$, and $y(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)x^n=1/(1-x)^2$ for $|x|<1$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Checklist for differentiating the desired series.** Suppose that $y(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}a_nx^n$ has a radius $r>0$.

1. **Assumptions listed.** We must work strictly inside the radius of a power series.
2. **Assumptions checked.** For every $\rho$ such that $0<\rho<r$, the segment $[-\rho,\rho]$ remains strictly inside the interval of convergence.
3. **Theorem named.** We apply the theorem on <Terme id="derivation-terme-a-terme">term-by-term differentiation</Terme> of a power series, whose derivative series has the same radius.
4. **Conclusion.** For $|x|<r$,

$$
y'(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(n+1)a_{n+1}x^n.
$$

**2. Comparing coefficients.** After multiplying by $x$,

$$
xy'(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}na_nx^n.
$$

The coefficient of $x^n$ in $(1-x)y'$ is therefore $(n+1)a_{n+1}-na_n$, including for $n=0$. The equality $(1-x)y'=2y$ requires

$$
(n+1)a_{n+1}-na_n=2a_n,
$$

that is,

$$
a_{n+1}=\frac{n+2}{n+1}a_n
\qquad(n\ge0).
$$

The initial condition gives $a_0=y(0)=1$. An immediate induction yields

$$
a_n=n+1
\qquad(n\ge0).
$$

**3. Radius and identification.** For $a_n=n+1$,

$$
\frac{a_n}{a_{n+1}}
=\frac{n+1}{n+2}
\longrightarrow1.
$$

By the <Terme id="regle-de-d-alembert">ratio test</Terme>, the radius is $R=1$.

To identify the sum without simply guessing, start from the <Terme id="serie-geometrique">geometric series</Terme>. Fix $0<\rho<1$.

1. **Assumptions listed.** The functions $u_n(x)=x^{n+1}$ must be of class $\mathcal C^1$ on $[-\rho,\rho]$, the series $\sum u_n(0)$ must converge, and the series $\sum u_n'$ must have <Terme id="convergence-uniforme">uniform convergence</Terme> there.
2. **Assumptions checked.** The $u_n$ are polynomials, $u_n(0)=0$, and $|u_n'(x)|\le(n+1)\rho^n$. The numerical series $\sum(n+1)\rho^n$ converges by the ratio test, so the <Terme id="critere-de-weierstrass">Weierstrass M-test</Terme> gives uniform convergence of the derivatives.
3. **Theorem named.** We apply the theorem on term-by-term differentiation of a series of functions on a segment.
4. **Conclusion.** For $|x|\le\rho$,

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}(n+1)x^n
=\frac{d}{dx}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}x^{n+1}\right)
=\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{x}{1-x}\right)
=\frac1{(1-x)^2}.
$$

Since $\rho<1$ is arbitrary, this identity holds for every $|x|<1$.

**4. Direct check.** The function obtained satisfies

$$
y'(x)=\frac2{(1-x)^3},
$$

then

$$
(1-x)y'(x)=\frac2{(1-x)^2}=2y(x),
\qquad
y(0)=1.
$$

The constructed series therefore does give the requested solution on the interval $(-1,1)$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Forgetting to reindex $y'$.** Unless both sides are written with the same power $x^n$, comparing coefficients produces a shifted recurrence.
- **Obtaining $a_{n+1}=(n+2)a_n$.** The coefficient coming from $y'$ is $(n+1)a_{n+1}$; you had to divide by $n+1$.
- **Recognizing $1/(1-x)^2$ without justifying differentiation of the infinite sum.** Term-by-term differentiation required the checklist, valid on every segment strictly inside $R=1$.

</CommonErrors>
</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-an-td5-06">
<Statement>

For $x\in[0,1]$, define the <Terme id="serie-entiere">power series</Terme>

$$
S(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n^2}.
$$

On $]0,1[$, define the auxiliary function

$$
F(x)=S(x)+S(1-x)+\ln x\,\ln(1-x).
$$

1. Justify the <Terme id="derivation-terme-a-terme">term-by-term differentiation</Terme> of $S$ inside its interval of convergence and show that

   $$
   S'(x)=-\frac{\ln(1-x)}x.
   $$

2. Show by differentiating that $F$ is constant on $]0,1[$.
3. Determine this constant by taking the limit as $x\to1^-$, then evaluate $F(1/2)$.
4. Deduce a relation between $S(1/2)$ and the Basel value

   $$
   S(1)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6},
   $$

   which is assumed here.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>
<Hint>On every segment $[0,r]$ with $r<1$, bound $x^{n-1}/n$ by $r^{n-1}$.</Hint>
<Hint>Use $S'(1-x)=-\ln x/(1-x)$, without forgetting the sign from the derivative of $1-x$.</Hint>
<Hint>As $x\to1^-$, set $t=1-x$: the logarithmic product tends to $0$ because $t\ln t\to0$.</Hint>
</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

$F'(x)=0$ on $]0,1[$ and $F(x)=S(1)=\pi^2/6$; therefore $2S(1/2)+(\ln2)^2=\pi^2/6$, that is, $S(1/2)=\pi^2/12-(\ln2)^2/2$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

**1. Checklist for differentiating $S$.** Fix $r\in]0,1[$ and set $u_n(x)=x^n/n^2$ on $[0,r]$.

1. **Assumptions listed.** The $u_n$ must be of class $\mathcal C^1$ on $[0,r]$, the series $\sum u_n(0)$ must converge, and the series $\sum u_n'$ must converge uniformly on this segment.
2. **Assumptions checked.** The $u_n$ are polynomials, $u_n(0)=0$, and

$$
|u_n'(x)|=\frac{x^{n-1}}n\le r^{n-1}.
$$

The numerical <Terme id="serie-geometrique">geometric series</Terme> $\sum r^{n-1}$ converges; the <Terme id="critere-de-weierstrass">Weierstrass M-test</Terme> therefore gives <Terme id="convergence-uniforme">uniform convergence</Terme> of the series of derivatives.
3. **Theorem named.** We apply the theorem on term-by-term differentiation of a series of functions on a segment.
4. **Conclusion.** The sum $S$ is differentiable on $[0,r]$ and

$$
S'(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^{n-1}}n
=\frac1x\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{x^n}{n}
=-\frac{\ln(1-x)}x
$$

for $0<x\le r$, where we used the standard <Terme id="developpement-en-serie-entiere">power series expansion</Terme> $-\ln(1-x)=\sum_{n\ge1}x^n/n$. Since $r<1$ is arbitrary, the formula holds for every $x\in]0,1[$.

**2. Vanishing of the derivative.** For $x\in]0,1[$, the chain rule gives

$$
\frac{d}{dx}S(1-x)
=-S'(1-x)
=\frac{\ln x}{1-x}.
$$

On the other hand,

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(\ln x\,\ln(1-x)\bigr)
=\frac{\ln(1-x)}x-\frac{\ln x}{1-x}.
$$

Consequently,

$$
F'(x)
=-\frac{\ln(1-x)}x
+\frac{\ln x}{1-x}
+\frac{\ln(1-x)}x
-\frac{\ln x}{1-x}
=0.
$$

By the mean value theorem, a differentiable function whose derivative is zero on an interval is constant. Thus $F$ is constant on $]0,1[$.

**3. Checklist for <Terme id="continuite">continuity</Terme> up to the endpoint.** It remains to identify the constant without substituting $x=1$ into the logarithms.

1. **Assumptions listed.** Each $u_n(x)=x^n/n^2$ must be continuous on $[0,1]$, and the series $\sum u_n$ must converge uniformly there.
2. **Assumptions checked.** The $u_n$ are polynomials and $|u_n(x)|\le1/n^2$. The <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> $\sum1/n^2$ converges; the Weierstrass M-test gives <Terme id="convergence-normale">normal convergence</Terme>, hence uniform convergence, on $[0,1]$.
3. **Theorem named.** We apply the theorem on continuity of the sum of a uniformly convergent series of continuous functions.
4. **Conclusion.** The function $S$ is continuous on $[0,1]$, so

$$
S(x)\longrightarrow S(1)
\qquad\text{et}\qquad
S(1-x)\longrightarrow S(0)=0
$$

as $x\to1^-$.

For the last term, set $t=1-x$. Since $\ln(1-t)\sim-t$ and $t\ln t\to0$ as $t\to0^+$,

$$
\ln x\,\ln(1-x)
=\ln(1-t)\ln t
\longrightarrow0.
$$

The constant is therefore

$$
F(x)=\lim_{u\to1^-}F(u)=S(1).
$$

**4. Evaluation at the midpoint.** At $x=1/2$,

$$
F\!\left(\frac12\right)
=2S\!\left(\frac12\right)
+\ln^2\!\left(\frac12\right)
=2S\!\left(\frac12\right)+(\ln2)^2.
$$

The Basel value, assumed in the statement, is $S(1)=\pi^2/6$. Since $F(1/2)=S(1)$,

$$
2S\!\left(\frac12\right)+(\ln2)^2
=S(1)
=\frac{\pi^2}{6}.
$$

Finally,

$$
S\!\left(\frac12\right)
=\frac{\pi^2}{12}-\frac{(\ln2)^2}{2}.
$$

The auxiliary function was built so that the four terms of its derivative cancel in pairs; its constant then relates an interior value to the reference value at the endpoint.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- **Forgetting the sign in the derivative of $S(1-x)$.** The chain rule gives $-S'(1-x)$; without this sign, the terms no longer cancel.
- **Writing $F(1)$ directly despite $\ln(1-x)$.** The auxiliary function is defined only on $]0,1[$; you had to take the limit and control the product of the logarithms.
- **Moving the limit inside the series without justification.** Continuity of $S$ at the endpoint comes from the checklist: the bound by the Riemann series $\sum1/n^2$, uniform convergence, then the continuity theorem.
