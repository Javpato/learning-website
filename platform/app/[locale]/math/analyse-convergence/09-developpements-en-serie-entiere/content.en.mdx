<GuidingQuestion>

Every infinitely differentiable function has a Taylor series. That series may have an infinite radius of convergence. And yet its sum may bear **no relation at all** to the function it came from. **How can a function betray all its derivatives at once, and what must be checked to be sure it does not?**

</GuidingQuestion>

## 1. Being expandable, and being so in only one way

**Definition 1 (power series expansion).** A function $f$ admits a <Def id="developpement-en-serie-entiere">**power series expansion**</Def> in a neighbourhood of $0$ if there exist $r>0$ and coefficients $(a_n)$ such that

$$
f(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n\qquad\text{for every }x\in(-r,r).
$$

Problem: nothing says a priori that a function has only one such expansion. If two different sequences of coefficients gave the same function, the notion would be unusable. That door must be closed first.

For that, a preliminary tool: differentiating a power series does not change its radius.

**Proposition 1 (the derived series has the same radius).** The series $\sum a_nx^n$ and $\sum_{n\ge1} n\,a_nx^{n-1}$ have the same <Terme id="rayon-de-convergence">radius of convergence</Terme>.

<Proof title="the factor n is crushed by any geometric ratio">

**Idea.** Multiplying by $n$ seems to make the series worse, but $n$ grows infinitely more slowly than a geometric sequence of ratio $<1$: this is rank 2 of the ladder of <Terme id="croissances-comparees">comparative growth</Terme>.

Write $R$ for the radius of $\sum a_nx^n$ and $R'$ for that of the derived series.

Let $0<r<R$. Choose $\rho$ with $r<\rho<R$. Since $\rho<R$, the sequence $(a_n\rho^n)$ is bounded by some $M$, and

$$
\bigl|n\,a_nr^{n-1}\bigr|=|a_n\rho^{n}|\cdot\frac{n}{\rho}\left(\frac{r}{\rho}\right)^{n-1}\le\frac{M}{\rho}\;n\,q^{\,n-1},
\qquad q=\frac r\rho<1 .
$$

The sequence $nq^{n-1}$ tends to $0$, hence is bounded: so is $(n\,a_nr^{n-1})$, giving $r\le R'$. Letting $r$ tend to $R$ yields $R\le R'$.

Conversely, $|a_nr^{n}|\le r\,\bigl|n\,a_nr^{n-1}\bigr|$ as soon as $n\ge1$, so any bound for the derived series gives one for the original: $R'\le R$.

**What the proof teaches.** The coefficients may be multiplied by any power of $n$ without moving the radius, because the geometric factor always wins. It can, however, change the behaviour **at the boundary** — which is one more illustration of the fact that the radius ignores the endpoints. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Theorem 1 (uniqueness of the coefficients).** If $f(x)=\sum a_nx^n$ on $(-r,r)$, then $f$ is of class $C^\infty$ on $(-r,r)$ and

$$
a_n=\frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}\qquad\text{for every }n .
$$

In particular two power series expansions of the same function have the same coefficients.

<Proof title="differentiate term by term, then evaluate at zero">

**Idea.** Each differentiation lowers the degree of every monomial by one; evaluating at $0$, everything vanishes except the constant term, which isolates exactly one coefficient.

By Theorem 2 of lesson 08, the series converges normally on every segment of $(-r,r)$; by Proposition 1, the series of derivatives has the same radius, so it too converges normally on every segment. The <Terme id="derivation-terme-a-terme">term-by-term differentiation</Terme> theorem of lesson 07 therefore applies, and it applies **again** to the resulting series, and so on indefinitely: $f$ is of class $C^\infty$ and

$$
f^{(k)}(x)=\sum_{n\ge k}n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\,a_nx^{\,n-k}.
$$

Evaluate at $x=0$: every term with $n>k$ contains a vanishing factor $x^{n-k}$, and the term $n=k$ equals $k!\,a_k$. So $f^{(k)}(0)=k!\,a_k$.

**What the proof teaches.** Uniqueness is not a formal fact: it rests entirely on the right to differentiate term by term, hence on normal convergence inside the radius. It is also the practical recipe — to identify coefficients, differentiate and evaluate at $0$. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Definition 2 (Taylor series).** The <Def id="serie-de-taylor">**Taylor series**</Def> of $f$ at $0$ is the power series

$$
\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}x^n ,
$$

defined as soon as $f$ is of class $C^\infty$ in a neighbourhood of $0$.

Theorem 1 therefore says: **if** $f$ is expandable, its expansion is necessarily its Taylor series. The whole remaining question is whether the converse holds.

### Mini-exercises

1. A function satisfies $f(x)=\sum a_nx^n$ on $(-1,1)$ and $f$ is even. What can be said about the $a_n$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The function $x\mapsto f(-x)$ has expansion $\sum(-1)^na_nx^n$, and equals $f$. By uniqueness, $(-1)^na_n=a_n$, so $a_n=0$ for every odd $n$.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why is Proposition 1 needed before Theorem 1?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because term-by-term differentiation requires uniform convergence of the series **of derivatives** (lesson 07). Without knowing that it has the same radius, we would have no right to differentiate, hence no access to the $f^{(n)}(0)$.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Being of class $C^\infty$ is not enough

Problem: the Taylor series is manufactured from the derivatives **at a single point**. Nothing guarantees that this local information determines the function anywhere else.

**Counterexample 1 (the flat function).** Set

$$
f(x)=\begin{cases}e^{-1/x^2},&x\ne0,\\[2pt]0,&x=0.\end{cases}
$$

This function is of class $C^\infty$ on $\mathbb R$, and **all** its derivatives at $0$ vanish.

<Collapsible title="Verification — why all the derivatives vanish">

For $x\ne0$, an immediate induction shows that $f^{(n)}(x)=P_n\!\left(\tfrac1x\right)e^{-1/x^2}$ for some polynomial $P_n$: differentiating only adds powers of $1/x$ in front of the same exponential.

Now, as $x\to0$, set $u=1/x^2\to+\infty$; every power of $1/x$ is dominated by $e^{u}$ by the ladder of comparative growth, so $P_n(1/x)e^{-1/x^2}\to0$.

By induction, $f^{(n)}(0)=0$ for every $n$: the difference quotient $f^{(n-1)}(x)/x$ also tends to $0$ for the same reason.

</Collapsible>

Its Taylor series is therefore the **zero** series. It converges on the whole of $\mathbb R$ — its radius is infinite — and its sum is $0$ everywhere. Yet $f(x)>0$ for every $x\ne0$.

**The implication that fails is "$f$ of class $C^\infty$ $\Rightarrow$ $f$ expandable as a power series".** It fails in the worst possible way: the Taylor series exists, converges everywhere, and represents $f$ at the single point $0$.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Keep the distinction in mind, for it is the main source of error in this chapter. There are **three** distinct questions:

1. does the Taylor series exist? — yes, as soon as $f$ is $C^\infty$;
2. does it converge? — a question of radius, settled by lesson 08;
3. does it converge **to $f$**? — a new question, which neither 1 nor 2 answers.

Checking 1 and 2 proves nothing about 3. That is exactly the mistake Counterexample 1 punishes.

### Mini-exercises

1. A Taylor series has radius of convergence $5$. Can we conclude that $f$ is expandable on $(-5,5)$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   No. That is question 2, not question 3. The flat function has a Taylor series of infinite radius that represents the function nowhere except at $0$.

   </Collapsible>

2. Where does Counterexample 1 contradict Theorem 1?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Nowhere. Theorem 1 says: *if* $f$ is expandable, then its coefficients are the Taylor ones. It does not say that it is. The flat function is simply not expandable at $0$.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. The criterion that settles it: controlling the Taylor remainder

Problem: question 3 concerns the gap between $f$ and its Taylor polynomials. So we need a statement that **measures** that gap, not one that assumes it small.

Write $T_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\dfrac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}x^k$ for the Taylor polynomial of order $n$, and $R_n(x)=f(x)-T_n(x)$ for the remainder. By definition, $f$ is expandable on $(-r,r)$ if and only if $R_n(x)\to0$ for each $x$ of that interval. It remains to know how to bound $R_n$.

**Theorem 2 (criterion via bounded derivatives).** Let $a>0$ and let $f$ be of class $C^\infty$ on $[-a,a]$. If there exists $M$ such that

$$
\bigl|f^{(n)}(t)\bigr|\le M\qquad\text{for every }n\ \text{and every }t\in[-a,a],
$$

then $f$ is expandable as a power series on $[-a,a]$, and

$$
|R_n(x)|\le M\,\frac{|x|^{\,n+1}}{(n+1)!}.
$$

<Proof title="the Taylor-Lagrange inequality, then a factorial in the denominator">

**Idea.** The Taylor–Lagrange inequality turns the unknown remainder into an expression containing a single derivative of order $n+1$. The hypothesis bounds it, and the factorial does the rest.

The Taylor–Lagrange inequality at order $n$ between $0$ and $x$ gives

$$
|R_n(x)|\le\frac{|x|^{\,n+1}}{(n+1)!}\;\sup_{t\in[0,x]}\bigl|f^{(n+1)}(t)\bigr|\le M\,\frac{|x|^{\,n+1}}{(n+1)!}.
$$

It remains to see that this bound tends to $0$ with $x$ fixed. That is rank 3 of the ladder of comparative growth: the factorial crushes every exponential, so $|x|^{n+1}/(n+1)!\to0$. One can also see it with Proposition 1 of the toolbox, the ratio of two consecutive terms being $|x|/(n+2)\to0<1$.

So $R_n(x)\to0$ for each $x$ of $[-a,a]$, that is $T_n(x)\to f(x)$: the Taylor series converges to $f$.

**What the proof teaches.** The criterion does not look at the series, it looks at **the function** — through its derivatives on a whole interval, not only at $0$. It is that passage from pointwise to global which was missing in Counterexample 1, whose derivatives are bounded on no neighbourhood of $0$ uniformly in $n$. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Example 1 (the exponential).** On $[-a,a]$, every derivative of $e^x$ equals $e^x$, bounded by $e^{a}$. Theorem 2 applies with $M=e^a$, so for every real $x$ (taking $a=|x|$)

$$
e^{x}=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}.
$$

**Example 2 (sine and cosine).** All the derivatives of $\sin$ and $\cos$ are $\pm\sin$ or $\pm\cos$, hence bounded by $1$ on $\mathbb R$. Theorem 2 gives, for every $x$,

$$
\sin x=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!},
\qquad
\cos x=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{(2n)!}.
$$

**Remark (inconclusive case).** The condition of Theorem 2 is **sufficient, not necessary**. The function $1/(1-x)$ is expandable on $(-1,1)$ although its derivatives $n!/(1-x)^{n+1}$ are bounded by no common $M$. When the criterion fails we conclude nothing: we change method, and the next section shows which one.

### Mini-exercises

1. Bound the remainder of order $3$ for $e^x$ on $[-1,1]$.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   With $M=e^1=e$ and $|x|\le1$, Theorem 2 gives $|R_3(x)|\le e/4!=e/24\approx0{,}113$.

   </Collapsible>

2. Why does Theorem 2 not apply to the flat function of Counterexample 1?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Because no $M$ bounds all its derivatives on a neighbourhood of $0$: the polynomials $P_n(1/x)$ take arbitrarily large values as $n$ grows. The hypothesis is violated, and the counterexample shows that the conclusion is too.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. The library generated by a single identity

Computing $f^{(n)}(0)$ for every $n$ is almost always painful and often pointless. In practice we **manufacture** expansions from a single one, the geometric series, and the uniqueness of Theorem 1 guarantees that we obtain the right one.

**Method 1 (the four moves).** Starting from $\dfrac1{1-x}=\sum_{n\ge0}x^n$ for $|x|<1$:

1. **substitute** $x\mapsto -x$, $x^2$, $-x^2$, $\lambda x$ — the radius transforms accordingly;
2. **differentiate** term by term (Proposition 1: same radius);
3. **integrate** term by term on an interior segment (lesson 07);
4. **multiply** by a power, or combine two expansions.

The resulting library, valid for $|x|<1$ unless stated otherwise:

| Function | Expansion | Obtained by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\dfrac1{1-x}$ | $\sum_{n\ge0}x^{n}$ | the starting identity |
| $\dfrac1{1+x}$ | $\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^nx^{n}$ | substitution $x\mapsto-x$ |
| $\dfrac1{(1-x)^2}$ | $\sum_{n\ge1}n\,x^{\,n-1}$ | differentiation |
| $-\ln(1-x)$ | $\sum_{n\ge1}\dfrac{x^{n}}{n}$ | integration |
| $\arctan x$ | $\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{2n+1}$ | substitution then integration |
| $e^{x}$ | $\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{x^{n}}{n!}$ | Theorem 2, infinite radius |
| $\cosh x$ | $\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{x^{2n}}{(2n)!}$ | half-sum of $e^x$ and $e^{-x}$ |
| $(1+x)^{\alpha}$ | $\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{\alpha(\alpha-1)\cdots(\alpha-n+1)}{n!}x^{n}$ | binomial series |

**Definition 3 (complex exponential).** The <Def id="exponentielle-complexe">**complex exponential**</Def> is defined by the same series, which converges for every complex $z$ since its radius is infinite:

$$
e^{z}=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}.
$$

Separating the even-index and odd-index terms in $e^{i\theta}$, we recognise the expansions of Example 2 and obtain Euler's formula:

$$
e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta .
$$

This is the precise meaning of the epigraph of lesson 08: the real expansions of $\cos$ and $\sin$ are only two halves of a single complex object.

**Remark (inconclusive case).** Substituting changes the radius and it must be recomputed: from $1/(1-x)$ of radius $1$, the substitution $x\mapsto x^2$ gives $1/(1-x^2)$ of radius $1$, but $x\mapsto 3x$ gives radius $1/3$. Never copy the original radius without checking.

### Mini-exercises

1. Expand $\dfrac{1}{2-x}$ and give its radius.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Write $\dfrac1{2-x}=\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac1{1-x/2}=\dfrac12\sum_{n\ge0}\left(\dfrac x2\right)^{n}=\sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{x^{n}}{2^{\,n+1}}$, valid for $|x/2|<1$, hence of radius $2$.

   </Collapsible>

2. Deduce $\sum_{n\ge1}n/2^n$ from the library.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   From $\sum_{n\ge1}nx^{n-1}=1/(1-x)^2$, multiply by $x$: $\sum_{n\ge1}nx^{n}=x/(1-x)^2$. At $x=1/2$, legitimate since $|x|<1$, we obtain $(1/2)/(1/4)=2$.

   </Collapsible>

## Worked example — solving a differential equation by power series

Let us find the solution of $y'-2xy=0$ with $y(0)=1$, in power series form.

**Step 1 — set the unknown and earn the right to differentiate.** Look for $y(x)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n$ with radius $R>0$. On $(-R,R)$, Proposition 1 and lesson 07 authorise term-by-term differentiation:

$$
y'(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}n\,a_nx^{\,n-1}.
$$

*Why?* We do not differentiate an infinite sum out of habit: it is Proposition 1 (same radius) that makes the hypothesis of lesson 07 checkable.

**Step 2 — align the exponents before identifying.** The equation reads

$$
\sum_{n\ge1}n\,a_nx^{\,n-1}=2\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^{\,n+1}.
$$

Set $m=n-1$ on the left and $m=n+1$ on the right, so that both sides concern $x^m$.

*Why?* Two power series can be identified coefficient by coefficient only if they are written on the **same** power; without an index shift, the identification is false.

**Step 3 — identify, thanks to uniqueness.** The coefficient of $x^0$ gives $a_1=0$. For $m\ge1$ we obtain

$$
(m+1)\,a_{m+1}=2\,a_{m-1},\qquad\text{that is}\qquad a_{m+1}=\frac{2\,a_{m-1}}{m+1}.
$$

With $a_0=y(0)=1$ and $a_1=0$, all odd-index coefficients vanish, and $a_{2k}=1/k!$ by induction.

*Why?* Identifying coefficients is legitimate only by Theorem 1 — it is uniqueness, not a computation, that authorises writing a term-by-term equality.

**Step 4 — recognise the sum and compute the radius.** We obtain

$$
y(x)=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{x^{2k}}{k!}=e^{x^2},
$$

by substituting $x\mapsto x^2$ in Example 1. The radius is infinite, which validates the assumption $R>0$ of Step 1 after the fact.

*Why?* A series solution is complete only once its radius is established: without it, we have manipulated a formal expression with no domain of validity.

<Collapsible title="Your turn — answer">

Solve likewise $y'=y$ with $y(0)=1$, then $(1+x)y'=\alpha y$ with $y(0)=1$.

For the first: $\sum_{n\ge1}na_nx^{n-1}=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^n$ gives, after alignment, $(m+1)a_{m+1}=a_m$, so $a_m=1/m!$ and $y=e^x$, of infinite radius.

For the second, identification gives $(m+1)a_{m+1}+m\,a_m=\alpha a_m$, that is $a_{m+1}=a_m(\alpha-m)/(m+1)$, which rebuilds exactly the binomial coefficients, so $y=(1+x)^\alpha$. The ratio $|a_{m+1}/a_m|\to1$ gives a radius equal to $1$ — and this time it is finite, which can be seen from the singularity of $(1+x)^\alpha$ at $x=-1$.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Confusing "the Taylor series converges" with "it converges to $f$".** These are questions 2 and 3. The flat function $e^{-1/x^2}$ answers yes to the first and no to the second.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believing that $C^\infty$ implies expandable.** The implication is false, and bluntly so: the series can be zero while the function is not.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Identifying coefficients without aligning the exponents.** Shift the indices first so that both sides concern the same power, otherwise the identification compares terms that do not correspond.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Copying the radius after a substitution.** $x\mapsto3x$ divides the radius by $3$, $x\mapsto x^2$ replaces it by its square root. Recompute every time.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting to conclude on the radius when solving an equation.** Until the radius is established, the series obtained is only a formal expression with no domain of validity.

</Pitfall>

<GuidingAnswer>

A function can betray all its derivatives because the Taylor series uses only **pointwise** information: the values $f^{(n)}(0)$. But knowing every derivative at one point does not determine the function elsewhere, and $e^{-1/x^2}$ proves it in the sharpest way: it is flat at $0$ to every order, so its Taylor series is zero, while it is strictly positive everywhere else.

What must therefore be checked is not a property of the series but a property of the function **on a whole interval**: that the remainder $R_n=f-T_n$ tends to $0$. Theorem 2 gives the usable version — derivatives bounded by one same $M$ on $[-a,a]$ — and the factorial in the denominator then does all the work. That is how $e^x$, $\sin$ and $\cos$ are expandable on the whole of $\mathbb R$.

In practice we almost never check that: we **manufacture** expansions from the geometric series by substitution, differentiation and integration, and the uniqueness of Theorem 1 guarantees that the result obtained really is the Taylor series sought. It is the same trade as everywhere in this track — replacing a costly verification by the reuse of an already proved result.

</GuidingAnswer>

## Summary — three questions never to be confused

<KeyResults title="The decision sheet">

| Question asked | What is needed | Conclusion | Inconclusive case |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| does the Taylor series exist? | $f$ of class $C^\infty$ | yes, always | says nothing more |
| does it converge? | compute the radius (lesson 08) | domain of convergence | says nothing about $f$ |
| does it converge to $f$? | $R_n(x)\to0$ | $f$ expandable | this is the only real question |
| derivatives bounded on $[-a,a]$ | one $M$ common to every $n$ | expandable, with a remainder bound | sufficient criterion only |
| two expansions coincide | Theorem 1 | same coefficients | — |
| derived series | Proposition 1 | same radius | the boundary may change |

**Proofs worth being able to reconstruct.** The factor $n$ is crushed by a geometric ratio, so differentiation does not change the radius; differentiating $k$ times and evaluating at $0$ isolates $k!\,a_k$; Taylor–Lagrange plus a factorial in the denominator gives convergence to $f$; and the mechanism of the flat function, where every power of $1/x$ is dominated by $e^{1/x^2}$.

**The idea to take away.** A Taylor series is built from local data and claims to describe a function globally. That claim must be **paid for**, either by controlling the remainder or by reusing an expansion already obtained. Nothing grants it for free — not even infinitely many derivatives.

</KeyResults>
