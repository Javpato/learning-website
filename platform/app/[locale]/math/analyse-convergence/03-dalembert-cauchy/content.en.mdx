# Choosing between ratio and root, then handling the boundary

<LessonMeta id="math-an-c03" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

When a term contains factorials or a power with exponent $n$, looking at its growth factor often reveals its nature in a single line—except exactly at the boundary.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="The intensity of a photon after successive scattering events in a cavity">

**Situation:** after the $n$-th scattering event in a cavity, a photon contributes the intensity $u_n=a^n\,n!/n^n$, with $a\ge0$ determined by the wall material.
**Precise question:** do the cumulative contributions remain finite for $a=2{,}5$, then for $a=3$? What exact value of $a$ separates the two behaviours?
**The obstacle:** the factor between two scattering events changes with $n$; the constant-ratio formula used for the exponential signal no longer applies.
**The contract:** this chapter provides 3 tools (inspect the ratio, extract a root, analyse the boundary). By the end, we will decide the two settings and the limiting value, with figures to support the conclusions.

</Mission>

*Feeling confident? [Go straight to the TD (tutorial sheet)](../td-2/)—the theory will still be here when you get stuck.*

## The battle plan

To decide without expanding long sums, we need three tools.

1. **Compare two consecutive terms:** eliminate factorials and products.
2. **Extract the average exponential growth:** simplify expressions raised to the power $n$.
3. **Recognise the boundary and change tools:** return to the term, take a logarithm, or find an asymptotic equivalent.

## 1. Comparing two consecutive terms

**Tool 1—calculate the factor from one index to the next.** This is the first reflex when $u_n$ contains a factorial, a product, or powers whose exponents shift with $n$.

Problem: the quotient $u_{n+1}/u_n$ is generally not constant, so the formula for a <Terme id="serie-geometrique">geometric series</Terme> does not apply directly.
We therefore need a test that asks only which number this quotient approaches.

**Theorem 1 (ratio test).** Let $\sum u_n$ be a <Terme id="serie-numerique">numerical series</Terme> whose terms are non-zero from some index onwards. The <Def id="regle-de-d-alembert">**ratio test**</Def> (inspecting the factor from one index to the next) consists in calculating

$$\ell=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\left|\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}\right|.$$

- if $\ell<1$, the series $\sum |u_n|$ converges, so <Terme id="serie-convergente">the series $\sum u_n$ converges</Terme>;
- if $\ell>1$, the term $u_n$ does not tend to $0$, so the series diverges.

<Collapsible title="Proof—comparing the tail with a geometric series">

First suppose that $\ell<1$. Choose a number $q$ such that $\ell<q<1$. By the definition of the limit, there is an index $N$ such that, for every $n\ge N$,

$$\left|\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}\right|\le q.$$

Repeating this inequality gives

$$|u_{N+k}|\le |u_N|q^k.$$

The tail $\sum_{k\ge0}|u_{N+k}|$ is therefore bounded above by a convergent geometric series. The <Terme id="critere-de-comparaison">comparison test</Terme> gives the convergence of $\sum|u_n|$, and hence that of $\sum u_n$.

Now suppose that $\ell>1$. Choose $q$ such that $1<q<\ell$. From some index onwards, $|u_{n+1}|\ge q|u_n|$: the absolute values increase and cannot tend to $0$. The necessary condition from the chapter on numerical series implies divergence. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If $\ell=1$, Theorem 1 gives **no verdict**. If the limit does not exist, this version of the test is inconclusive as well. In both situations, return to the term or choose another tool; never write “therefore divergent” from $\ell=1$.

**Example (physics) 1.** For the photon intensity,

$$
\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}
=a\,\frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1)^{n+1}}\frac{n^n}{n!}
=a\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n.
$$

Now $(1+1/n)^n\to e$, so

$$\ell=\frac ae.$$

Theorem 1 already gives two regions: if $0\le a<e$, the cumulative contributions remain finite; if $a>e$, they do not. The setting $a=e$ will be handled with Tool 3.

**Remark (laboratory notation).** In a TP (practical class), you would be more likely to denote by $I_n$ the intensity after the $n$-th scattering event and by $G$ the wall setting. Here $I_n=u_n$ and $G=a$: the same discrete signal in different clothes.

**Example 2.** For $v_n=3^n/n!$,

$$\left|\frac{v_{n+1}}{v_n}\right|=\frac{3}{n+1}\longrightarrow0<1.$$

The series $\sum 3^n/n!$ converges by Theorem 1. The factorial has simplified to a single factor: this is the typical signal that calls for the ratio.

## 2. Extracting the average exponential growth

**Tool 2—take the root of order $n$.** When the whole term looks like an $n$-th power, the root removes that power in a single step.

Problem: for $u_n=b_n^n$, the quotient of two indices mixes $b_n$, $b_{n+1}$, and their exponents.
We therefore need a test that directly measures the average exponential size of $u_n$.

**Theorem 2 (root test).** The <Def id="regle-de-cauchy">**root test**</Def> (extracting the average exponential factor) consists in calculating

$$\rho=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\sqrt[n]{|u_n|}.$$

- if $\rho<1$, the series $\sum |u_n|$ converges, so $\sum u_n$ converges;
- if $\rho>1$, the term $u_n$ does not tend to $0$, so the series diverges.

<Collapsible title="Proof—the root produces a geometric upper bound">

If $\rho<1$, choose $q$ such that $\rho<q<1$. From some index onwards,

$$\sqrt[n]{|u_n|}\le q,\qquad\text{donc}\qquad |u_n|\le q^n.$$

The comparison test with the geometric series $\sum q^n$ gives convergence.

If $\rho>1$, choose $q$ such that $1<q<\rho$. For every sufficiently large index, $|u_n|\ge q^n$; the term does not tend to $0$, so the series diverges. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** If $\rho=1$, Theorem 2 says nothing; if the limit does not exist, its elementary version says nothing either. A root equal to $1$ is neither proof of convergence nor proof of divergence.

**Example 3.** Let

$$v_n=\left(\frac{2n+1}{3n-1}\right)^n.$$

The root immediately reveals

$$\sqrt[n]{v_n}=\frac{2n+1}{3n-1}\longrightarrow\frac23<1.$$

The series $\sum v_n$ converges by Theorem 2. With the ratio, we would have had to compare two fractions raised to two different exponents.

**Example (physics) 4.** For the photon and $a>0$,

$$\sqrt[n]{u_n}=a\,\frac{(n!)^{1/n}}{n}.$$

We have $(n!)^{1/n}/n\to1/e$, so the root test recovers $\rho=a/e$ and exactly the two regions from Example (physics) 1.

<Collapsible title="Justification—why the root of the factorial produces e">

Take the logarithm:

$$
\ln\left(\frac{(n!)^{1/n}}n\right)
=\frac1n\sum_{k=1}^{n}\ln\left(\frac{k}{n}\right).
$$

This sum is a Riemann sum for the improper integral $\int_0^1\ln x\,dx=-1$. It therefore tends to $-1$. Exponentiating gives $(n!)^{1/n}/n\to e^{-1}=1/e$.

</Collapsible>

**Method 1 (choosing between ratio and root).** Read the form before calculating.

| Signal in $u_n$ | First step | Intended effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| factorial, product, neighbouring terms | $|u_{n+1}/u_n|$ | cancel almost all the factors |
| entire expression raised to the power $n$ | $\sqrt[n]{|u_n|}$ | remove the exponent $n$ |
| fixed power such as $n^3$ | neither one as a priority | compare with a known series |
| both steps are simple | choose the shorter one | both boundaries are the same |

## 3. Recognising the boundary and changing tools

**Tool 3—refuse the false verdict.** The number $1$ is a silent boundary: it tells you to look more closely, not to guess.

Problem: two series of opposite natures can produce exactly the same limit $1$ with both the ratio and the root.
We therefore need to name this situation so that it becomes a signal to change methods.

**Definition 1 (inconclusive case).** An <Def id="cas-douteux">**inconclusive case**</Def> (the test reaches its boundary and falls silent) is a situation in which the limit of the ratio or root equals $1$.

**Counterexample 1 (the boundary does not mean divergence).** The statement “if $u_{n+1}/u_n\to1$, then $\sum u_n$ diverges” is false. Consider $\sum 1/n^2$. The hypothesis does hold because $u_{n+1}/u_n=n^2/(n+1)^2\to1$. Yet the conclusion fails because this <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> converges: its exponent $2$ exceeds $1$.

**Counterexample 2 (the boundary does not mean convergence).** The statement “if $u_{n+1}/u_n\to1$, then $\sum u_n$ converges” is false. Consider the <Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme> $\sum1/n$. The hypothesis does hold because $u_{n+1}/u_n=n/(n+1)\to1$. Yet the conclusion fails because its partial sums are unbounded.

The root also gives $1$ in both examples. Thus, “limit equal to $1$” means only “inconclusive test”.

**Method 2 (escaping an inconclusive case).**

1. first check whether $u_n\to0$; if not, divergence is immediate;
2. if there is a power or a product, take the logarithm to turn products into sums and exponents into factors;
3. use the <Terme id="formule-de-taylor-young">Taylor-Young formula</Terme> at the first non-zero order;
4. then look for an <Terme id="equivalent">asymptotic equivalent</Terme> and name the reference series;
5. write two distinct conclusions: “the first test is inconclusive”, then “the new argument decides”.

**Example (physics) 5.** At the boundary $a=e$, the intensity ratio is

$$r_n=\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=e\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n.$$

Theorem 1 finds $r_n\to1$ and falls silent. Apply Method 2:

$$
\ln r_n=1-n\ln\left(1+\frac1n\right)
=\frac1{2n}+O\!\left(\frac1{n^2}\right).
$$

This quantity is positive from some index onwards, so $r_n>1$: the intensities $u_n$ eventually increase and do not tend to $0$. The sum diverges at $a=e$. This is the boundary mechanism explored in exercise math-an-td2-03.

| Wall setting | Limit of the ratio | Final verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $0\le a<e$ | $a/e<1$ | finite sum, Theorem 1 |
| $a=e$ | $1$ | non-finite sum, after analysing $\ln r_n$ |
| $a>e$ | $a/e>1$ | non-finite sum, Theorem 1 |

**Example 6.** Set

$$w_n=e\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n-1.$$

The preceding expansion, followed by $e^x-1\sim x$ at $0$, gives

$$w_n\sim\frac1{2n}.$$

The series $\sum w_n$ therefore diverges like the harmonic series. The logarithm is not decoration: it revealed the reference series that the ratio was hiding.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $$\ln r_n=\frac1{2n}+O\!\left(\frac1{n^2}\right)>0$$

   from some index onwards. Thus $r_n>1$, the $u_n$ do not tend to $0$, and the series diverges.

   </Collapsible>

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   These are two Riemann series: the exponent $3/2>1$ gives convergence of the first, while the exponent $1$ gives harmonic divergence of the second. The inconclusive case specifically requires this change of tool.

   </Collapsible>

## Worked example—choosing the step that really simplifies

**Example 7—handling factorials with the ratio.** Study

$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}v_n,\qquad v_n=\frac{2^n(n!)^2}{(2n)!}.$$

**Step 1—check the setting and choose the ratio.** The terms are positive and non-zero; the three factorials shift by one index.

*Why?* Method 1 associates factorials with Theorem 1 because the quotient turns each factorial into a few simple factors.

**Step 2—simplify before taking the limit.** Write

$$
\frac{v_{n+1}}{v_n}
=\frac{2^{n+1}((n+1)!)^2}{(2n+2)!}\frac{(2n)!}{2^n(n!)^2}
=\frac{2(n+1)^2}{(2n+2)(2n+1)}
=\frac{n+1}{2n+1}.
$$

*Why?* Theorem 1 concerns the simplified quotient; expanding the factorials into long lists would hide the cancellations that make the calculation short.

**Step 3—calculate the limit and locate it.** We obtain

$$\ell=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{n+1}{2n+1}=\frac12<1.$$

*Why?* The number alone is not enough: its position relative to the boundary $1$ selects the first conclusion of Theorem 1.

**Step 4—write the verdict and name the test.** By the ratio test, the series $\sum v_n$ converges.

*Why?* A written solution must present positivity, the quotient, its limit, the comparison with $1$, and the name of the test; this is the mechanism of exercise math-an-td2-01.

**Example 8—your turn, with less help.** Study

$$\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\left(\frac{3n+2}{4n-1}\right)^n.$$

**Your turn:** choose between ratio and root, calculate the relevant limit, then write a sentence stating the verdict.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

The $n$-th power calls for the root test:

$$
\sqrt[n]{\left(\frac{3n+2}{4n-1}\right)^n}
=\frac{3n+2}{4n-1}\longrightarrow\frac34<1.
$$

The series converges by Theorem 2. The ratio would have worked, but at the cost of unnecessary shifted exponents. This is the mechanism of exercise math-an-td2-02.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Understanding question—answer">

**Why is the fact that $u_{11}/u_{10}<1$ for $a=2{,}5$ not enough, by itself, to prove that the entire sum is finite?**

A single ratio describes only the transition from index $10$ to index $11$. Theorem 1 requires the behaviour of all sufficiently distant ratios, captured by their limit. Here the limit $2{,}5/e<1$ guarantees that a single number $q<1$ eventually bounds all these ratios from above; it is this lasting geometric domination, rather than a one-off decrease, that proves convergence.

</Collapsible>

**Cycle 2—looking closely at the boundary.**

- **Predict.** Since $r_n(e)$ approaches $1$, can Theorem 1 alone tell you whether it approaches from above or below?
- **Act.** Multiply each $b_n$ by $e\approx2{,}7183$, then compare $r_n(e)-1$ between $n=10$ and $n=50$.
- **Observe.** The positive gap shrinks from $0{,}0480$ to $0{,}0099$: the limit equals $1$, but the intensities continue to increase.

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Confusing a ratio below $1$ with a limit below $1$.** A few decreases are not enough. The ratio test requires the limit of the ratio, or at least an upper bound by a single $q<1$ from some index onwards.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Drawing a conclusion when the limit equals $1$.** The series $\sum1/n^2$ and $\sum1/n$ both give ratio and root equal to $1$ in the limit, but their natures are opposite. Write “inconclusive case”, then change tools.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Reversing the quotient without reversing the verdict.** The theorem is stated with $|u_{n+1}/u_n|$. If you calculate $|u_n/u_{n+1}|$, a limit greater than $1$ then corresponds to convergence, not divergence.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the general-term test at the boundary.** When the test falls silent, first check whether $u_n\to0$. For the photon with $a=e$, the terms eventually increase: divergence is then immediate, without calculating the partial sums.

</Pitfall>

<MissionSolved>

**1. Calculate the factor between two scattering events.** By Example (physics) 1,

$$
r_n(a)=\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=a\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^n
\longrightarrow\frac ae.
$$

This calculation selects Tool 1: the factorials disappear in the quotient.

**2. Decide the two numerical settings.** For $a=2{,}5$,

$$\ell=\frac{2{,}5}{e}\approx0{,}9197<1.$$

By Theorem 1, the cumulative contributions remain finite. For $a=3$,

$$\ell=\frac3e\approx1{,}1036>1.$$

By the same theorem, $u_n$ does not tend to $0$ and the cumulative contributions do not remain finite.

**3. Resolve the boundary exactly.** The equality $a/e=1$ gives $a=e$. At this value, the ratio test is in the inconclusive case of Definition 1. Tool 3 and Example (physics) 5 give

$$\ln r_n(e)=\frac1{2n}+O\!\left(\frac1{n^2}\right)>0$$

from some index onwards. The intensities then increase and do not tend to $0$: the sum is not finite at the threshold.

Thus, for the physical model $a\ge0$, **the cumulative contributions remain finite exactly when $0\le a<e$**. They are finite for $a=2{,}5$, non-finite for $a=3$, and the exact separating value is $e\approx2{,}7183$, excluded from the finiteness region.

</MissionSolved>

## Lesson summary

You now have the three abilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="Key takeaways">

- **Choose the ratio:** factorials, products, and neighbouring terms call for $|u_{n+1}/u_n|$; a limit strictly below $1$ gives convergence, and one strictly above gives divergence.
- **Choose the root:** an expression raised to the power $n$ calls for $\sqrt[n]{|u_n|}$; the same threshold $1$ applies.
- **Name the boundary:** a ratio or root with limit $1$ means “inconclusive case”, never “convergence” or “divergence”.
- **Change tools:** check whether $u_n\to0$, take a logarithm, apply Taylor-Young, find an asymptotic equivalent, then name a reference series.
- **Write the argument:** state the test, calculate and simplify, give the limit, compare it with $1$, then write the verdict—or explicitly state that the test is inconclusive.
- **For the photon:** $u_{n+1}/u_n\to a/e$ and the sum is finite exactly when $0\le a<e$; at the threshold $a=e$, the finer analysis shows divergence.

</KeyResults>
