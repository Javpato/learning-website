<GuidingQuestion>

A <Terme id="serie-geometrique">geometric series</Terme> is settled by its constant ratio. **If the quotient or the root varies with $n$ but approaches a limit, is that local speed enough to settle the whole series?**

</GuidingQuestion>

## 1. D'Alembert: comparing two consecutive terms

When a term contains a factorial or a product, the quotient $u_{n+1}/u_n$ often makes almost the whole expression disappear.

Problem: a variable ratio close to a number $L<1$ ought eventually to be dominated by a fixed geometric ratio $r<1$.

**Theorem 1 (ratio test).** Let $\sum u_n$ be a <Terme id="serie-numerique">numerical series</Terme> whose terms are non-zero from some index on. <Def id="regle-de-d-alembert">**D'Alembert's rule**</Def> studies

$$
\left|\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}\right|\longrightarrow L.
$$

- If $L<1$, the series converges absolutely.
- If $L>1$ or $L=+\infty$, the term $u_n$ does not tend to $0$ and the series diverges.
- If $L=1$, the rule concludes nothing.

<Proof title="manufacturing a geometric domination">

**Case $L<1$.** Choose a number $r$ with $L<r<1$. By definition of the limit, there exists $N_0$ such that, for $n\ge N_0$,

$$
|u_{n+1}|\le r|u_n|.
$$

Repeating the inequality,

$$
|u_{N_0+k}|\le |u_{N_0}|r^k.
$$

The tail of $\sum|u_n|$ is dominated by a convergent geometric series. The <Terme id="critere-de-comparaison">comparison test</Terme> gives absolute convergence.

**Case $L>1$.** Choose $r$ with $1<r<L$. From some index on, $|u_{n+1}|\ge r|u_n|$: the moduli do not tend to $0$. The necessary condition of the previous lesson gives divergence.

**What the proof teaches.** The test is not an isolated formula: it reduces a complicated series to a geometric one. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** When $L=1$, no ratio $r<1$ can permanently bound the quotient. The tool must be changed.

**Example 1.** For $u_n=n!/n^n$,

$$
\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}
=\frac{(n+1)!}{(n+1)^{n+1}}\frac{n^n}{n!}
=\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^n\longrightarrow e^{-1}<1.
$$

The series converges. The factorial was the useful signal; the quotient removed it.

### Mini-exercises

1. For $u_n=3^n/n!$, compute the limit of the ratio.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $u_{n+1}/u_n=3/(n+1)\to0$: the series converges absolutely.

   </Collapsible>

2. For $u_n=1/n^2$, the ratio tends to $1$. What does d'Alembert conclude?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   Nothing. The series converges by Riemann, but the ratio test is silent.

   </Collapsible>

## 2. Cauchy: extracting the average exponential factor

When the whole term is raised to the power $n$, taking an $n$-th root can reveal its exponential base directly.

Problem: we must turn information about $|u_n|^{1/n}$ into a bound on $|u_n|$.

**Theorem 2 (root test).** <Def id="regle-de-cauchy">**Cauchy's rule**</Def> studies

$$
\sqrt[n]{|u_n|}\longrightarrow L.
$$

- If $L<1$, the series converges absolutely.
- If $L>1$ or $L=+\infty$, the series diverges.
- If $L=1$, the rule concludes nothing.

<Proof title="raising a geometric bound to the power n">

If $L<1$, choose $r$ with $L<r<1$. From some index on,

$$
\sqrt[n]{|u_n|}\le r,
$$

so $|u_n|\le r^n$. Comparison with $\sum r^n$ gives absolute convergence.

If $L>1$, choose $r$ with $1<r<L$. Then $|u_n|\ge r^n$ from some index on; in particular $u_n$ does not tend to $0$.

**What the proof teaches.** Ratio and root build the same geometric comparison, but they do not simplify the same shapes. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Example 2.** For

$$
u_n=\left(\frac{2n+1}{3n-4}\right)^n,
$$

we have $\sqrt[n]{u_n}=(2n+1)/(3n-4)\to2/3<1$: the series converges.

**Method 1 (choosing between ratio and root).**

| Dominant shape | First attempt | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $n!$, product, multiplicative recurrence | ratio | two consecutive indices simplify |
| whole expression to the power $n$ | root | it removes the exponent |
| rational fraction, logarithm | <Terme id="equivalent">equivalent</Terme> | Riemann comparison is more direct |

### Mini-exercises

1. Which test would you try for $u_n=n^4/5^n$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The ratio: $u_{n+1}/u_n=((n+1)/n)^4/5\to1/5$.

   </Collapsible>

2. Which test would you try for $u_n=[(n+2)/(2n+1)]^n$?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The root: it equals $(n+2)/(2n+1)\to1/2$ directly.

   </Collapsible>

## 3. The frontier L = 1: two opposite answers

Problem: a rule is understood only once you know exactly where its proof stops working.

**Definition 1 (doubtful case).** The <Def id="cas-douteux">**doubtful case**</Def> is the situation where the limit of the ratio or of the root equals $1$.

That name means "inconclusive test", never "series of doubtful behaviour".

**Counterexample 1 (no verdict at the frontier).** For every $\alpha>0$,

$$
u_n=\frac1{n^\alpha}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^\alpha\longrightarrow1.
$$

Yet the <Terme id="serie-harmonique">harmonic series</Terme> $\alpha=1$ diverges and the <Terme id="serie-de-riemann">Riemann series</Terme> $\alpha=2$ converges.

**Why does the proof fail?** When $L=1$, we can no longer choose $r$ with $L<r<1$. The dominating geometric series that carried the whole proof disappears.

**Method 2 (getting out of the doubtful case).**

1. First look for a positive equivalent and a Riemann series.
2. If the term contains $(1+x_n)^{y_n}$, take the logarithm.
3. If two dominant terms cancel, use a <Terme id="formule-de-taylor-young">Taylor-Young expansion</Terme> precise enough.
4. Never repeat the same test in another form without a reason.

**Example 3.** For $u_n=(1+1/n)^{-n\ln n}$,

$$
\ln u_n=-n\ln n\,\ln(1+1/n)\sim-\ln n,
$$

so $u_n\sim1/n$. The series diverges. The root would have tended to $1$ and said nothing.

### Mini-exercises

1. The ratio of $u_n=1/[n(\ln n)^2]$ tends to $1$. Which tool decides?

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   The series-integral comparison: this is a Bertrand series with logarithmic exponent $2>1$, so it converges.

   </Collapsible>

2. Give two series of opposite behaviour for which the $n$-th root tends to $1$.

   <Collapsible title="Answer">

   $\sum1/n$ diverges and $\sum1/n^2$ converges; in both cases $(1/n^\alpha)^{1/n}\to1$.

   </Collapsible>

## 4. Understanding and using a term O(1/n²)

In asymptotic expansions, an expression such as $O(1/n^2)$ denotes neither a single function nor an automatically convergent series. It asserts a precise bound.

**Definition 2 (big O for a sequence).** Writing

$$
r_n=O(v_n)
$$

means that there exist $C>0$ and $N_0$ such that $|r_n|\le C|v_n|$ for every $n\ge N_0$.

**Proposition 1 (summability of a controlled remainder).** If $r_n=O(1/n^p)$ with $p>1$, then $\sum r_n$ converges absolutely.

<Proof title="the O symbol hides a comparison, not a miracle">

By definition, there exists $C>0$ such that, for $n$ large enough,

$$
|r_n|\le\frac C{n^p}.
$$

The positive series $\sum C/n^p$ converges since $p>1$. The comparison test gives the convergence of $\sum|r_n|$, hence the absolute convergence of $\sum r_n$.

**What the proof teaches.** To justify "the remainder converges", the $O$ must be unfolded, the inequality produced and the reference series named. $\blacksquare$

</Proof>

**Remark (inconclusive case).** $r_n=O(1/n)$ is not enough: $r_n=1/n$ satisfies that estimate and its series diverges. Likewise $O(n)$ obviously implies no decay at all.

**Example 4.** If

$$
u_n=\frac{(-1)^n}{n}-\frac1{n^{3/2}}+O\!\left(\frac1{n^2}\right),
$$

the third series converges absolutely by Proposition 1. The first two pieces still have to be classified separately; that decomposition will be used in the next lesson.

## Worked example — the exact parameter of a family

Let us study $u_n=a^n n!/n^n$ for $a>0$.

**Step 1 — choose the ratio.** The factorial and the powers of neighbouring index call for d'Alembert.

*Why?* Method 1 chooses the move that simplifies, before any computation of a limit.

**Step 2 — simplify completely.**

$$
\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=a\left(\frac n{n+1}\right)^n\longrightarrow\frac ae.
$$

*Why?* The verdict depends on the number to be compared with $1$.

**Step 3 — handle the strict cases.** If $a<e$ the series converges; if $a>e$ it diverges.

*Why?* These are exactly the two conclusions of Theorem 1.

**Step 4 — isolate the frontier.** For $a=e$ the ratio tends to $1$: d'Alembert does not conclude.

*Why?* Writing "doubtful case" is information about the tool, not yet about the series.

**Step 5 — change scale.** Stirling's formula gives

$$
n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac ne\right)^n,
$$

so, when $a=e$, $u_n\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}$ and the term does not tend to $0$. The series diverges.

*Why?* The frontier demands a finer tool; here the equivalent even supplies an immediate divergence.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**Why is the result $L=1$ never a conclusion?**

Because the proofs of both rules need a geometric ratio strictly separated from $1$. At the frontier that comparison no longer exists, and opposite behaviours become possible.

</Collapsible>

## Classic traps

<Pitfall>

**Concluding when $L=1$.** The series $\sum1/n$ and $\sum1/n^2$ suffice to refute both possible conclusions.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the absolute value.** For a signed series, ratio and root apply to the moduli and prove absolute convergence.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Writing "$O(1/n^2)$ converges".** It is the series of a remainder $r_n$ that converges, because $|r_n|\le C/n^2$ from some index on.

</Pitfall>

<GuidingAnswer>

Ratio and root decide when they reveal an exponential speed strictly below or above $1$. Their proof compares with a <Terme id="serie-convergente">convergent</Terme> geometric series. At $L=1$ that comparison disappears: we must go back to the shape of the term and look for an equivalent, an integral or a more precise expansion.

</GuidingAnswer>

## Summary — the radar before the computation

<KeyResults title="Which signal, which tool?">

| Signal | Tool | Safe conclusion | Frontier |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| factorial, product | ratio | $L<1$ converges absolutely; $L>1$ diverges | $L=1$: nothing |
| expression to the power $n$ | root | same conclusions | $L=1$: nothing |
| fraction or logarithm | equivalent / integral | compare with Riemann or Bertrand | check the sign |
| $r_n=O(1/n^p)$ | absolute comparison | summable if $p>1$ | $p=1$: nothing |

**Proofs worth being able to reconstruct:** choosing $r$ between $L$ and $1$; iterating a ratio inequality; raising a root bound; unfolding a big O into a comparison.

</KeyResults>
