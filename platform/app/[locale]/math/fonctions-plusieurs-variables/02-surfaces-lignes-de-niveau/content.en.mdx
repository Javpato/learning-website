## Intuition

Imagine the surface $z = f(x, y)$ as a relief: above each point $(x, y)$ on the ground, an altitude $f(x, y)$.

- Cut the relief with a horizontal plane $z = c$ and project the cut onto the ground: we obtain the **level curve** with value $c$, like the altitude curves of an IGN map.
- Cut the relief by a vertical plane $y = b$: we obtain the graph of the **partial function** $x \mapsto f(x, b)$, an ordinary curve.

A well-read elevation map says it all: tight lines indicate a steep slope, spaced lines a plateau, closed loops a summit or basin, and X-shaped intersections a saddle.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Graph: $z=f(x,y)$; level curve: $L_c=\{(x,y)\in D:f(x,y)=c\}$.
- A partial function freezes a variable, for example $x\mapsto f(x,b)$.
- Tight levels signal a steep slope; spaced levels, a slight slope.

</KeyResults>

## Formal definition

A **scalar field** is a function

$$
f : D \subset \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R .
$$

For $n = 2$, its **graph** is the equation surface $z = f(x, y)$ in $\mathbb R^3$. The **level curve** of value $c$ is the set

$$
L_c = \{(x, y) \in D : f(x, y) = c\} \subset \mathbb R^2 .
$$

A **partial function** freezes all variables except one: $x \mapsto f(x, b)$ or $y \mapsto f(a, y)$.

### Prototypes to recognize at a glance

<Rappel title="Recognize a conic">

A sum of two squares equal to a positive constant gives an ellipse; a difference of squares gives a hyperbola. At level zero, the latter can be factorized into two lines.

</Rappel>

- **plan**: $f(x,y) = ax + by + c$ — levels: parallel lines;
- **elliptical paraboloid**: $\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{y^2}{b^2}$ — levels: nested ellipses;
- **hyperbolic paraboloid** (saddle): $\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{y^2}{b^2}$ — levels: hyperbolas, and two crossed lines at level $0$;
- **Gaussian hill**: $A\,e^{-Q(x,y)}$ with $Q$ positive definite — levels: ellipses, altitudes crashing towards $0$ in the distance;
- **radial potential**: $f(x,y) = g\bigl(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\bigr)$ — levels: circles centered at the origin;
- **Reaction energy surface**: local minima, saddles and valleys combined.

### The level sets method

To study an unknown scalar field:

1. determine **domain** $D$;
2. exploit **symmetries** (parity, rotation invariance, etc.);
3. solve $f(x, y) = c$ for a generic $c$;
4. identify how the **geometry changes with $c$** (ellipse? hyperbola? empty? point?);
5. inspect some **partial functions**;
6. identify **singular** or non-smooth points.

</Collapsible>

## Worked example

Let's apply the method to $f(x, y) = x^2 + 4y^2$.

**Step 1 — domain.** $D = \mathbb R^2$; there is no domain restriction.

**Step 2 — symmetries.** $f(-x, y) = f(x, -y) = f(x, y)$: symmetry about both axes. Plus $f \ge 0$ everywhere.

**Step 3 — levels.** For given $c$, $x^2 + 4y^2 = c$:

- if $c$ is strictly negative: **empty set** (the sum of squares cannot be negative);
- if $c = 0$: the single point $(0, 0)$;
- if $c$ is strictly positive: the **ellipse**

$$
\frac{x^2}{(\sqrt c\,)^2} + \frac{y^2}{(\sqrt c / 2)^2} = 1,
$$

half-axes $\sqrt c$ along $x$ and $\sqrt c / 2$ along $y$ — twice as “crushed” in $y$ as in $x$.

**Step 4 — evolution with $c$.** The ellipses grow as $\sqrt c$: for evenly spaced levels $c = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, the curves *tighten* away from the origin — signature of an increasing slope.

**Step 5 — partial functions.** $f(x, 0) = x^2$ and $f(0, y) = 4y^2$: two parabolas upward, the second four times steeper.

**Step 6 — conclusion.** The surface is an **elliptical paraboloid**, an anisotropic bowl: stiff in the $y$ direction, soft in the $x$ direction. In chemist's language: a minimum with a stiff mode and a soft mode.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — two separate level curves never intersect">

Let $c_1 \ne c_2$ be two values, and suppose that a point $(a, b)$ belongs to both $L_{c_1}$ and $L_{c_2}$. So, by definition,

$$
f(a, b) = c_1
\qquad\text{et}\qquad
f(a, b) = c_2 ,
$$

therefore $c_1 = c_2$ — contradiction. A *function* assigns only one value to each point: the curves of a level map therefore never intersect between different values. $\blacksquare$

Pay attention to the nuance: the line of the **same** value can perfectly intersect itself — this is exactly what happens at the neck of the $x^2 - y^2$ saddle, where $L_0$ is the pair of lines $y = \pm x$.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — the levels of a radial potential are circles">

Or $f(x, y) = g(r)$ with $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$. The level curve $L_c$ is the set of points where $g(r) = c$. This equation only concerns $r$: its solutions form a set of values $\{r_1, r_2, \dots\}$, and

$$
L_c = \bigcup_i \{(x,y) : x^2 + y^2 = r_i^2\}
$$

is a union of circles centered at the origin (possibly empty, or reduced to the origin if $r_i = 0$). Conversely, a field whose *all* levels are concentric circles is rotation invariant. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Link with chemistry

For molecular coordinates $(q_1, q_2)$ — bond lengths, angles — the **surface of potential energy** $V(q_1, q_2)$ can be read on its level map:

- the **minima** model the stable structures (reactants, products, intermediates);
- the **saddle points** mark the transition states: the reaction path passes through the X crossing of the level curves;
- the directions where the levels are **tight** are the *stiff* modes (rigid connections, high vibration frequencies);
- the directions where they are **spaced** are the *soft* modes (easy twists, large amplitudes).

Reading a PES means applying the level sets method to a molecule.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Confuse the graph and the level curves.** The graph lives in $\mathbb R^3$ (this is the surface $z = f(x,y)$); the level curves live in $\mathbb R^2$, the plane of variables. A level curve does not have a *drawn* altitude — its altitude is the $c$ label attached to it.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believe that each value $c$ gives a curve.** $L_c$ can be empty ($c$ negative for $x^2 + 4y^2$), an isolated point ($c = 0$), a meeting of several components (double well), or even straight lines which intersect (level of the neck of a saddle). Always discuss the possibilities *as $c$ varies*.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Misreading line spacing.** Tight level curves (for evenly spaced $c$ values) mean a **steep** slope, not weak: it takes little horizontal distance to gain a level. This is the opposite of the “dense = flat” intuition that some people import from weather maps.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forget the domain.** For $f(x,y) = \ln(x^2 + y^2)$ or $\sqrt{1 - x^2 - y^2}$, the study of levels begins with “where does the formula make sense?” A level that appears to cross a forbidden area is a sign that step 1 of the method has been skipped.

</Pitfall>

  <QItem>

    The level curve $c = 4$ of $f(x, y) = x^2 + 4y^2$ is…

    <QOption id="a">a circle of radius $2$</QOption>
    <QOption id="b" correct>an ellipse of semi-axes $2$ (along $x$) and $1$ (along $y$)</QOption>
    <QOption id="c">a hyperbola</QOption>
    <QFeedback of="a">

      This would be the case for $x^2 + y^2 = 4$. Here the coefficient $4$ in front of $y^2$ squashes the curve in the direction $y$: divide the equation by $4$ to put it in normalized form.

    </QFeedback>
    <QFeedback of="b">

      Correct: $x^2 + 4y^2 = 4$ is rewritten $\frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{y^2}{1} = 1$.

    </QFeedback>
    <QFeedback of="c">

      A hyperbola would require a **minus** sign between the two squares — that's the signature of the $x^2 - 4y^2$ saddle, not the paraboloid.

    </QFeedback>
    <QExplain>

      Standardized form: $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ with $a = \sqrt c$ and $b = \sqrt c/2$. The sign between the squares decides everything: plus = ellipse (bowl), minus = hyperbola (saddle).

    </QExplain>

  </QItem>
  <QItem>

    On a map, two level curves labeled $c = 1$ and $c = 2$ intersect at a point. What can we conclude from this?

    <QOption id="a">the crossing point is a saddle</QOption>
    <QOption id="b">the function is discontinuous</QOption>
    <QOption id="c" correct>the card is wrong: it is impossible for a function</QOption>
    <QFeedback of="a">

      A saddle makes the line of the **same** value cross with itself (like $y = \pm x$ at $0$ of the saddle) — never two *different* values.

    </QFeedback>
    <QFeedback of="b">

      Even discontinuous, a function assigns **only one** value to each point: it cannot be worth both $1$ and $2$ at the intersection.

    </QFeedback>
    <QFeedback of="c">

      Yes: at the crossing point, $f$ should equal $1$ *and* $2$ — a contradiction with the very definition of a function.

    </QFeedback>
    <QExplain>

      This is the collapsible proof above. Remember the nuance: *distinct* levels never intersect, but the line of the same level can intersect at a saddle — a valuable clue for identifying transition states on a PES.

    </QExplain>

  </QItem>

</Quiz>

<MissionSolved>

For $E=(q_1^2-1)^2+q_2^2$, the two valley floors are $(q_1,q_2)=(\pm1,0)$ with $E=0$. The central passage is $(0,0)$, energy $1$. Below $1$, the map shows two separate loops; at level $1$, they touch at the saddle; above, they merge. The level map therefore already tells the story of the reactant–product transition.

</MissionSolved>

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- Scalar field $f : D \subset \mathbb R^2 \to \mathbb R$: three readings — graph (surface), level curves (map), partial functions (vertical sections).
- $L_c = \{(x,y) : f(x,y) = c\}$ ; discuss its geometry **as $c$ varies** (empty, point, ellipse, hyperbola, several loops, etc.).
- Prototypes: plane, elliptical paraboloid, saddle, Gaussian hill, radial potential (circular levels).
- Method in 6 steps: domain → symmetries → solve $f = c$ → evolution with $c$ → partial functions → singular points.
- Tight lines = steep slope; closed loop = summit or basin; crossing in X of the same level = saddle.
- PES in chemistry: minima = stable structures, necks = transition states, tight/spaced = stiff/soft modes.

</KeyResults>
