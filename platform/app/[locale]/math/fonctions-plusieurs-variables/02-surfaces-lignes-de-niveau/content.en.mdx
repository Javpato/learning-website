# Reading a chemical reaction without drawing its 3D relief

<Mission fil="The life of a reaction on its energy surface — reading the landscape E(q₁,q₂)">

**Situation.** A molecule has two shapes and its energy is $E(q_1,q_2)=(q_1^2-1)^2+q_2^2$ eV.
**Precise question.** How many stable zones are there, where are they, and how much energy must be supplied?
**Obstacle.** In L1 (first year), you plotted a curve with one input; here two angles vary and the relief is hard
to read. **Contract.** In this chapter: 3 tools (attaching
a number to every shape, connecting the points of equal energy, then slicing one variable at a time).
By the end, we solve this problem, with the numbers to back it up.

</Mission>

## The battle plan

To honour the contract, we need three tools:

1. **represent** the energy attached to each geometry as a relief;
2. **replace** that relief with a flat map of equal values;
3. **slice** the landscape along one direction, then combine map and slices.

Each tool corresponds to a section below, always applied to the system $E(q_1,q_2)$.

## 1. Representing an energy that depends on two variables

**Tool 1 — represent.** Let's pin down what the formula assigns to each point, then how that
result becomes a relief.

Problem: each molecular geometry corresponds to a single number, here an energy. So we
need a word for this rule.

**Definition 1 (scalar field).** A <Def id="champ-scalaire">**scalar field**</Def> is a
function attaching a number to every point of a domain, like an energy or a concentration:

$$
f:D\subset\mathbb R^n\longrightarrow\mathbb R.
$$

**Example (chemistry) 1.** The formula $E:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ assigns an energy to $(q_1,q_2)$:

$$
E(-1,0)=0,\qquad E(0,0)=1,\qquad E(1,0)=0.
$$

These computations announce two low-energy geometries separated by a more costly one.

**Remark.** The rule $(q_1,q_2)\mapsto(q_1^2,q_2^2)$ is not a scalar field: it returns
two numbers. By contrast, $(q_1,q_2)\mapsto q_1^2+q_2^2$ is one.

Problem: a table does not show how the energy evolves between two geometries. So we
need an object that turns the value into a height.

**Definition 2 (graph).** For $f:D\subset\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$, the
<Def id="graphe">**graph**</Def> (the relief above the plane) is the surface of $\mathbb R^3$
formed by the points $(x,y,z)$ such that

$$
z=f(x,y).
$$

**Example 2.** For $f(x,y)=x^2+4y^2$, $(1,1,5)$ belongs to the graph because $5=1^2+4\cdot1^2$.
The point $(1,1,4)$ does not: above $(1,1)$, the only height is $5$.

**Remark.** With two inputs, the domain lives in $\mathbb R^2$ but the graph in $\mathbb R^3$.

## 2. Replacing the relief with a flat map

**Tool 2 — replace.** The 3D relief is impractical on a sheet of paper; the map keeps the values.

Problem: we want to know where the energy is worth exactly $c$ without drawing the whole surface.
So we need a name for the resulting curve.

**Definition 3 (level curve).** The <Def id="ligne-de-niveau">**level curve**</Def> of value
$c$ of $f:D\subset\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ is the set of points where $f$ takes that value:

$$
L_c=\{(x,y)\in D:f(x,y)=c\}.
$$

It is the altitude-$c$ curve of a topographic map, that is, a hiking map seen
from above.

**Example (chemistry) 3.** For the chapter's energy, the value $c=0$ forces a sum of squares to vanish:

$$
(q_1^2-1)^2+q_2^2=0.
$$

Each square must therefore be zero. We get exactly $(-1,0)$ and $(1,0)$: the map pinpoints the
two lowest-energy shapes.

**Remark.** A level curve can be empty, reduced to a point, made up of several loops
or formed by lines that cross.

Problem: with three variables, the points of equal value form a surface rather than a line.
So we need a name valid in every dimension.

**Definition 4 (level set).** For $f:D\subset\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$,
the <Def id="ensemble-de-niveau">**level set**</Def> of value $c$ is

$$
N_c=\{X\in D:f(X)=c\}.
$$

It generalises the level curve: with three variables, $N_c$ is often a surface.

**Example 4.** The sphere $x^2+y^2+z^2=4$ is the value-$4$ set of the field
$(x,y,z)\mapsto x^2+y^2+z^2$. For our two-variable energy, $N_c=L_c$.

**Remark (words of the chemical map).** A stable structure appears at the bottom of a
<Terme id="extremum-local">local minimum</Terme> (lower than its neighbours). The lowest
passage between two bottoms is often a <Terme id="point-selle">saddle point, or mountain pass</Terme>: there the relief
rises in one direction and falls in another. Computing them comes in lesson 6.

### Prototypes to recognise

<Rappel title="Recognising a conic">

A sum of two squares equal to a positive constant gives an ellipse; a difference gives
a hyperbola. At value zero, the difference may factor into two lines.

</Rappel>

| Formula | Relief and map |
| --- | --- |
| $f(x,y)=ax+by+d$ | plane; straight, parallel lines |
| $f(x,y)=x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2$ | elliptic paraboloid; nested ellipses |
| $f(x,y)=x^2/a^2-y^2/b^2$ | hyperbolic paraboloid; hyperbolas, then two lines at value $0$ |
| $f(x,y)=A e^{-Q(x,y)}$, with $Q$ positive away from the origin | Gaussian hill; ellipses whose value tends to $0$ far away |
| $f(x,y)=g\!\left(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\right)$ | radial potential; circles centred at the origin |
| $E(q_1,q_2)$ | reaction landscape; stable bottoms and a passage between them |

**Method 1 (reading a landscape by its values).** To study an unknown field:

1. determine its domain $D$;
2. exploit its symmetries to avoid repeated computations;
3. solve $f(x,y)=c$ for a general value $c$;
4. discuss the geometry according to $c$: empty, a point, an ellipse, a hyperbola or several pieces;
5. compare a few vertical slices, introduced in tool 3;
6. flag where the formula is not smooth or the map changes shape abruptly.

**Proposition 1 (reading the spacing).** For regularly spaced values $c$, curves close
together indicate a strong variation: little distance is enough to change $f$ a lot.
Curves far apart indicate a gentler variation.

In chemistry, a tightly packed direction is called *stiff*: the deformation quickly costs energy.
A widely spaced direction is called *soft*: the same deformation costs less.

**Proposition 2 (non-intersection).** Two curves associated with distinct values never
cross.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — non-intersection">

Let $c_1\ne c_2$. If $(a,b)$ belonged to $L_{c_1}$ and to $L_{c_2}$, Definition 3 would give

$$
f(a,b)=c_1\qquad\text{et}\qquad f(a,b)=c_2.
$$

Hence $c_1=c_2$, a contradiction: a function assigns only one value to each point.
$\blacksquare$

A curve of one **same** value can cross itself: for $f(x,y)=x^2-y^2$, $f=0$ gives
$y=\pm x$, two lines meeting at the saddle point.

</Collapsible>

**Proposition 3 (radial symmetry).** The curves of a potential that depends only on
$r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ are circles centred at the origin — possibly several, empty or reduced
to the origin.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — radial level sets">

For $f(x,y)=g(r)$, the equation $f=c$ becomes $g(r)=c$ and no longer depends on the angle.
If its positive solutions are $r_1,r_2,\ldots$, then

$$
L_c=\bigcup_i\{(x,y):x^2+y^2=r_i^2\}.
$$

It is a union of circles centred at the origin, empty if no $r$ works, reduced to the origin
if only $r=0$ works. Conversely, if all the curves are concentric circles, the value
does not change under a rotation. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## 3. Slicing the landscape one variable at a time

**Tool 3 — slice and combine.** A map shows the whole plane; a slice recovers an
ordinary curve and L1 computations.

Problem: to isolate the effect of $x$, we must hold $y$ fixed. So we need a tool
that temporarily turns the two-variable formula into a one-variable formula.

**Definition 5 (partial function).** A <Def id="fonction-partielle">**partial function**</Def>
(a vertical slice of the graph) is obtained by freezing all the variables but one:

$$
x\longmapsto f(x,b)
\qquad\text{ou}\qquad
y\longmapsto f(a,y).
$$

**Example (chemistry) 5.** For the chapter's energy, two useful slices are

$$
q_1\longmapsto E(q_1,0)=(q_1^2-1)^2,
\qquad
q_2\longmapsto E(1,q_2)=q_2^2.
$$

The first follows the direct route between the two shapes; the second measures the cost of a
deformation along $q_2$ around the shape $(1,0)$.

**Remark (chemical notation).** The letters $q_1,q_2$ signal internal coordinates:
angles, torsions or bond lengths. Writing $E(q_1,0)$ holds $q_2$ at zero. In
thermodynamics, an "at fixed pressure" subscript plays the same role.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep in sight">

- Graph: $z=f(x,y)$.
- Level curve: $L_c=\{(x,y)\in D:f(x,y)=c\}$.
- Partial function: $x\mapsto f(x,b)$ or $y\mapsto f(a,y)$.
- Regularly spaced values: tight curves = strong variation; spaced-out curves = gentle variation.

</KeyResults>

## Worked example — reconstructing a relief from its map and its slices

**Example 6.** Let's study $f(x,y)=x^2+4y^2$ completely with Method 1.

**Step 1 — determine the domain.** Squares are defined for all reals, so
$D=\mathbb R^2$.

*Why?* The domain keeps us from drawing where the formula makes no sense.

**Step 2 — exploit the symmetries and the sign.** We have $f(-x,y)=f(x,-y)=f(x,y)$ and
$f(x,y)\ge0$.

*Why?* The symmetries cut the drawing down by four; the sign sets the first value at $c=0$.

**Step 3 — solve the value equation.** By Definition 3, $L_c$ satisfies
$x^2+4y^2=c$.

*Why?* This equation classifies every value instead of guessing the map from a few points.

- if $c<0$, $L_c$ is empty because a sum of squares is not negative;
- if $c=0$, $L_c$ reduces to the point $(0,0)$;
- if $c>0$, we divide by $c$:

$$
\frac{x^2}{(\sqrt c)^2}
+\frac{y^2}{(\sqrt c/2)^2}=1.
$$

The curve is an ellipse with semi-axes $\sqrt c$ along $x$ and $\sqrt c/2$ along $y$.

**Step 4 — follow the evolution as $c$ increases.** The semi-axes grow like $\sqrt c$.
For $c=1,2,3,\ldots$, the ellipses bunch up far from the origin.

*Why?* Proposition 1 translates this spacing: the variation becomes stronger far from the centre.

**Step 5 — compute two partial functions.** By Definition 5,

$$
f(x,0)=x^2,\qquad f(0,y)=4y^2.
$$

*Why?* The slices reveal the costly direction and prepare the reconstruction of TD 2.1.

**Step 6 — gather the clues.** Nested ellipses and parabolic slices describe an
elliptic paraboloid: it rises everywhere, four times faster in value along $y$ than along $x$.

*Why?* The wrap-up links the three representations. In chemistry, $y$ would be a stiff mode and $x$
a softer one.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

Why do ellipses twice as short along $y$ signal a stronger variation?

At fixed value $c$, the curve is reached as soon as $|y|=\sqrt c/2$, versus $|x|=\sqrt c$. Half
as much displacement along $y$ earns the same value: the variation there is stronger.

</Collapsible>

**Example 7 — your turn, with less help.** For $h(x,y)=x^2/4-y^2$, the slices are an upward
parabola at fixed $y$ and a downward one at fixed $x$. **Your turn:** factor the value $0$, then
decide the orientation of the hyperbolas for $c=1$ and $c=-1$. Which prototype do you recognise?

<Collapsible title="Answer">

For $c=0$,

$$
\frac{x^2}{4}-y^2=\left(\frac x2-y\right)
\left(\frac x2+y\right)=0,
$$

so $y=\pm x/2$. For $c=1$, the hyperbolas open along $x$; for $c=-1$, along $y$.
The opposite slices characterise the hyperbolic paraboloid of TD 2.1.

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Confusing the graph and the map.** The graph $z=f(x,y)$ lives in $\mathbb R^3$; the map, in
$\mathbb R^2$. There, the height is carried by the label $c$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believing every value gives an ordinary curve.** A curve can be empty, a point, several
pieces or two crossing lines. You must discuss according to $c$, as in Example 6.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Reading the spacing backwards.** With regularly spaced values, tight curves mean
a strong variation: little distance is enough to reach the next value.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the domain.** For $f(x,y)=\ln(x^2+y^2)$, the origin is forbidden; for
$g(x,y)=\sqrt{1-x^2-y^2}$, only the unit disc is allowed. Method 1 starts there.

</Pitfall>

<MissionSolved>

**1. Locate the stable zones.** $E$ is a sum of squares, so $E(q_1,q_2)\ge0$. Equality requires
$q_2=0$ and $q_1^2=1$: two bottoms, $(-1,0)$ and $(1,0)$, of energy $0$ eV.

**2. Measure the minimal passage.** Definition 5 gives $E(q_1,0)=(q_1^2-1)^2$: the direct route
reaches $1$ eV at $q_1=0$. Every continuous route between the two bottoms passes through $q_1=0$,
where $E(0,q_2)=1+q_2^2\ge1$. The minimal barrier is therefore worth exactly $1$ eV.

**3. Read the transformation.** With Definition 3 and Method 1: two loops for $0<c<1$,
contact at $(0,0)$ at $c=1$, then one loop for $c>1$. The contract is fulfilled: two stable shapes
at $(\pm1,0)$ and $1$ eV to supply.

</MissionSolved>

## Lesson summary

You now have the three capabilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Represent:** recognise $f:D\subset\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ and its graph $z=f(x,y)$.
- **Replace the relief:** solve $L_c=\{(x,y)\in D:f(x,y)=c\}$ and discuss according to $c$.
- **Slice:** freeze one variable with $x\mapsto f(x,b)$ or $y\mapsto f(a,y)$, then cross the views.
- **Recognise:** plane, elliptic and hyperbolic paraboloids, Gaussian hill, radial potential.
- **Interpret:** tight curves = strong variation; a change in the number of loops = a passage.
- **Read a reaction:** low-energy bottoms = stable shapes; passage = energy to supply.

</KeyResults>
