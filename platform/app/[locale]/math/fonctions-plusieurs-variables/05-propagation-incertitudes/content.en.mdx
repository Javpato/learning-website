# Giving a reliable margin to a calculated density

<Mission fil="A density measured in TP (lab session) — reporting ρ with an honest margin">

**Situation.** In the laboratory, we measure $m=(12{,}50\pm0{,}01)\ \mathrm g$ and $V=(5{,}00\pm0{,}02)\ \mathrm{cm^3}$, then compute $\rho=m/V$.
**Precise question.** Which value of $\rho$ and which margin $\pm$ should be reported, and should we first improve the balance or the volume measurement?
**Obstacle.** In L1 (first year), only one input varied; here two small doubt zones pass through the same division.
**Contract.** In this chapter: 3 tools (quantifying each margin, combining the two effects, correcting when they move together). By the end, we solve this problem, numbers in hand.

</Mission>

## The battle plan

To fulfil the contract, we need three tools:

1. **quantify** the doubt zone attached to each measurement;
2. **combine** the contributions when the instruments work separately;
3. **correct** the computation when two errors tend to move together.

## 1. Quantifying the doubt zone of a measurement

**Tool 1 — quantifying.** Before pushing a margin through a formula, you must know exactly what the number placed after the symbol $\pm$ means.

Problem: the measured value alone does not tell the typical spread of the results that repetitions would give. So we need a number for this width.

**Definition 1 (standard uncertainty).** The <Def id="incertitude-type">**standard uncertainty**</Def> (the standard deviation attached to a measurement, hence the typical half-width of its doubt zone) of a quantity $x$ is written $u(x)$. Its square $u^2(x)$ is the associated variance.

**Example (chemistry) 1.** In the mission,

$$u(m)=0{,}01\ \mathrm g,\qquad u(V)=0{,}02\ \mathrm{cm^3}.$$

The unitless quotients

$$
\frac{u(m)}m=8{,}0\times10^{-4}=0{,}080\%,\qquad
\frac{u(V)}V=4{,}0\times10^{-3}=0{,}400\%
$$

compare the precisions of the two instruments: the volume is five times less precise, relative to its value.

**Remark (laboratory notation).** We often write $u(m)$ in a metrology report and $\sigma_m$ in a probability course. Here these two notations denote the same standard deviation. Writing $m\pm u(m)$ does not claim that every possible value is locked inside this interval: it announces a typical width, with its unit.

**Example 2 (what is not a standard uncertainty).** Saying only "the balance displays to the hundredth of a gram" describes a display resolution. A measurement model or repetitions are still needed to attach a standard deviation to it. A manufacturer's maximum tolerance is, likewise, a bound and not automatically $u(m)$.

**Method 1 (reading a measurement datum).** Identify the central value and its unit, determine whether the margin is a standard deviation, a tolerance or a resolution, then convert it if the protocol justifies it. Compute $u(x)/|x|$ only for a relative comparison.

## 2. Combining measurements made separately

**Tool 2 — combining.** We are going to turn the margins on $m$ and $V$ into a margin on $\rho=m/V$.

<Rappel title="The direct bridge to the previous lesson">

The <Terme id="differentielle-totale">total differential</Terme> gives, at first order,

$$dz\approx\sum_i\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\,dx_i.$$

The <Terme id="derivee-partielle">partial derivatives</Terme> are therefore local sensitivity coefficients: they amplify or attenuate each small input variation.

</Rappel>

Two measurement errors are said to be **independent** when knowing one brings no information about the other. This is the natural model for a mass and a volume obtained with two separate instruments.

Problem: the contributions can be positive or negative at random; adding them with their sign could produce a misleading cancellation. So we need to combine their size without losing the effect of the sensitivities.

**Definition 2 (propagation).** The <Def id="propagation-des-incertitudes">**propagation of uncertainty**</Def> (computing the uncertainty of a derived quantity, where each measurement's contribution is weighted by the partial derivatives then added in quadrature) gives, for small independent errors,

$$u^2(z)\approx\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right)^2u^2(x_i).$$

**Example (chemistry) 3.** For $\rho=m/V$,

$$
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial m}=\frac1V,\qquad
\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial V}=-\frac{m}{V^2},
$$

so

$$u^2(\rho)\approx\frac{u^2(m)}{V^2}+\frac{m^2}{V^4}u^2(V).$$

The negative sign of the volume's effect disappears when squared: a spread upward or downward widens the doubt zone on $\rho$ in both cases.

**Remark.** Adding in quadrature means "square each contribution, add, then take the square root". We add neither the standard uncertainties themselves nor the signed percentages.

**Proposition 1 (relative form for a product of powers).** If

$$z=Cx^ay^b$$

and if $x$ and $y$ are independent, then

$$
\left(\frac{u(z)}{|z|}\right)^2\approx
a^2\left(\frac{u(x)}x\right)^2+
b^2\left(\frac{u(y)}y\right)^2.
$$

<Collapsible title="Proof — the product-of-powers formula">

The logarithmic differential is

$$\frac{dz}{z}=a\frac{dx}{x}+b\frac{dy}{y}.$$

The coefficients of $dx$ and $dy$ are $a/x$ and $b/y$. Definition 2 therefore gives

$$
\frac{u^2(z)}{z^2}\approx
\frac{a^2}{x^2}u^2(x)+\frac{b^2}{y^2}u^2(y),
$$

which is the announced formula. The signs of the exponents disappear, but their absolute value matters: a squared radius produces a factor $4=(-2)^2$ in the relative variance. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Example (chemistry) 4.** For the mission, $\rho=mV^{-1}$; Proposition 1 directly gives

$$
\left(\frac{u(\rho)}\rho\right)^2\approx
\left(\frac{u(m)}m\right)^2+
\left(\frac{u(V)}V\right)^2.
$$

The volume's contribution is $(0{,}004)^2$, versus $(0{,}0008)^2$ for the mass. Improving the volume therefore promises the main gain.

Problem: if the number placed after $\pm$ is only a maximum bound, the random model of Definition 2 is not available. So we need a guarantee that covers the most unfavourable scenario.

**Proposition 2 (cautious bound).** If only bounds $\Delta x_i$ are known, then the <Terme id="linearisation">linearisation</Terme> and the triangle inequality give

$$
|\Delta z|\lesssim
\sum_i\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right|\Delta x_i.
$$

This linear sum assumes that all the contributions push simultaneously in the most unfavourable direction. It answers a different question from Definition 2 and generally gives a larger margin.

**Example 5.** With the mission's numbers treated as bounds,

$$
|\Delta\rho|\lesssim
\left|\frac1V\right|\Delta m+
\left|\frac{m}{V^2}\right|\Delta V
=0{,}002+0{,}010=0{,}012\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}.
$$

The sum in quadrature will give about $0{,}0102\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. The two values do not contradict each other: they rest on two different interpretations of the input margins.

**Method 2 (choosing then applying the right model).** Write the formula and its sensitivities at the measured point; decide whether the data are bounds or independent standard deviations; apply Proposition 2 or Definition 2 respectively; finally come back to an absolute margin with its unit.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Maximum bounds: $|\Delta z|\lesssim\sum_i|f_{x_i}|\Delta x_i$.
- Independent standard deviations: $u^2(z)\approx\sum_i f_{x_i}^2u^2(x_i)$.
- Product of powers: the relative contributions are multiplied by the square of the exponents.

</KeyResults>

## 3. Correcting when two errors move together

**Tool 3 — correcting.** A slope and an intercept obtained from the same calibration line generally do not fluctuate separately.

Problem: knowing each separate width does not say whether two errors tend to rise together or in opposite directions. So we need to measure this linkage.

**Definition 3 (covariance).** The <Def id="covariance">**covariance**</Def> (the measure of the tendency of two errors to vary together, zero for independent measurements) of two random quantities $X$ and $Y$ is

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)=
\mathbb E\!\left[(X-\mathbb E[X])(Y-\mathbb E[Y])\right].
$$

It is positive if the deviations tend to have the same sign and negative if they tend to have opposite signs.

**Example (chemistry) 6.** A calibration line gives $c=(A-b)/a$, where $a$ is its slope and $b$ its intercept. Since $a$ and $b$ come from the same experimental points, $\operatorname{Cov}(a,b)$ can be nonzero. On the other hand, an absorbance $A$ measured afterwards on another sample can reasonably be treated as independent of the line's two parameters.

**Remark (chemical notation).** A matrix supplied by regression software is not a side detail: its diagonal terms are the variances $u^2(a)$ and $u^2(b)$, and its off-diagonal terms contain $\operatorname{Cov}(a,b)$. The units of each row and each column must be kept.

**Proposition 3 (general form with linkages).** For $\mathbf x=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$, write $\Sigma$ for the table of all the variances and covariances, and $\mathbf g$ for the <Terme id="gradient">gradient</Terme> of the sensitivities of $f$. Then

$$u^2(z)\approx\mathbf g^\top\Sigma\mathbf g.$$

For two inputs,

$$
u^2(z)\approx f_x^2u^2(x)+f_y^2u^2(y)
+2f_xf_y\operatorname{Cov}(x,y).
$$

The cross term can increase or decrease the margin depending on the signs of the sensitivities and of the linkage.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — where does the matrix form come from?">

The linearisation gives $\Delta z\approx\mathbf g^\top\Delta\mathbf x$. After centring,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(z)
\approx\mathbb E\!\left[
(\mathbf g^\top\Delta\mathbf x)
(\Delta\mathbf x^\top\mathbf g)\right]
=\mathbf g^\top
\mathbb E[\Delta\mathbf x\Delta\mathbf x^\top]\mathbf g.
$$

Now $\mathbb E[\Delta\mathbf x\Delta\mathbf x^\top]=\Sigma$, hence Proposition 3. If $\Sigma$ is diagonal, all the covariances are zero and we immediately recover Definition 2. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Conditions and limits — when first order is no longer enough">

The formulas of this lesson rest on the linearisation of L04. They are reliable when the margins are small, when the function locally satisfies <Terme id="differentiabilite">differentiability</Terme> and when the measured point stays far from a singularity.

Near a vanishing denominator, a threshold or strong curvature, the higher-order terms can become important. One can then propagate a full distribution by Monte Carlo simulation or carry out a nonlinear analysis. This change of method is not a failure: it only signals that the flat model is no longer precise enough at the scale of the margins.

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — preparing the uncertainty on a cylinder

**Example 7.** A cylinder has a mass $m=(12{,}500\pm0{,}005)\ \mathrm g$, a radius $R=(0{,}800\pm0{,}003)\ \mathrm{cm}$ and a height $h=(2{,}000\pm0{,}005)\ \mathrm{cm}$. The three measurements are independent. Let us compute $\rho=m/(\pi R^2h)$ and spot the instrument to improve; this computation directly prepares TD 3.4.

**Step 1 — translate the geometry into a formula.** The volume is $\pi R^2h$, so

$$\rho=\frac{m}{\pi R^2h}.$$

*Why?* Method 2 starts by identifying the function that receives the measurements; without it, no sensitivity can be attributed.

**Step 2 — compute the central value.**

$$
\rho=\frac{12{,}500}{\pi(0{,}800)^2(2{,}000)}
\approx3{,}108\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}.
$$

*Why?* The final margin must bear on a numerical value and keep the unit produced by the formula.

**Step 3 — recognise a product of powers.**

$$\rho=\frac1\pi\,m\,R^{-2}h^{-1}.$$

*Why?* This form makes Proposition 1 applicable and immediately reveals the factor $4$ associated with the radius.

**Step 4 — combine the relative contributions.**

$$
\left(\frac{u(\rho)}\rho\right)^2=
\left(\frac{0{,}005}{12{,}500}\right)^2+
4\left(\frac{0{,}003}{0{,}800}\right)^2+
\left(\frac{0{,}005}{2{,}000}\right)^2
\approx6{,}27\times10^{-5}.
$$

*Why?* Definition 2 is the model suited to the three independent measurements announced in the statement.

**Step 5 — come back to an absolute margin.**

$$
\frac{u(\rho)}\rho\approx7{,}9\times10^{-3},\qquad
u(\rho)\approx3{,}108\times7{,}9\times10^{-3}
\approx0{,}025\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}.
$$

*Why?* A laboratory result must report $\rho$ and $u(\rho)$ in the same unit: $\rho=(3{,}108\pm0{,}025)\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.

**Step 6 — choose the useful improvement.** The radius term is about $5{,}63\times10^{-5}$ out of a total of $6{,}27\times10^{-5}$, that is, nearly $90\%$ of the variance.

*Why?* Comparing the squares of the contributions answers the second experimental question: measuring the radius more precisely will bring the main gain.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

Why does the radius count twice in the relative uncertainty, then four times in the relative variance?

The radius enters with the exponent $-2$. The logarithmic differential therefore multiplies its relative deviation by $-2$; Definition 2 then squares this contribution, hence $(-2)^2=4$ in the variance.

</Collapsible>

**Example 8 — your turn, with less help.** The Beer–Lambert law gives

$$c=\frac{A}{\varepsilon\ell},$$

with $A=0{,}600\pm0{,}006$, $\varepsilon=120\pm2\ \mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,cm^{-1}}$ and $\ell=1{,}000\pm0{,}005\ \mathrm{cm}$. The three measurements are independent.

First compute $c$. **Your turn for the last two moves:** use the exponents $1,-1,-1$ to obtain $u(c)/c$, then decide which measurement dominates.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

$$
c=\frac{0{,}600}{120\times1{,}000}
=5{,}00\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}},
$$

and

$$
\left(\frac{u(c)}c\right)^2=
\left(\frac{0{,}006}{0{,}600}\right)^2+
\left(\frac2{120}\right)^2+
\left(\frac{0{,}005}{1{,}000}\right)^2.
$$

Thus $u(c)/c\approx2{,}01\%$ and $u(c)\approx0{,}10\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. We report $c=(5{,}00\pm0{,}10)\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. The contribution of $\varepsilon$ dominates; improving only the cell would have little effect.

</Collapsible>

## Guided visualisation

In the plane of small errors $(\delta m,\delta V)$, the most plausible pairs occupy an ellipse-shaped zone around $(0,0)$. The local formula $\delta\rho\approx\delta m/V-(m/V^2)\delta V$ projects this zone onto an interval of values of $\rho$. Bring this picture to life with the TP numbers.

**Cycle 1 — seeing the most unfavourable scenario.**

- **Predict.** To push $\rho=m/V$ up as much as possible, should you take $m$ and $V$ both above their central value?
- **Act.** Mentally evaluate the sign of $\delta\rho$ for $\delta m=+0{,}01\ \mathrm g$ and $\delta V=-0{,}02\ \mathrm{cm^3}$.
- **Observe.** Why do both contributions become positive despite the opposite signs of the two deviations?
- **Connect.** Proposition 2 chooses precisely this combination of signs and adds $0{,}002$ then $0{,}010\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.

**Cycle 2 — seeing which measurement really widens the result.**

- **Predict.** If the errors change sign at random, which of the two measurements will occupy the larger share of the final width?
- **Act.** Compare the two squares $(0{,}0008)^2$ and $(0{,}004)^2$ on one same scale.
- **Observe.** Which square is twenty-five times larger, and what fraction of the total does it represent?
- **Connect.** Definition 2 turns the ellipse of the inputs into a width $u(\rho)$; here the volume supplies about $96\%$ of the relative variance.

Then explore other ratios $m/V$ freely: double one margin at a time and predict which one changes $u(\rho)$ most before recomputing.

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Adding independent standard uncertainties.** It is the contributions to the variance that add. The linear sum corresponds to the cautious bound of Proposition 2, not to the model of Definition 2.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Mixing absolute and relative.** For a product of powers, Proposition 1 naturally works with $u(x)/|x|$. After the square root, multiply by $|z|$ to come back to the output's unit.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting to square the exponent.** In $\rho=m/(\pi R^2h)$, the radius carries the exponent $-2$: its contribution to the relative variance is therefore multiplied by $4$, not by $-2$ nor by $2$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Throwing away the off-diagonal terms of a calibration.** The slope and the intercept come from the same data and are generally linked. Ignoring their covariance can overestimate or underestimate the final margin.

</Pitfall>

**1. Quantify.** By Definition 1, $u(m)/m=0{,}01/12{,}50=0{,}0008$ and $u(V)/V=0{,}02/5{,}00=0{,}004$.

**2. Combine.** Since the instruments are separate, Definition 2 and Proposition 1 give

$$
\frac{u(\rho)}\rho\approx
\sqrt{(0{,}0008)^2+(0{,}004)^2}
\approx0{,}00408.
$$

Since $\rho=12{,}50/5{,}00=2{,}500\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$,

$$
u(\rho)\approx2{,}500\times0{,}00408
\approx0{,}010\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}.
$$

**3. Decide.** We therefore report $\rho=(2{,}500\pm0{,}010)\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. The volume accounts for $(0{,}004)^2/[(0{,}0008)^2+(0{,}004)^2]\approx96\%$ of the relative variance: its measurement is the one to improve first. If $m$ and $V$ came from one same procedure and moved together, Definition 3 would force us to add the cross term of Proposition 3. The contract is fulfilled, numbers in hand.

## Lesson summary

You now have the three capabilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Quantify:** read $u(x)$ as the standard deviation attached to a measurement and keep its unit.
- **Combine:** use $u^2(z)\approx\sum_i f_{x_i}^2u^2(x_i)$ for independent sources.
- **Guarantee:** use $|\Delta z|\lesssim\sum_i|f_{x_i}|\Delta x_i$ when the data are only bounds.
- **Simplify:** for $z=C\prod_i x_i^{a_i}$, combine the relative margins with the factors $a_i^2$.
- **Correct:** keep the linkages with $u^2(z)\approx\mathbf g^\top\Sigma\mathbf g$.
- **Decide in chemistry:** compare the contributions to the variance before choosing the instrument to improve.
- **Check the model:** stay at first order only for small margins far from singularities.

</KeyResults>
