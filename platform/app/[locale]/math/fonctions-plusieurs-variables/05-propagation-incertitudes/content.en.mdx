## Intuition — a small box around the measured point

Near a point $\mathbf x$, a differentiable function is almost linear:

$$
f(\mathbf x+\Delta\mathbf x)-f(\mathbf x)
\approx \nabla f(\mathbf x)\cdot\Delta\mathbf x.
$$

Each partial derivative is a local amplification factor. A large derivative means that a small change in the input produces a large change in the output.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Conservative bound: $|\Delta z|\lesssim\sum_i|f_{x_i}|\Delta x_i$.
- Independent sources: $\sigma_z^2\approx\sum_i f_{x_i}^2\sigma_i^2$.
- Correlated sources: $\sigma_z^2\approx\nabla f^\top\Sigma\nabla f$.

</KeyResults>

<Collapsible title="The rigorous version — definitions and conditions">

<Rappel title="The differential as a local approximation">

Near a measurement $\mathbf x$, the previous lesson gives $\Delta f\approx\nabla f(\mathbf x)\cdot\Delta\mathbf x$. Each partial derivative is therefore a local sensitivity coefficient.

</Rappel>

## Three models to distinguish

### Conservative bound

If only $\Delta x_i$ bounds are known, the triangular inequality gives

$$
|\Delta z|\lesssim
\sum_i\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right|\Delta x_i.
$$

It assumes the unfavorable scenario where all contributions push in the same direction.

### Independent uncertainties

For independent and small standard uncertainties,

$$
\sigma_z^2\approx
\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right)^2\sigma_i^2.
$$

We add **variances**, not standard deviations. The signs of sensitivities disappear because they are squared away.

### Correlated variables

With the sensitivities vector $\mathbf g=\nabla f$ and the covariance matrix $\Sigma$,

$$
\boxed{\ \sigma_z^2\approx\mathbf g^\top\Sigma\mathbf g\ }.
$$

For two variables, the additional term is $2f_xf_y\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)$. Depending on the signs, a correlation can increase or reduce uncertainty.

<KeyResults title="Product of powers">

For $z=Cx^ay^b$, the logarithmic differential is

$$
\frac{dz}{z}=a\frac{dx}{x}+b\frac{dy}{y}.
$$

If the sources are independent,

$$
\left(\frac{\sigma_z}{z}\right)^2
\approx a^2\left(\frac{\sigma_x}{x}\right)^2
+b^2\left(\frac{\sigma_y}{y}\right)^2.
$$

</KeyResults>

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — Beer–Lambert law

We deduce a concentration of

$$
c=\frac{A}{\varepsilon\ell},
$$

with $A=0{,}600\pm0{,}006$, $\varepsilon=120\pm2\ \mathrm{L\,mol^{-1}\,cm^{-1}}$ and $\ell=1{,}000\pm0{,}005\ \mathrm{cm}$, the three sources being independent.

The central value is

$$
c=\frac{0{,}600}{120\times1{,}000}
=5{,}00\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}.
$$

Like $c\propto A\varepsilon^{-1}\ell^{-1}$,

$$
\left(\frac{\sigma_c}{c}\right)^2
=\left(\frac{0{,}006}{0{,}600}\right)^2
+\left(\frac2{120}\right)^2
+\left(\frac{0{,}005}{1{,}000}\right)^2.
$$

We obtain $\sigma_c/c\approx1{,}99\%$, therefore $\sigma_c\approx0{,}10\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. The contribution of $\varepsilon$ dominates: improving only the tank would have little effect.

## Operational visualization

Imagine the uncertainty ellipsoid of the inputs. gradient is the direction in which $f$ varies the fastest; its projection onto this ellipsoid gives the probable range of the output values. A covariance tilts the ellipsoid: this is why ignoring correlations can be as wrong as forgetting a variable.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — where does the matrix form come from?">

linearization gives $\Delta z\approx\mathbf g^\top\Delta\mathbf x$. After centering, the variance is

$$
\operatorname{Var}(z)
\approx\mathbb E\!\left[
(\mathbf g^\top\Delta\mathbf x)
(\Delta\mathbf x^\top\mathbf g)\right]
=\mathbf g^\top
\mathbb E[\Delta\mathbf x\Delta\mathbf x^\top]\mathbf g.
$$

Or $\mathbb E[\Delta\mathbf x\Delta\mathbf x^\top]=\Sigma$, hence $\sigma_z^2\approx\mathbf g^\top\Sigma\mathbf g$. If $\Sigma$ is diagonal, we immediately find the sum in quadrature.

</Collapsible>

## Model limitations

The propagation presented here is of the first-order. It works when the uncertainties are small, the function fairly regular and the domain far from a singularity. Near a zero denominator, a threshold or with strong nonlinearities, a Monte Carlo simulation or a more complete analysis becomes necessary.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Add the independent uncertainties.** It is their variances that are added. The linear sum corresponds to a conservative bound, not to the independent probabilistic model.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Mix absolute and relative.** For a power product, work in relative uncertainties; for a sum, the linearization naturally concerns absolute uncertainties.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the covariance of a calibration.** The slope and intercept from the same regression are generally correlated.

</Pitfall>

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- The differential transforms small input variations into output variations.
- Conservative bound: sum of the absolute values of the contributions.
- Independent sources: quadrature sum.
- Correlated sources: $\sigma_z^2=\nabla f^\top\Sigma\nabla f$.
- Always identify the dominant contribution and check the validity of the linear approximation.

</KeyResults>
