<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td4-01">

<Statement>Classify all the <Terme id="point-critique">critical points</Terme> of $f(x,y)=x^3-3x+y^2$.</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>Solve $f_x=0$ and $f_y=0$ simultaneously.</Hint>
<Hint>Use $D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2$ at each point.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>$(1,0)$ is a <Terme id="extremum-local">local minimum</Terme> and $(-1,0)$ is a <Terme id="point-selle">saddle point</Terme>.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

$\nabla f=(3x^2-3,2y)$, so the critical points are $(\pm1,0)$.
The <Terme id="hessienne">Hessian</Terme> is $\operatorname{diag}(6x,2)$ and $D=12x$. At $(1,0)$,
$D>0$ and $f_{xx}>0$: minimum. At $(-1,0)$, $D<0$: saddle.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Classifying before imposing both <Terme id="gradient">gradient</Terme> equations.
- Concluding “maximum” when $f_{xx}<0$ without looking at $D$.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td4-02">

<Statement>Classify the origin for $x^4+y^4$, $x^4-y^4$, and $(y-x^2)^2$.</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>In all three cases, the <Terme id="hessienne">Hessian</Terme> at the origin is degenerate.</Hint>
<Hint>Use the sign of the function and its zero sets directly.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>Strict global minimum; <Terme id="point-selle">saddle</Terme>; non-strict global minimum.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

$x^4+y^4\ge0$ and vanishes only at the origin: strict minimum.
$x^4-y^4$ is positive on the $x$-axis and negative on the $y$-axis: saddle.
Finally, $(y-x^2)^2\ge0$, but vanishes along the entire parabola $y=x^2$:
non-strict global minimum. The Hessian test does not replace this analysis
when $D=0$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Interpreting $D=0$ as “saddle.”
- Saying “strict” without examining other nearby zeros.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td4-03">

<Statement>

For $V=\frac12(ax^2+2bxy+cy^2)$, give the necessary and
sufficient conditions for a strict minimum at $(0,0)$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>The <Terme id="hessienne">Hessian</Terme> is the symmetric matrix $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{pmatrix}$.</Hint>
<Hint>Apply Sylvester's criterion in dimension two.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>$a>0$ and $ac-b^2>0$.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

The origin is <Terme id="point-critique">critical</Terme> and $V=\frac12\mathbf x^\top H\mathbf x$. It is a
strict minimum exactly when $H$ is positive definite. For a
symmetric $2\times2$ matrix, Sylvester's criterion gives
$a>0$ followed by $\det H=ac-b^2>0$. This is equivalent to two positive <Terme id="valeur-propre">eigenvalues</Terme>.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Requiring only $a>0$ and $c>0$; the coupling $b$ can create a negative direction.
- Forgetting that the factor $1/2$ does not change the classification.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td4-04">

<Statement>Minimize $x^2+4y^2$ subject to the constraint $x+y=3$.</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>Set $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$ with $g=x+y$.</Hint>
<Hint>The equations give $2x=\lambda$ and $8y=\lambda$.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>Minimum $36/5$, attained at $(12/5,3/5)$.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

The Lagrange system is
$2x=\lambda$, $8y=\lambda$, $x+y=3$. Thus $x=4y$, so $5y=3$:
$(x,y)=(12/5,3/5)$. The value is
$144/25+4(9/25)=180/25=36/5$. The restriction to the line is a
strictly convex quadratic; this point is therefore the unique global minimum.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Forgetting the constraint after solving the <Terme id="gradient">gradient</Terme> equality.
- Looking for a maximum: the function tends to infinity along the line.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td4-05">

<Statement>

For $V=x^4-2x^2+y^2+\alpha xy$, find and classify all the
<Terme id="point-critique">critical points</Terme>, for $\alpha\in\mathbb R$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>$V_y=0$ gives $y=-\alpha x/2$.</Hint>
<Hint>After substitution, $V_x=0$ factors by $x$.</Hint>
<Hint>Evaluate the <Terme id="hessienne">Hessian</Terme> determinant separately at the origin and at the nonzero points.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

The origin is always a <Terme id="point-selle">saddle</Terme>. The two points

$$
\left(\pm\sqrt{1+\frac{\alpha^2}{8}},
\mp\frac{\alpha}{2}\sqrt{1+\frac{\alpha^2}{8}}\right)
$$

are strict minima.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

The critical equations give $y=-\alpha x/2$ and

$$
x\left(4x^2-4-\frac{\alpha^2}{2}\right)=0.
$$

The Hessian is
$H=\begin{pmatrix}12x^2-4&\alpha\\\alpha&2\end{pmatrix}$.
At the origin, $\det H=-8-\alpha^2<0$: saddle. At the two nonzero points,
$x^2=1+\alpha^2/8$, so $V_{xx}=8+3\alpha^2/2>0$ and
$\det H=16+2\alpha^2>0$: strict minima.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Losing a solution by immediately dividing by $x$.
- Assuming that the coupling makes the pair of minima disappear.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>
