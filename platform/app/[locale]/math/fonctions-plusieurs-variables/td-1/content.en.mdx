<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td1-01">

<Statement>

Let $\Pi_1:x+2y-z=3$ and $\Pi_2:2x-y+z=1$.
Find a parametrization of their intersection, then the point on this
line that is closest to the origin.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>A direction vector is the cross product of the two normal vectors.</Hint>
<Hint>Set $z=0$ to find a common point, then write $\mathbf r(t)$.</Hint>
<Hint>Minimize $\|\mathbf r(t)\|^2$; its derivative is an affine polynomial in $t$.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

$\mathbf r(t)=(1,1,0)+t(1,-3,-5)$ and the closest point is
$(37/35,29/35,-2/7)$.

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

The normal vectors are $\mathbf n_1=(1,2,-1)$ and $\mathbf n_2=(2,-1,1)$, so
$\mathbf u=\mathbf n_1\times\mathbf n_2=(1,-3,-5)$. With $z=0$, the system
gives $(x,y)=(1,1)$. Thus $\mathbf r(t)=(1,1,0)+t\mathbf u$.

The minimum of $\|\mathbf r(t)\|^2$ satisfies
$\mathbf r(t)\cdot\mathbf u=0$, that is,
$-2+35t=0$. Hence $t=2/35$ and

$$
\mathbf r(2/35)=\left(\frac{37}{35},\frac{29}{35},-\frac27\right).
$$

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Using a normal vector as the direction of the line.
- Finding a minimum without checking orthogonality to the direction vector.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td1-02">

<Statement>

Describe $x^2+y^2=z^2$, with $z\ge0$, in <Terme id="coordonnees-cylindriques">cylindrical</Terme> and then <Terme id="coordonnees-spheriques">spherical</Terme>
coordinates. Identify the surface and its half-angle.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>In cylindrical coordinates, $x^2+y^2=r^2$.</Hint>
<Hint>In spherical coordinates, $z=\rho\cos\theta$ and $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=\rho\sin\theta$.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>In cylindrical coordinates, $z=r$; in spherical coordinates, $\theta=\pi/4$. It is the upper cone with half-angle $\pi/4$.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

The condition becomes $r^2=z^2$. Since $r,z\ge0$, it is equivalent to $z=r$.
In spherical coordinates,
$\rho^2\sin^2\theta=\rho^2\cos^2\theta$. On the upper nappe,
$0\le\theta\le\pi/2$, so $\tan\theta=1$ and $\theta=\pi/4$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Keeping both signs $z=\pm r$ despite the condition $z\ge0$.
- Confusing the polar angle $\theta$ with the azimuth $\varphi$.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td1-03">

<Statement>

Let
$\ell_1:(1,0,1)+s(1,2,-1)$ and
$\ell_2:(0,1,2)+t(2,-1,1)$.
Show that they are skew and calculate the distance between them.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>Two nonparallel lines are skew if the scalar triple product of their displacement and direction vectors is nonzero.</Hint>
<Hint>Use $d=|\mathbf w\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)|/\|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\|$.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>The lines are skew and $d=9/\sqrt{35}$.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

With $\mathbf u=(1,2,-1)$, $\mathbf v=(2,-1,1)$, and the displacement
$\mathbf w=(-1,1,1)$, we obtain

$$
\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=(1,-3,-5),\qquad
\mathbf w\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)=-9.
$$

The nonzero scalar triple product rules out a common plane. The distance is therefore
$|-9|/\sqrt{1+9+25}=9/\sqrt{35}$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Calculating the distance between two arbitrary points on the lines.
- Forgetting the absolute value of the scalar triple product.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td1-04">

<Statement>

Recover $dV=\rho^2\sin\theta\,d\rho\,d\theta\,d\varphi$ from the
local lengths in <Terme id="coordonnees-spheriques">spherical coordinates</Terme>.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>Vary one coordinate at a time.</Hint>
<Hint>The three local edges have lengths $d\rho$, $\rho\,d\theta$, and $\rho\sin\theta\,d\varphi$.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>$dV=\rho^2\sin\theta\,d\rho\,d\theta\,d\varphi$.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

A radial variation gives $d\ell_\rho=d\rho$. Along a meridian, the arc length is
$d\ell_\theta=\rho\,d\theta$. Along the parallel of radius
$\rho\sin\theta$, it is
$d\ell_\varphi=\rho\sin\theta\,d\varphi$. The small curvilinear box is
asymptotically orthogonal; multiplying the three lengths gives the stated
Jacobian.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Forgetting that the parallel has radius $\rho\sin\theta$.
- Using $r$ both for the spherical distance and the cylindrical radius.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td1-05">

<Statement>

$A=(a,0,0)$, $B=(-a/2,\sqrt3a/2,0)$, and
$C=(-a/2,-\sqrt3a/2,0)$. Show that $ABC$ is equilateral. A point $D$,
on the side $z>0$, is at distance $L$ from all three vertices. Find $D$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>Compare $AB^2$, $BC^2$, and $CA^2$.</Hint>
<Hint>The locus of points equidistant from the three vertices is the normal to the plane through the center of the triangle.</Hint>
<Hint>Write $AD^2=a^2+z_D^2=L^2$.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>The triangle is equilateral with side length $\sqrt3a$, and $D=(0,0,\sqrt{L^2-a^2})$, with $L\ge a$.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

The three differences have squared norm $3a^2$, so the
triangle is equilateral. Its center is the origin because
$A+B+C=0$. Thus $D=(0,0,h)$ with $h>0$. Now
$AD^2=a^2+h^2=L^2$, hence $h=\sqrt{L^2-a^2}$. A real solution requires
$L\ge a$.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Taking $\sqrt3a$ as the distance from the center to a vertex.
- Forgetting the existence condition $L\ge a$ or the choice of the positive side.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>
