# Deciding whether a mixture prediction stays reliable at the composition corner

<LessonMeta id="math-fmv-c03" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

A chemical model is robust if nearly identical preparations give nearly identical predictions, even when two settings vary together.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="Robustness of a binary mixture near the corner x = 0 and τ = 0">

**Situation:** near the pure mixture A at 298 K, we write $x$ for the mole fraction of B and $\tau=(T-298\,\mathrm K)/(10\,\mathrm K)$; the model gives $\Delta H(x,\tau)=x^2\tau/(x^2+\tau^2)$ kJ·mol⁻¹ away from $(0,0)$.
**Question:** can we record $\Delta H(0,0)=0$ and guarantee $|\Delta H|<0{,}015$ kJ·mol⁻¹ whenever $0\le x\le0{,}01$ and $|\tau|\le0{,}01$?
**The obstacle:** in L1 (first year), you approached a number from the left or from the right; here $x$ and $\tau$ can change together in infinitely many ways.
**The contract:** this chapter gives you 3 tools (demanding one same arrival number, making that demand fail with two approaches, certifying it with a global bound). By the end, we settle the question, numbers in hand.

</Mission>

*Feeling confident? [Go straight to the TD (tutorial session)](../td-2/) — the theory will still be here when you get stuck.*

## The battle plan

To decide whether the model is reliable, we need three tools.

1. **Fix the contract:** make precise the idea "every approach gives the same number".
2. **Refute:** build two approaches that give different numbers.
3. **Prove:** trap the error under a bound that works in every direction.

## 1. Demanding the same number whatever the approach

**Tool 1 — fixing the contract.** We are going to make precise the idea "the closer the settings get, the more the prediction stabilises".

Problem: testing only the left and the right no longer makes sense when two inputs move.
So we need a word for an arrival value shared by every approach.

**Definition 1 (limit in two variables).** Let $f:D\subset\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$, and let
$(a,b)$ be a point that distinct points of $D$ can approach. The number $L$ is the
<Def id="limite">**limit in two variables**</Def> of $f$ at $(a,b)$ if, whatever the path
of arrival within $D$, the values of $f$ approach $L$.

Rigorously, this means: for every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $\delta>0$ such that, for every $(x,y)\in D$,

$$
0<\|(x,y)-(a,b)\|<\delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f(x,y)-L|<\varepsilon.
$$

**Example (chemistry) 1.** For the mixture, the axes already give

$$
\Delta H(x,0)=0 \qquad\text{et}\qquad \Delta H(0,\tau)=0.
$$

The number $0$ is therefore a candidate. This computation is not enough: Definition 1 demands
control over all nearby points, not just two axes.

**Remark (chemical notation).** Here $x=x_B$ is a mole fraction and
$\tau=(T-298\,\mathrm K)/(10\,\mathrm K)$ is a temperature deviation made dimensionless. In a lab report you
would often write $\Delta H(x_B,T)$; the change of notation does not change the question.
The physical region imposes $0\le x\le1$, but a proof over a whole disc around the origin
will be stronger than a proof over only the physical half of that disc.

Problem: an arrival value can exist without matching the value recorded at the point.
So we need a word to say there is neither jump nor hole.

**Definition 2 (continuity at a point).** The <Def id="continuite">**continuity**</Def> of $f$ at
$(a,b)$ means that $f(a,b)$ is defined and that

$$
\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f(x,y)=f(a,b).
$$

**Example 2.** The simple model $P(x,\tau)=5+2x-\tau$ is continuous at $(0,0)$:
as $(x,\tau)$ approaches $(0,0)$, $P(x,\tau)$ approaches $5=P(0,0)$.
On the other hand, replacing only $P(0,0)$ by $7$ would create an isolated hole.

**Remark (robustness of a model).** Definition 2 expresses "small input error,
small output error". Here we call a **singularity** a point where this stability fails,
through the absence of an arrival value or through divergence. In chemistry, that signal can come
from an idealisation, like a $1/r$ potential for a point charge, from a forbidden region,
like a negative concentration or absolute zero, or from a model that has become invalid.

## 2. Making the contract fail with two approaches

**Tool 2 — refuting.** To invalidate an arrival value, there is no need to inspect the whole neighbourhood: a single well-built disagreement suffices.

**Method 1 (hunting for a destructive path).**

1. test the axes, which quickly give a first candidate;
2. test the lines $y=mx$, where the parameter $m$ compares all slopes;
3. if the degrees are unbalanced, try a power like $y=x^p$;
4. balance the terms of the denominator to build a custom path;
5. check that each path really arrives at the point under study.

**Proposition 1 (two-path test).** If two paths arriving at $(a,b)$ give two
different arrival numbers, then the limit of $f$ at $(a,b)$ does not exist.

**Justification.** If the limit existed, Definition 1 would impose the same number $L$ along
each of the two paths. Two different results contradict that requirement. $\blacksquare$

**Example (chemistry) 3.** For the mixture model, take the family $\tau=mx$. For $x\ne0$,

$$
\Delta H(x,mx)=\frac{x^2(mx)}{x^2+m^2x^2}=\frac{m}{1+m^2}\,x\longrightarrow 0.
$$

All the lines of finite slope agree with the axes: they reinforce the conjecture
$L=0$, but do not prove it. Infinitely many concordant paths are still a tiny fraction of
all possible approaches.

**Remark.** There is an asymmetry to remember: two disagreeing paths refute; no number
of agreeing paths proves.

## 3. Certifying every approach with a single bound

**Tool 3 — proving.** Paths can only yield a positive conjecture; we need to control a whole disc around the point in one stroke.

<Rappel title="Distance and polar coordinates">

At the origin, the distance to the point under study is

$$
r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.
$$

Hence $|x|\le r$ and $|y|\le r$. The
<Terme id="coordonnees-polaires">polar coordinates</Terme> write
$x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$: $r$ measures closeness, $\theta$ the direction.

</Rappel>

**Method 2 (bounding over the whole disc).**

1. pass to the absolute value $|f-L|$;
2. make $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ appear;
3. replace the directional factors by numerical bounds;
4. obtain $|f-L|\le Cr^\alpha$, with $C$ independent of $\theta$ and $\alpha>0$.

**Proposition 2 (uniform bound).** If there exist $C>0$, $\alpha>0$ and $r_0>0$ such that

$$
|f(x,y)-L|\le C r^\alpha
$$

for every point of $D$ satisfying $0<r<r_0$, then $f(x,y)$ tends to $L$ at the origin.

**Example (chemistry) 4.** For the mixture property,

$$
|\Delta H(x,\tau)|=\frac{x^2|\tau|}{x^2+\tau^2}\le|\tau|\le\sqrt{x^2+\tau^2}=r.
$$

Proposition 2 applies with $C=1$ and $\alpha=1$. It controls every way of varying
composition and temperature simultaneously, curved paths included.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — why the bound suffices">

Suppose $|f(x,y)-L|\le Cr^\alpha$ for $0<r<r_0$. Let $\varepsilon>0$ and set

$$
\delta=\min\!\left(r_0,\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{C}\right)^{1/\alpha}\right).
$$

If $0<\|(x,y)\|<\delta$, then

$$
|f(x,y)-L|\le Cr^\alpha<C\delta^\alpha\le\varepsilon.
$$

This is exactly Definition 1, for every point of the disc and hence for every path.
$\blacksquare$

The crucial point is that $C$ does not depend on $\theta$. An expression like
$r|\tan\theta|$ is not a uniform bound: the angular factor can become arbitrarily
large.

</Collapsible>

**Remark.** The most useful building blocks are $|x|\le r$, $|y|\le r$,
$|\cos\theta|\le1$ and $|\sin\theta|\le1$. It is not mandatory to replace the variables
by $r\cos\theta$ and $r\sin\theta$ if these inequalities directly give the power of $r$.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Rigorous definition: $0<\|(x,y)-(a,b)\|<\delta$ implies $|f(x,y)-L|<\varepsilon$.
- Continuity at $(a,b)$: $\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f(x,y)=f(a,b)$.
- Refuting: two paths giving two different numbers suffice.
- Proving: $|f-L|\le Cr^\alpha$ with $C>0$, $\alpha>0$ and $C$ independent of the direction.

</KeyResults>

## Worked example — choosing between refuting and proving

**Example 5 — refuting an arrival value.** Let us study
$f(x,y)=xy/(x^2+y^2)$ at $(0,0)$.

**Step 1 — choose the decision criterion.** We look for two paths that give different
results.

*Why?* Proposition 1 then lets us conclude without having to describe every approach.

**Step 2 — get a first number with the axis.** On $y=0$,
$f(x,0)=0$ for $x\ne0$, so the arrival number along this axis is $0$.

*Why?* A first path fixes the value against which the next computation must be compared.

**Step 3 — vary the slope.** On $y=mx$,

$$
f(x,mx)=\frac{mx^2}{x^2+m^2x^2}=\frac{m}{1+m^2}.
$$

For $m=1$, the value is constantly $1/2$.

*Why?* The family of lines searches, in a single computation, for a direction disagreeing with the axis.

**Step 4 — conclude about the patching.** The paths $y=0$ and $y=x$ give respectively
$0$ and $1/2$: the limit does not exist, and no value chosen for $f(0,0)$ would make
the function continuous.

*Why?* Proposition 1 settles existence; Definition 2 immediately turns that verdict
into the impossibility of filling the hole.

**Example 6 — proving an arrival value.** Let us study
$p(x,y)=x^2y/(x^2+y^2)$ at $(0,0)$.

**Step 1 — form a conjecture.** On $y=mx$,

$$
p(x,mx)=\frac{mx}{1+m^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$

*Why?* Simple paths suggest the number $L=0$ which must then be certified.

**Step 2 — produce a bound independent of the direction.** With
$x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$,

$$
|p(x,y)|=r\cos^2\theta\,|\sin\theta|\le r.
$$

One can also write directly

$$
|p(x,y)|=\frac{x^2|y|}{x^2+y^2}\le|y|\le r.
$$

*Why?* Proposition 2 turns the conjecture coming from the lines into a proof valid over
the whole disc.

**Step 3 — choose the value at the point.** Set $p(0,0)=0$. Then the computed limit equals the
recorded value: $p$ is continuous at the origin.

*Why?* Definition 2 gives exactly the no-hole patching test. Exercise M2.4
will show that this continuity does not entail
<Terme id="differentiabilite">differentiability</Terme>, a stronger requirement studied next.

**Example 7 — your turn, with less help.** Let
$g(x,y)=xy^2/(x^2+y^4)$. All the lines $y=mx$ give

$$
g(x,mx)=\frac{m^2x}{1+m^4x^2}\longrightarrow0.
$$

**Your turn:** build a path that balances $x^2$ and $y^4$, then settle the question.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

Take $x=y^2$. Then

$$
g(y^2,y)=\frac{y^4}{2y^4}=\frac12.
$$

The lines give $0$, but this parabola gives $1/2$. By Proposition 1, the limit
does not exist. Infinitely many concordant lines still did not amount to a proof.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

**Why are paths decisive in Example 5, yet insufficient to conclude in
Example 6?**

In Example 5, two paths contradict each other: a single counterexample suffices to violate
Definition 1. In Example 6, the paths agree; infinitely many untested approaches
remain. Only the bound $|p|\le r$, valid over the whole disc, controls them all at once.

</Collapsible>

## Guided visualisation

Here, the visualisation is numerical: you track the mixture property while the point
$(x,\tau)$ tightens around the composition corner.

**Cycle 1 — comparing a few approach recipes.**

- **Predict.** Before computing, will the recipes $\tau=0$ and $\tau=x$ give the same arrival number for $\Delta H$?
- **Act.** On $\tau=x$, evaluate $\Delta H$ for $x=0{,}1$, $0{,}01$ then $0{,}001$: you get $0{,}05$, $0{,}005$ then $0{,}0005$ kJ·mol⁻¹. On $\tau=0$, record three zeros.
- **Observe.** When the distance is divided by ten, what happens to the gap between the two series?
- **Connect.** Both series support the candidate $0$ of Definition 1, but the Remark after Example 3 still forbids speaking of a proof.

**Cycle 2 — looking at every direction at a fixed distance.**

- **Predict.** At distance $r=0{,}01$ from the corner, can changing the direction push past $0{,}01$ kJ·mol⁻¹?
- **Act.** Write $x=r\cos\theta$, $\tau=r\sin\theta$, then compare $\theta=0$, $\pi/4$ and $\pi/2$. The absolute values are $0$, $r\sqrt2/4$ and $0$.
- **Observe.** Which simple envelope contains these values and those of the intermediate directions?
- **Connect.** The envelope $|\Delta H|\le r$ is the one of Proposition 2: the whole vertical landscape tightens toward $0$ as the circle contracts.

You can now pick other angles or other radii: check that the value changes
with the direction, but that its amplitude stays trapped under $r$.

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**"Many paths agree, so the limit exists."** No. Example 7 shows that
all the lines can give $0$ while a parabola gives $1/2$. Concordant paths
build a conjecture; a uniform bound builds a proof.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Keeping a factor that depends on the direction.** An expression like
$|f|\le r|\tan\theta|$ is not enough, because $|\tan\theta|$ can become arbitrarily large.
In Proposition 2, $C$ must be one fixed number for all directions.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Computing successively in $x$, then in $y$.** These two computations, called iterated
limits, explore L-shaped paths along the axes. For Example 5, they both equal
$0$, whereas the double limit does not exist.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Choosing a path that does not arrive at the point under study.** For a study at $(0,0)$, the line
$y=x+1$ will not do: it arrives at $(0,1)$. Always check the arrival point before
using Proposition 1.

</Pitfall>

<MissionSolved>

**1. Fix the expected number.** By Definition 1 and Example (chemistry) 3, the axes and
all the lines $\tau=mx$ give $0$: that is our candidate.

**2. Certify every approach.** Example (chemistry) 4 gives

$$
|\Delta H(x,\tau)|\le\sqrt{x^2+\tau^2}=r.
$$

Proposition 2 therefore forces $\Delta H(x,\tau)\to0$. Setting $\Delta H(0,0)=0$,
Definition 2 confirms that the model is patched with no hole.

**3. Check the laboratory tolerance.** If $0\le x\le0{,}01$ and
$|\tau|\le0{,}01$, then

$$
r\le\sqrt{0{,}01^2+0{,}01^2}=0{,}01\sqrt2\approx0{,}0142<0{,}015.
$$

Thus $|\Delta H|<0{,}015$ kJ·mol⁻¹. The contract is fulfilled: the patching at $0$ is valid and
the announced precision is guaranteed, numbers in hand.

</MissionSolved>

## Lesson summary

You now have the three capabilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Fix the contract:** demand one same number $L$ for all paths, following Definition 1; obtain continuity when $L=f(a,b)$.
- **Refute:** find two paths giving two different results, often by balancing the powers of the denominator.
- **Prove:** establish $|f-L|\le Cr^\alpha$ with $C>0$, $\alpha>0$ and $C$ independent of $\theta$.
- **Keep the asymmetry in mind:** two disagreeing paths suffice; even infinitely many agreeing paths do not.
- **Read a chemical model:** continuity expresses its robustness to small errors of
  composition and temperature; a singularity signals an idealisation, a forbidden domain
  or a failing model.

</KeyResults>
