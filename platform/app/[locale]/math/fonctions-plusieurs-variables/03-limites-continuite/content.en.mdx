## Intuition

To say that $f(x, y) \to L$ when $(x, y) \to (a, b)$ is to say that the value of $f$ becomes arbitrarily close to $L$ as soon as we are sufficiently close to $(a, b)$ — **whatever the way of approaching**. Think of a hiker who converges towards a point in the relief: if the altitude limit depends on the chosen path, there is no well-defined altitude at the arrival point.

Hence a fundamental asymmetry between the two tasks:

- to **refute** a limit, it is enough to exhibit *two* paths in disagreement;
- to **prove** a limit, you need an argument valid for *all* the paths at once — testing paths, even many, is never enough.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Continuity in $(a,b)$: $\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f(x,y)=f(a,b)$.
- Two paths giving two different values refute a limit.
- A uniform bound $|f-L|\le Cr^\alpha$, with $\alpha>0$, proves $f\to L$.

</KeyResults>

<Collapsible title="The rigorous version — definitions and conditions">

## Formal definition

$$
\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)} f(x,y) = L
$$

means: for every strictly positive $\varepsilon$, there exists strictly positive $\delta$ such that

$$
0 < \|(x,y) - (a,b)\| < \delta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
|f(x,y) - L| < \varepsilon .
$$

The condition concerns an entire *disk* around $(a,b)$: it is independent of the path. $f$ is **continuous** at $(a,b)$ if the limit exists and equals $f(a,b)$.

### The path test (rebuttal)

If two paths leading to $(a, b)$ give different limits, then the limit **does not exist**. Useful paths:

- the straight lines $y = mx$ (all the slopes at once, thanks to the parameter $m$);
- $y = x^p$ powers (when straight lines are not enough);
- the polar substitution $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ leaving $\theta$ free;
- a **custom path**, constructed to balance the denominator terms.

Getting the same value along many paths proves **nothing**.

### The polar bound (proof)

<Rappel title="Norm and polar coordinates">

By definition, $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, therefore $|x|\le r$ and $|y|\le r$. Writing $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ separates the distance $r$ from the direction $\theta$.

</Rappel>

Near the origin, we look for an inequality of the form

$$
|f(x,y) - L| \le C\,r^{\alpha},
\qquad
r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2},
\quad \alpha > 0,
$$

with constant $C$ **independent of $\theta$**. If we succeed, then $f \to L$: the upper bound tends towards $0$ uniformly in all directions. The basic building blocks: $|x| \le r$, $|y| \le r$, $|\cos\theta| \le 1$, $|\sin\theta| \le 1$.

</Collapsible>

## Worked example

### A. Refute: $f(x,y) = \dfrac{xy}{x^2 + y^2}$ at $(0,0)$

**Step 1 — path $y = 0$.** $f(x, 0) = 0$ for all $x \ne 0$: limit $0$ along the axis.

**Step 2 — path $y = mx$.**

$$
f(x, mx) = \frac{m x^2}{x^2 + m^2 x^2} = \frac{m}{1 + m^2},
$$

*constant* value along each line… but which **depends on $m$**: $0$ for $m = 0$, $\tfrac12$ for $m = 1$, $-\tfrac12$ for $m = -1$.

**Conclusion.** Two paths, two limits: the limit does not exist, and $f$ cannot be extended by continuity inat $(0,0)$ by any value.

### B. Prove: $f(x,y) = \dfrac{x^2 y}{x^2 + y^2} \to 0$ at $(0,0)$

**Step 1 — guess.** Along $y = mx$: $f = \dfrac{m x^3}{(1 + m^2) x^2} = \dfrac{m x}{1 + m^2} \to 0$. All straight-line paths suggest $0$ — it's a *clue*, not proof.

**Step 2 — polar bound.** With $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$:

$$
|f| = \frac{r^2\cos^2\theta \cdot r\,|\sin\theta|}{r^2}
= r\,\cos^2\theta\,|\sin\theta|
\le r .
$$

The upper bound $r$ is independent of $\theta$ and tends towards $0$: therefore $f \to 0$. (Variant without polars: $|x| \le r$ and $|y| \le r$ directly give $|f| \le r^3/r^2 = r$.)

**Step 3 — conclusion.** Here $C = 1$ and $\alpha = 1$: the function, extended by $f(0,0) = 0$, is continuous at the origin. (Exercise M2.4 of TD 2 will show that this same function, although continuous, is *not* differentiable — continuity and differentiability are two distinct requirements.)

### C. The custom path: $g(x,y) = \dfrac{x y^2}{x^2 + y^4}$

Along **all** lines $y = mx$:

$$
g(x, mx) = \frac{m^2 x^3}{x^2 + m^4 x^4} = \frac{m^2 x}{1 + m^4 x^2}
\longrightarrow 0 .
$$

We would be tempted to conclude... but the denominator $x^2 + y^4$ suggests balancing its two terms: let's take the path $x = y^2$. SO

$$
g(y^2, y) = \frac{y^2\cdot y^2}{y^4 + y^4} = \frac12 .
$$

Limit $\tfrac12$ along the parabola, $0$ along the lines: **no limit** — while an infinity of paths agreed.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — why the polar bound is enough">

Assume $|f(x,y) - L| \le C r^{\alpha}$ for any point with $0 < r < r_0$, where $C$ and $\alpha$ are strictly positive. Let us show that the definition of the limit is satisfied.

Let $\varepsilon$ be strictly positive. Let's ask

$$
\delta = \min\!\left( r_0,\ \left(\frac{\varepsilon}{C}\right)^{1/\alpha}\right).
$$

If $0 < \|(x,y)\| < \delta$, then $r < \delta$ and

$$
|f(x,y) - L| \le C r^{\alpha} < C\,\delta^{\alpha}
\le C\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{C} = \varepsilon .
$$

That's exactly the definition, and the argument covers *all* points on the disk — so all paths at once. $\blacksquare$

The crucial point: $C$ must not depend on $\theta$. An upper bound like $r\tan\theta$ proves nothing, because it explodes in certain directions.

</Collapsible>

## Link with chemistry

Continuity is the local mathematical expression of the **robustness** of a model: small variations in temperature, concentration or molecular geometry produce small variations in the predicted quantity. It is she who authorizes working with uncertain measurements.

Conversely, a **singularity** — a point where the limit does not exist or diverges — is always a signal to be interpreted: an idealization (the potential in $1/r$ of a *point* charge), a physically forbidden domain (negative concentration, absolute $T = 0$), or a model which ceases to be valid and must be replaced. Knowing how to locate and classify singularities is part of the critical reading of a model.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**“Many paths match, so the limit exists. »** No — never. Example C above shows this: *all* the lines give $0$, and yet the parabola $x = y^2$ gives $\tfrac12$. Only an upper bound valid throughout a disk (or a continuity theorem) proves existence.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**A polar bound which still depends on $\theta$.** Arriving at $|f| \le r\,|\tan\theta|$ proves nothing: the factor explodes when $\theta \to \pi/2$. The constant $C$ must be a real number, valid in all directions. If $\theta$ survives in the upper bound, it is often a sign that the limit does not exist — then look for a path that disproves the limit.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confuse iterated limits and double limits.** Calculating first $\lim_{x\to0}$ then $\lim_{y\to0}$ (or the reverse) only explores rectangular paths aligned with the axes. The two iterated limits can exist, be equal... and the double limit not exist (example A: the two iterated limits equal $0$, the double limit does not exist).

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting to check that the path leads to the right point.** A destructive path must *pass through* the point studied: $y = x + 1$ is of no use for a limit at $(0,0)$. Always check that the path tends to the point when its parameter tends to its limit value.

</Pitfall>

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- Limit to two variables = same value for **all** approach paths; continuity = limit equal to the value.
- Refute: two disagreeing paths are enough — lines $y = mx$, powers $y = x^p$, custom path modeled on the denominator.
- Many matching paths **never** prove existence.
- Prove: upper bound $|f - L| \le C\,r^{\alpha}$ with $C$ and $\alpha$ strictly positive, $C$ independent of $\theta$; bricks: $|x| \le r$, $|y| \le r$.
- Typical example: $\frac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2} \to 0$ (maximum $r$); typical counterexample: $\frac{xy^2}{x^2+y^4}$ (straight lines $0$, parabola $\tfrac12$).
- In chemistry: continuity = robustness of the model; singularity = idealization, forbidden domain or failed model.

</KeyResults>
