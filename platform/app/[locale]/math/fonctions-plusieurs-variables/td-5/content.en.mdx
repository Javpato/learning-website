<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td5-01">

<Statement>

In the first quadrant, study
$x'=x(1-x-y)$, $y'=y(2-x-2y)$: <Terme id="isocline">nullclines</Terme>, <Terme id="point-d-equilibre">equilibria</Terme>, and
linear classification.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>Each component is already factored.</Hint>
<Hint>The nullclines are $x=0$, $x+y=1$, $y=0$, and $x+2y=2$.</Hint>
<Hint>Calculate the <Terme id="jacobienne">Jacobian</Terme>; watch for the zero <Terme id="valeur-propre">eigenvalue</Terme> at $(0,1)$.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>

Equilibria: $(0,0)$ is a source, $(1,0)$ is a <Terme id="point-selle">saddle</Terme>, and $(0,1)$ is nonhyperbolic
(attracting from the interior of the first quadrant).

</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

The admissible intersections give $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, and $(0,1)$. The
Jacobian is

$$
J=\begin{pmatrix}1-2x-y&-x\\-y&2-x-4y\end{pmatrix}.
$$

At the origin, the eigenvalues $1,2$ give a source. At $(1,0)$,
they are $-1,1$: saddle. At $(0,1)$, they are $0,-2$: <Terme id="linearisation">linearization</Terme>
alone is inconclusive. The sign table nevertheless shows
that, for small $x>0$, the flow drifts toward $x=0,y=1$; attraction there is
slower in the direction associated with the zero eigenvalue.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Forgetting the nullclines carried by the axes.
- Classifying a zero eigenvalue as a center.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td5-02">

<Statement>

For $x'=y$, $y'=-x-x^3$, prove that
$H=\frac12y^2+\frac12x^2+\frac14x^4$ is conserved and describe the nonstationary <Terme id="trajectoire">trajectories</Terme>.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>Calculate $\dot H=H_xx'+H_yy'$.</Hint>
<Hint>$H$ is coercive and positive definite.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>$\dot H=0$; the positive energy <Terme id="ligne-de-niveau">levels</Terme> are closed curves, so the motions are periodic.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

$H_x=x+x^3$ and $H_y=y$, hence
$\dot H=(x+x^3)y+y(-x-x^3)=0$. The trajectories remain on $H=E$.
For $E>0$, these level sets are bounded, regular, and surround only
the origin; they are closed curves. The vector field does not vanish on them, so
each nonstationary trajectory travels around them periodically.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Concluding conservation because the system “looks like” an oscillator.
- Confusing a closed curve with convergence to the origin.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td5-03">

<Statement>

For $X'=-\nabla V$ with
$V=\frac14(x^2-1)^2+\frac12y^2$, find the <Terme id="point-d-equilibre">equilibria</Terme>, classify them, and
prove that $V$ decreases.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>$\nabla V=(x(x^2-1),y)$.</Hint>
<Hint>Along a <Terme id="gradient">gradient</Terme> flow, $\dot V=\nabla V\cdot X'$.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>$(\pm1,0)$ are <Terme id="stabilite">stable</Terme> minima, $(0,0)$ is a <Terme id="point-selle">saddle</Terme>, and $\dot V=-\|\nabla V\|^2\le0$.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

The equilibria satisfy $y=0$ and $x(x^2-1)=0$. The <Terme id="hessienne">Hessian</Terme> is
$\operatorname{diag}(3x^2-1,1)$: positive at $x=\pm1$, indefinite at
$x=0$. Finally,

$$
\dot V=\nabla V\cdot(-\nabla V)=-\|\nabla V\|^2\le0,
$$

with equality only at the equilibria. The flow crosses <Terme id="ligne-de-niveau">level sets</Terme> toward
lower values, unlike a <Terme id="hamiltonien">Hamiltonian</Terme> flow.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Forgetting the equilibrium $x=0$ by dividing by $x$.
- Writing $\dot V=0$ as in a conservative system.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td5-04">

<Statement>

For $x'=y$, $y'=-x^3$, show that <Terme id="linearisation">linearization</Terme> at the origin is
inconclusive and determine its <Terme id="stabilite">stability</Terme>.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>The <Terme id="jacobienne">Jacobian</Terme> at the origin is nilpotent, and both its <Terme id="valeur-propre">eigenvalues</Terme> are zero.</Hint>
<Hint>Look for an energy of the form kinetic plus potential.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>The origin is stable in the sense of Lyapunov, but not asymptotically stable.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

The Jacobian
$\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-3x^2&0\end{pmatrix}$ becomes
$\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ at the origin: two zero eigenvalues,
so there is no linear verdict. However,

$$
H=\frac12y^2+\frac14x^4,\qquad
\dot H=x^3y+y(-x^3)=0.
$$

$H$ is positive definite: its small sublevel sets confine the
<Terme id="trajectoire">trajectories</Terme> near the origin, proving stability. The energy of a
nonstationary trajectory remains strictly positive, so it cannot
converge to the origin: there is no asymptotic stability.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Inferring instability from zero eigenvalues.
- Using a conserved energy to claim dissipation.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>
