## Intuition

Consider the system

$$
\begin{cases}
x' = f(x, y),\\
y' = g(x, y).
\end{cases}
$$

At each point $(x,y)$ of the plane, the vector $F(x,y) = (f, g)$ tells in which direction and at what speed the state is moving: it is a **vector field**, a wind map. A **trajectory** is the path of a particle released into this wind: at any point, its speed is exactly the local arrow. The **phase portrait** is the overall drawing: the equilibria, a few well-chosen trajectories, and the arrows of the flow.

Where the wind falls — $f = g = 0$ — the state no longer moves: it is an **equilibrium** (steady state, in chemistry).

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- autonomous system: $X'=F(X)$; equilibrium: $F(X_*)=0$.
- nullclines: $f=0$ gives vertical arrows, $g=0$ horizontal arrows.
- linearization: $u'\approx J_F(X_*)u$; it directly classifies hyperbolic equilibria.

</KeyResults>

## Formal definition

### System, vector field, equilibria

An autonomous plane **differential system** is the data of two functions $f, g$ of class $C^1$ and of the equations $x' = f(x,y)$, $y' = g(x,y)$. A **solution** is a pair $t \mapsto (x(t), y(t))$ which verifies them; its image curve in the $(x,y)$ plane — the **phase plane** — is a trajectory.

A point $X_* = (x_*, y_*)$ is an **equilibrium** if $f(x_*, y_*) = g(x_*, y_*) = 0$: the constant solution $X(t) = X_*$ stays there forever.

The Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem guarantees (for $f, g$ of class $C^1$) existence and uniqueness of the solution passing through a given point: capital geometric consequence, **two trajectories can never cross** at an ordinary point.

### Nullclines

- The nullcline $f(x,y) = 0$ is the place where $x' = 0$: the field there is **vertical** (zero horizontal component);
- nullcline $g(x,y) = 0$ is the place where $y' = 0$: the field there is **horizontal**.

The **intersections of the two families of nullclines are the equilibria**. The nullclines divide the plan into regions where the signs of $f$ and $g$ are constant: in each region, a simple study of the sign gives the quadrant of the arrow (↗, ↖, ↙ or ↘) — this is often enough to sketch the whole portrait.

### Linearization near an equilibrium

<Rappel title="eigenvalues and stability">

For $u'=Ju$, an own direction checking $Jv=\lambda v$ evolves like $e^{\lambda t}v$. A negative real part contracts this direction; a real positive part keeps it away.

</Rappel>

Near an equilibrium $X_*$, we set $u = X - X_*$ and expand the field to order 1:

$$
u' \approx J_F(X_*)\,u,
\qquad
J_F =
\begin{pmatrix}
f_x & f_y \\
g_x & g_y
\end{pmatrix},
$$

where $J_F$ is the **Jacobian matrix** of the field. The linear system $u' = J u$ (solved in detail in lesson 9) serves as a local magnifying glass: node, saddle, focus, center…

<KeyResults title="Validity of the linearization — the caveat">

If **no eigenvalue of $J_F(X_*)$ has a zero real part** (so-called *hyperbolic* equilibrium), then the local portrait of the nonlinear system resembles that of its linearization: same type, same stability.

If an eigenvalue has a zero real part (linear center, eigenvalue zero, etc.), the linearization **does not conclude**: the higher-order terms decide. Exercise 04 of TD 5 is built on this trap.

</KeyResults>

## Worked example

Let's analyze the system completely

$$
x' = x - y,
\qquad
y' = x^2 - 1 .
$$

**Step 1 — nullclines.** $x' = 0$ on the line $y = x$ (vertical field); $y' = 0$ on the two vertical lines $x = 1$ and $x = -1$ (horizontal field).

**Step 2 — equilibria.** Intersections: $x = \pm 1$ with $y = x$, i.e. $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, -1)$.

**Step 3 — Jacobian.**

$$
J(x,y) =
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & -1 \\
2x & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Step 4 — classification.**

- In $(1,1)$: $J = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, trace $\tau = 1$ and determinant $\Delta = 2$. Discriminant $\tau^2 - 4\Delta = 1 - 8 = -7 < 0$: eigenvalues real part complexes $\tau/2 = 1/2 > 0$ → **unstable focus** (diverging spiral).
- In $(-1,-1)$: $J = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, of real determinant $\Delta = -2 < 0$: eigenvalues of opposite signs → **saddle** (unstable).

Both equilibria are hyperbolic (no zero real part): the linear classification is valid for the nonlinear system.

**Step 5 — arrows.** In $(0,0)$: $x' = 0$ and $y' = -1$ — point is *on* the nullcline $y = x$, vertical arrow down. In $(2, 0)$: $x' = 2$ and $y' = 3$, arrow ↗. In $(0, 2)$: $x' = -2$ and $y' = -1$, arrow ↙. These few arrows, plus the two classified equilibria, are enough to sketch the portrait.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — where the linearization comes from, and why it may fail">

Let $X_*$ be an equilibrium and $u = X - X_*$ the deviation. By the Taylor formula applied to each component of the field $F = (f, g)$ in $X_*$:

$$
F(X_* + u) = \underbrace{F(X_*)}_{=\,0} + J_F(X_*)\,u + R(u),
\qquad
\|R(u)\| = o(\|u\|).
$$

Like $u' = X' = F(X_* + u)$, we obtain exactly

$$
u' = J_F(X_*)\,u + R(u).
$$

As long as $\|u\|$ is small, the remainder $R(u)$ is negligible compared to the linear term *provided that the latter is not itself degenerate*. This is the content of the Hartman–Grobman theorem: if no eigenvalue of $J_F(X_*)$ has a zero real part, the nonlinear flow is locally equivalent to the linear flow.

**Why the condition is necessary.** Let's compare $x' = -x^3$ and $x' = +x^3$ (in dimension 1). Both have the same linearization in $0$: $u' = 0$ (eigenvalue zero). However, the first converges towards $0$ (stable) and the second explodes (unstable): when the linear term vanishes, it is the remainder $R(u)$ — invisible for the Jacobian — which decides the fate of the trajectories. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Link with chemistry

A network of reactions is translated word for word into differential system: each species gives an equation, each reaction a speed term.

- **Reversible reaction** $A \rightleftharpoons B$: $a' = -k_1 a + k_{-1} b$, $b' = k_1 a - k_{-1} b$ — *linear* system, a stable equilibrium (chemical equilibrium).
- **Autocatalysis** $A + B \to 2B$: the term $k\,ab$ makes the system *non-linear* — several possible equilibria, ignition thresholds.
- **Competition of two species** for a resource: this is the system of exercise 01 of TD 5, where the analysis of nullclines predicts *which species survives*.
- **Enzymatic kinetics** (Michaelis–Menten): a nonlinear system whose study of nullclines justifies the approximation of the quasi-steady state.

In any case, the chemical question "does the mixture reach a steady state, and which one?" » becomes the mathematical question “which equilibria are stable?” » — Jacobian, eigenvalues, portrait.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Confusing nullcline with trajectory.** A nullcline is where *a component* of the velocity vanishes — it is almost never a curve followed by a solution. The trajectories *cross* the nullclines (vertically the nullcline of $x'$, horizontally that of $y'$).

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Drawing two trajectories that cross.** The uniqueness of Cauchy–Lipschitz prohibits this: exactly one trajectory passes through each point. What looks like a crossing on a drawing is always an equilibrium (which trajectories only reach in infinite time).

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Classify a non-hyperbolic equilibrium with the Jacobian alone.** If an eigenvalue has a zero real part ($\tau = 0$ with $\Delta > 0$, or $\Delta = 0$), the linearization does not conclude: $x' = y$, $y' = -x^3$ has a nilpotent Jacobian at the origin, and only an analysis using a first integral settles the question (TD 5, exercise 04).

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forget the physical domain.** In kinetics, only the quadrant $x \ge 0$, $y \ge 0$ has any meaning (concentrations!). The axes are invariant for systems of type $x' = x(\cdots)$: a trajectory starting from the positive quadrant remains there — check it before interpreting.

</Pitfall>

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- autonomous system plane: $x' = f(x,y)$, $y' = g(x,y)$ = vector field $F = (f,g)$; the trajectories follow the arrows and never cross.
- Equilibria: $f = g = 0$ — the stationary states of chemistry.
- nullclines: $f = 0$ (vertical field), $g = 0$ (horizontal field); their intersections are the equilibria, the sign study gives the arrows region by region.
- linearization: $u' \approx J_F(X_*)u$ with the Jacobian $J_F = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & f_y \\ g_x & g_y \end{pmatrix}$.
- Validity: reliable conclusions only if no eigenvalue has a zero real part (hyperbolic equilibrium).
- Chemistry: reaction networks → differential systems; stability of a steady state = sign of the real parts of eigenvalues.

</KeyResults>
