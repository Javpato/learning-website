# Predicting whether concentrations stabilise or oscillate

<LessonMeta id="math-fmv-c07" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

Two rate laws are enough to watch a mixture circle around a constant state instead of dying out.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="An oscillating autocatalytic reaction — following two concentrations that answer each other">

**Situation.** Two concentrations satisfy $x'=x(1-y)$, $y'=y(x-1)$, with
$x(0)=2$, $y(0)=0{,}5$ mmol·L⁻¹. **Precise question.** Which way does the mixture set off, where do the
rates vanish, and do the concentrations settle there? **Obstacle.** In L1 (first year), you solved
one equation; here each rate depends on both concentrations, with no simple explicit formula.
**Contract.** In this chapter: 3 tools (drawing the rates, spotting the stops, testing their neighbourhood). By the end, we solve this problem, with the numbers to back it up.

</Mission>

## The battle plan

To honour the contract, we need three tools:

1. **transform** two rate laws into arrows and paths in the plane of the concentrations;
2. **spot** the motionless states and the direction of motion without solving the equations;
3. **test** what happens after a small perturbation around a motionless state.

Each tool corresponds to a section below and comes back to the mixture $x'=x(1-y)$, $y'=y(x-1)$.

## 1. Turning rate laws into paths

**Tool 1 — transform.** Let's start by gathering the two evolutions into one same object, then by drawing what they impose.

Problem: the rate of $x$ depends on $y$, and that of $y$ depends on $x$; two separate equations hide this coupling. So we need a name for reading them together.

**Definition 1 (system of ODEs).** A <Def id="systeme-differentiel">**system of ODEs**</Def>
is a set of coupled equations: each rate may depend on all the variables. In the planar autonomous case,

$$
\begin{cases}x'=f(x,y),\\y'=g(x,y),\end{cases}
\qquad\text{soit}\qquad X'=F(X),\quad F(x,y)=(f(x,y),g(x,y)).
$$

The function $F$ is the **vector field**: at the point $(x,y)$, the arrow $F(x,y)$ gives the instantaneous direction and speed.

**Example (chemistry) 1.** The scaled network

$$
A+X\longrightarrow2X,\qquad X+Y\longrightarrow2Y,\qquad Y\longrightarrow B
$$

produces $X$ at rate $x$, consumes $X$ and produces $Y$ at rate $xy$, then consumes $Y$ at rate $y$. Its balance is precisely

$$
x'=x-xy=x(1-y),\qquad y'=xy-y=y(x-1).
$$

**Remark (chemical notation).** The lowercase $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ denote the concentrations of the species $X$ and $Y$. The symbol $x'$ means $\mathrm dx/\mathrm dt$: a concentration rate, not a new species.

The same dictionary covers $A\rightleftharpoons B$, autocatalysis in $k\,ab$, competition and the Michaelis–Menten steady-state approximation.

Problem: an arrow only tells the present instant; we must follow the same mixture over time. So we need a word for the path it travels.

**Definition 2 (trajectory).** A solution $t\mapsto(x(t),y(t))$ satisfies Definition 1.
Its curve is a <Def id="trajectoire">**trajectory**</Def> (the path followed by the state over time); the plane of the variables is the phase plane.

**Example (chemistry) 2.** At the start,
$F(2,0{,}5)=\bigl(2(1-0{,}5),0{,}5(2-1)\bigr)=(1,0{,}5)$: the trajectory sets off upward and to the right.

**Proposition 1 (tangency).** At every point of a trajectory, the arrow $F(x,y)$ is tangent to the path and oriented in the direction of time.

Problem: a single solution does not show what other initial concentrations would produce. So we need an overall map.

**Definition 3 (phase portrait).** The <Def id="portrait-de-phase">**phase portrait**</Def> is the map of all possible trajectories, oriented by a few arrows.

**Example (chemistry) 3.** In $x\ge0$, $y\ge0$, it shows the path issued from $(2,0{,}5)$ and those of neighbouring mixtures.

**Proposition 2 (non-crossing).** If $f$ and $g$ are of class $C^1$, two distinct trajectories do not cross.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — uniqueness forbids crossings">

The Cauchy–Lipschitz theorem guarantees a unique solution for each initial condition. A crossing
at $P$ would give two solutions issued from $P$, a contradiction. They may tend to the same point as $t$ tends to infinity, without passing through it. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## 2. Spotting the stops and the direction of motion

**Tool 2 — spot.** We are going to find the states that no longer change, then cut the plane into regions where the direction of each rate is constant.

Problem: to know whether a mixture can stay unchanged, we must impose the simultaneous vanishing of all its rates. So we need a name for this state.

**Definition 4 (equilibrium point).** An <Def id="point-d-equilibre">**equilibrium point**</Def>
is a state $X_*$ where $F(X_*)=0$; the solution $X(t)=X_*$ stays there forever.

**Example (chemistry) 4.** In the physical quadrant, $x(1-y)=y(x-1)=0$ gives $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$; the second has two positive concentrations.

**Remark (chemistry).** A **steady state** keeps constant concentrations despite the reactions; a thermodynamic equilibrium demands more.

Problem: we want to spot where a single concentration momentarily stops varying. So we need curves that separate the signs of the rates.

**Definition 5 (nullcline).** A <Def id="isocline">**nullcline**</Def> is a curve where one of the rates vanishes:

- on $f(x,y)=0$, we have $x'=0$ and the arrows are vertical;
- on $g(x,y)=0$, we have $y'=0$ and the arrows are horizontal.

The intersections of the two families are the equilibrium points.

**Example (chemistry) 5.** The nullclines are $x=0$ or $y=1$ for $x'=0$, and $y=0$ or $x=1$ for $y'=0$; the interior lines cross at $(1,1)$.

**Proposition 3 (reading by signs).** In a region without nullclines, the signs of $f$ and $g$ do not change. They immediately give the direction of the arrow:

| Signs | Direction |
| --- | --- |
| $f>0$, $g>0$ | right and up ↗ |
| $f<0$, $g>0$ | left and up ↖ |
| $f<0$, $g<0$ | left and down ↙ |
| $f>0$, $g<0$ | right and down ↘ |

**Example (chemistry) 6.** For $x>0$ and $y>0$, the running example's table becomes

| Region | Sign of $x'$ | Sign of $y'$ | Direction |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x>1$, $y<1$ | $+$ | $+$ | ↗ |
| $x>1$, $y>1$ | $-$ | $+$ | ↖ |
| $x<1$, $y>1$ | $-$ | $-$ | ↙ |
| $x<1$, $y<1$ | $+$ | $-$ | ↘ |

The arrows therefore turn anticlockwise around $(1,1)$.

**Method 1 (building a phase portrait).**

1. specify the physical domain;
2. solve $f=g=0$ to find the equilibrium points;
3. plot $f=0$ and $g=0$ separately;
4. test one point in each region to orient the arrows;
5. join the arrows with trajectories without letting them cross.

## 3. Testing the neighbourhood of a motionless state

**Tool 3 — test.** The sign table gives a global view; near a stop, a local computation often lets us decide whether a small perturbation comes back or grows.

Problem: finding a motionless point does not say what a slightly perturbed mixture will do. So we need to distinguish the states that hold the paths back from those that push them away.

**Definition 6 (stability).** <Def id="stabilite">**Stability**</Def> describes a perturbation:
stable if every start close enough stays close, asymptotically stable if it comes back, unstable if it can move away.

**Example (chemistry) 7.** Around $(1,1)$, the table of Example 6 makes the arrows turn. It suggests that the concentrations stay close, without proving that they return to the point.

Problem: we must gather the field's first derivatives to compute the effect of a small perturbation. So we need a matrix of its local variations.

**Definition 7 (Jacobian matrix).** The <Def id="jacobienne">**Jacobian matrix**</Def> of $F=(f,g)$ gathers its first partial derivatives:

$$
J_F(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}f_x(x,y)&f_y(x,y)\\g_x(x,y)&g_y(x,y)\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Example (chemistry) 8.** For the running example,

$$
J_F(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1-y&-x\\y&x-1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad J_F(1,1)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Problem: we still have to connect this matrix to the motion near $X_*$. So we need to replace the complicated formulas locally by their simplest part.

**Definition 8 (linearisation).** The <Def id="linearisation">**linearisation**</Def> replaces
the system near an equilibrium point $X_*$ by its linear approximation:
$u'\approx J_F(X_*)u$, where $u=X-X_*$.

**Example (chemistry) 9.** Writing $x=1+u$ and $y=1+v$, the linearisation of the running example is

$$
\begin{pmatrix}u'\\v'\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}u\\v\end{pmatrix},
\qquad\text{soit}\qquad
u'=-v,\quad v'=u.
$$

Its <Terme id="valeur-propre">eigenvalues</Terme> are $i$ and $-i$: the linear approximation rotates without contracting or expanding.

<Rappel title="Eigenvalues and local evolution">

For $u'=Ju$, an eigendirection associated with $\lambda$ evolves like $e^{\lambda t}$. A negative real
part contracts, a positive one expands, and a zero one gives no verdict on the neglected terms.

</Rappel>

**Proposition 4 (validity of the linear verdict).** If no eigenvalue of $J_F(X_*)$ has
zero real part, the local nonlinear portrait has the same type and the same stability as its
linearisation. The equilibrium point is then said to be **hyperbolic**.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — origin and limits of linearisation">

Taylor's formula applied to $F$ at $X_*$ gives

$$
F(X_*+u)=\underbrace{F(X_*)}_{=\,0}+J_F(X_*)u+R(u),
\qquad \|R(u)\|=o(\|u\|).
$$

Since $u'=X'=F(X_*+u)$,

$$
u'=J_F(X_*)u+R(u).
$$

The Hartman–Grobman theorem states that, if no eigenvalue has zero real part, the nonlinear motion is locally equivalent to the linear motion.

The condition is necessary: $z'=-z^3$ and $z'=z^3$ have the same linearisation $u'=0$ at $0$,
but the first brings back toward $0$ and the second moves away from it. When the linear term vanishes, the remainder decides. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Method 2 (obtaining a local verdict).**

1. compute the Jacobian and evaluate it at the equilibrium point;
2. compute its eigenvalues;
3. if their real parts are all nonzero, apply Proposition 4;
4. if one of them is zero, suspend the verdict and look for an extra argument.

In the hyperbolic case, two negative real eigenvalues give a stable
<Terme id="noeud">node</Terme>; two positive ones, an unstable node; opposite signs, a
<Terme id="point-selle">saddle point</Terme>; a complex pair, a <Terme id="foyer">focus</Terme>
that is stable or unstable according to the sign of its real part.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Autonomous system: $X'=F(X)$; equilibrium point: $F(X_*)=0$.
- Nullclines: $f=0$ gives vertical arrows; $g=0$, horizontal arrows.
- Jacobian: $J_F=\begin{pmatrix}f_x&f_y\\g_x&g_y\end{pmatrix}$.
- Linearisation: $u'\approx J_F(X_*)u$; zero real part = no automatic verdict.

</KeyResults>

## Worked example — deciding whether the mixture settles or keeps circling

**Example 10.** Let's take up $x'=x(1-y)$, $y'=y(x-1)$ again from $(2,0{,}5)$.

**Step 1 — compute the initial rate.** By Definition 1,
$F(2,0{,}5)=(1,0{,}5)$.

*Why?* This value answers the mission's first question: both concentrations
increase at first, at respective rates $1$ and $0{,}5$.

**Step 2 — locate all the physical stops.** Definition 4 gives $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$.
Since each rate contains its concentration as a factor, a positive start crosses no axis.

*Why?* The physical domain rules out negative concentrations and keeps the interior stop $(1,1)$.

**Step 3 — orient the four regions.** The nullclines $x=1$ and $y=1$, then Proposition 3, give ↗, ↖, ↙, ↘ around $(1,1)$.

*Why?* This anticlockwise tour rules out direct extinction without demanding an explicit formula.

**Step 4 — test the interior point.** By Definitions 7 and 8,
$J_F(1,1)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\pm i$.

*Why?* Proposition 4 suspends the verdict: a zero real part does not say whether the model converges, diverges or loops.

**Step 5 — build a control suited to the model.** For $x>0$ and $y>0$, set
$H(x,y)=x-\ln x+y-\ln y$. Along a solution,

$$
\frac{\mathrm dH}{\mathrm dt}
=\left(1-\frac1x\right)x'
+\left(1-\frac1y\right)y'
=(x-1)(1-y)+(y-1)(x-1)=0.
$$

*Why?* This is the extra argument of Method 2: $H$ stays equal to
$H(2,0{,}5)=2{,}5$, versus $H(1,1)=2$. The trajectory therefore does not converge to $(1,1)$.

**Step 6 — conclude about the path.** $H$ tends to infinity at the axes and far away; the curve
$H=2{,}5$ around $(1,1)$ is therefore closed and the field does not vanish on it.

*Why?* The path travels this curve periodically: the concentrations oscillate without dying out or settling.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

Why were the eigenvalues $\pm i$ not enough, while the computation with $H$ concludes?

The eigenvalues only describe order 1, with neither contraction nor expansion here. The exact computation
of $H$ forbids changing the curve $H=c$ and proves the persistence of the oscillation.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Example 11 — keeping the classification for the nonlinear field">

For $x'=x-y$ and $y'=x^2-1$, the nullclines are $y=x$ and $x=\pm1$, so the equilibrium
points are $(1,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$. The Jacobian is
$J(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\2x&0\end{pmatrix}$.

At $(1,1)$, trace $1$, determinant $2$ and discriminant $-7$ give an unstable focus.
At $(-1,-1)$, the determinant $-2$ gives a saddle point. Both are hyperbolic.

Finally, $F(0,0)=(0,-1)$ points downward, $F(2,0)=(2,3)$ toward ↗ and $F(0,2)=(-2,-1)$ toward ↙.

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Confusing a nullcline and a trajectory.** A nullcline cancels a single rate; trajectories
cross it vertically if $x'=0$, horizontally if $y'=0$, and almost never follow it.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Letting two trajectories cross.** An initial condition determines a single future. Two curves can tend to the same point without crossing there in finite time.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Classifying a borderline case with the Jacobian alone.** A zero real part suspends the verdict; the running example and $z'=\pm z^3$ show that the nonlinear terms decide.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the physical domain.** Concentrations satisfy $x\ge0$, $y\ge0$. If
$x'=x(\cdots)$, the axis $x=0$ cannot be crossed: check this invariance before interpreting.

</Pitfall>

## Lesson summary

You now have the three capabilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Transform:** read $X'=F(X)$ as an arrow and follow trajectories that do not cross.
- **Spot:** solve $F(X_*)=0$, plot $f=0$, $g=0$, then orient by the signs.
- **Test:** compute $J_F(X_*)$ and study $u'\approx J_F(X_*)u$.
- **Know when to suspend:** a zero real part demands an extra nonlinear argument.
- **Interpret in chemistry:** an equilibrium point is stationary; a closed loop describes periodic concentrations.
- **Running example:** from $(2,0{,}5)$, the rate is first $(1,0{,}5)$ and the mixture circles on $H=2{,}5$ around $(1,1)$.

</KeyResults>
