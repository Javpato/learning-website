<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td2-01">

<Statement>

For $f(x,y)=x^2/4-y^2$, study the slices $x=\mathrm{constante}$ and
$y=\mathrm{constante}$, then the <Terme id="ligne-de-niveau">level sets</Terme> $f=-1,0,1$. Identify the <Terme id="graphe">surface</Terme>.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>The two quadratic terms have opposite signs.</Hint>
<Hint>The zero level factors into two lines.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>Hyperbolic paraboloid; $f=0$ gives $y=\pm x/2$, and the other level sets are hyperbolas with opposite orientations.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

At $y=y_0$, the slice is an upward-opening parabola; at $x=x_0$, it is a
downward-opening parabola. The level sets satisfy
$x^2/4-y^2=c$. For $c>0$, the transverse axis is the $x$-axis; for
$c<0$, it is the $y$-axis; for $c=0$, $(x/2-y)(x/2+y)=0$. These crossed
slices characterize a <Terme id="point-selle">saddle</Terme>: the hyperbolic paraboloid.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Calling the surface an “elliptic paraboloid” despite the opposite signs.
- Drawing all level sets with the same orientation.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td2-02">

<Statement>

Study the <Terme id="limite">limit</Terme> at $(0,0)$ of
$f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2y}{x^4+y^2}$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>The lines $y=mx$ are not suited to the degree of the denominator.</Hint>
<Hint>Try the family $y=mx^2$.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>The limit does not exist: along $y=mx^2$, $f=m/(1+m^2)$.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

Substituting $y=mx^2$ with $x\ne0$ gives

$$
f(x,mx^2)=\frac{mx^4}{x^4+m^2x^4}
=\frac{m}{1+m^2}.
$$

The value depends on $m$: for example, it is $0$ for $m=0$ and $1/2$
for $m=1$. Two paths that give different limits are enough to
disprove the existence of the limit.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Testing only the axes, both of which give zero.
- Believing that several paths with the same limit prove existence.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td2-03">

<Statement>

Prove that
$\displaystyle\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}\dfrac{x^3y^2}{x^4+y^4}=0$.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>Use $2x^2y^2\le x^4+y^4$.</Hint>
<Hint>Separate a factor $|x|$ in the numerator.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>The absolute value is bounded above by $|x|/2$, so the <Terme id="limite">limit</Terme> is zero.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

For $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$,

$$
\left|\frac{x^3y^2}{x^4+y^4}\right|
=|x|\frac{x^2y^2}{x^4+y^4}
\le\frac{|x|}{2}.
$$

Since $|x|\le\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\to0$, the squeeze theorem gives the zero
limit, independently of the path.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Using the inequality in the wrong direction.
- Concluding after path tests instead of providing a uniform bound.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td2-04">

<Statement>

Set $f(0,0)=0$ and, elsewhere,
$f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2}$. Prove <Terme id="continuite">continuity</Terme>, calculate the <Terme id="derivee-partielle">partial
derivatives</Terme> at the origin, and decide whether $f$ is <Terme id="differentiabilite">differentiable</Terme> there.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>For continuity, bound $|x|^2/(x^2+y^2)$.</Hint>
<Hint>On the axes, the function is identically zero.</Hint>
<Hint>For differentiability, test $f(x,x)/\sqrt{2x^2}$.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>$f$ is continuous, $f_x(0,0)=f_y(0,0)=0$, but it is not differentiable at the origin.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

We have $|f(x,y)|\le|y|\to0$, so $f$ is continuous. The difference quotients
$f(h,0)/h$ and $f(0,h)/h$ are zero: both partial derivatives equal
zero. If $f$ were differentiable, its <Terme id="differentielle-totale">differential</Terme> would be zero and
$f(x,y)/\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ would tend to zero. But along $y=x>0$,

$$
\frac{f(x,x)}{\sqrt{2x^2}}
=\frac{x/2}{\sqrt2x}=\frac1{2\sqrt2}.
$$

The remainder criterion fails.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Inferring differentiability from the existence of partial derivatives.
- Forgetting to divide the remainder by the norm of the increment.

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>

<ExerciseView id="math-fmv-td2-05">

<Statement>

Let
$V(x,y)=3e^{-(x^2+2y^2)}-2e^{-((x-2)^2+2y^2)}$.
Without completely solving $\nabla V=0$, describe its symmetry, behavior at
infinity, hill and well regions, and the appearance of a <Terme id="point-selle">saddle</Terme>.

</Statement>
<HintLadder>

<Hint>Observe the dependence on $y^2$.</Hint>
<Hint>Each exponential is localized near its center.</Hint>
<Hint>A saddle is revealed by a change in the topology of the <Terme id="ligne-de-niveau">level curves</Terme>.</Hint>

</HintLadder>
<FinalAnswer>$V$ is even in $y$, tends to zero, has a positive hill near $(0,0)$ and a negative well near $(2,0)$; a saddle region separates their level-set basins.</FinalAnswer>
<Solution>

The transformation $y\mapsto-y$ does not change $V$, so <Terme id="point-critique">critical points</Terme>
off the axis would appear in pairs; here both bumps are centered on
$y=0$. The exponentials tend to zero at infinity. Near $(0,0)$, the
positive term dominates; near $(2,0)$, the negative term dominates. As a
level varies between these two regions, closed components deform
and then reconnect: the transition level passes through a saddle point,
where the landscape rises in one direction and falls in the other.

</Solution>
<CommonErrors>

- Presenting the origin and $(2,0)$ as exact critical points without calculation.
- Confusing “tends to zero” with “becomes identically zero.”

</CommonErrors>

</ExerciseView>
