<ExamProblem title="Exercise 1 — A curve in polar coordinates" points={4}>

Convert the Cartesian equation

$$
x^2 + y^2 = 2x
$$

to <Terme id="coordonnees-polaires">polar coordinates</Terme>, then precisely identify the resulting curve
(type, center, radius).

<Collapsible title="Corrigé and indicative barème (marking scheme)">

**Conversion (2 pts).** With $x = r\cos\theta$ and $y = r\sin\theta$, we have
$x^2 + y^2 = r^2$, so the equation becomes

$$
r^2 = 2r\cos\theta .
$$

For $r \neq 0$, we can simplify by $r$:

$$
r = 2\cos\theta .
$$

The point $r = 0$ (the origin) is reached by this curve anyway at
$\theta = \pi/2$, so no solution is lost through simplification.

**Identification (2 pts).** Returning to Cartesian coordinates:

$$
x^2 - 2x + y^2 = 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
(x-1)^2 + y^2 = 1 .
$$

This is the **circle with center $(1, 0)$ and radius $1$**, which passes through
the origin.

**What the examiner rewards.** Clean substitution
($x^2+y^2 = r^2$, $x = r\cos\theta$), discussion of the case $r = 0$ (do not
divide blindly by $r$), and complete identification: saying
“a circle” without its center or radius earns only some of the points.

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>

<ExamProblem title="Exercise 2 — Continuity and differentiability at the origin" points={6}>

Let the function be

$$
f(x,y) = \frac{x\,y^2}{x^2 + y^2}
\quad\text{pour } (x,y) \neq (0,0),
\qquad
f(0,0) = 0 .
$$

1. Study the <Terme id="continuite">continuity</Terme> of $f$ at $(0,0)$.
2. Calculate the <Terme id="derivee-partielle">partial derivatives</Terme> $f_x(0,0)$ and $f_y(0,0)$.
3. Is the function $f$ <Terme id="differentiabilite">differentiable</Terme> at $(0,0)$? Justify your answer
   completely.

<Collapsible title="Corrigé and indicative barème">

**1. Continuity — polar bound (2 pts).** In <Terme id="coordonnees-polaires">polar coordinates</Terme>,
$|x| \le r$ and $y^2 \le r^2$, with $r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, so

$$
|f(x,y)| = \frac{|x|\,y^2}{x^2+y^2} \le \frac{r \cdot r^2}{r^2} = r
\xrightarrow[(x,y)\to(0,0)]{} 0 = f(0,0).
$$

The bound $|f| \le r$ (of the form $|f| \le C r^\alpha$ with
$\alpha = 1$) proves that $f$ is **continuous at the origin**.

**2. Partial derivatives at the origin (2 pts).** We return to the
definition because the differentiation formulas do not apply at the joining
point:

$$
f_x(0,0) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,0) - f(0,0)}{h}
= \lim_{h\to0}\frac{0 - 0}{h} = 0,
\qquad
f_y(0,0) = \lim_{k\to0}\frac{f(0,k) - 0}{k} = 0 .
$$

Both partial derivatives exist and **equal $0$**.

**3. Nondifferentiability (2 pts).** If $f$ were differentiable at
$(0,0)$, its <Terme id="differentielle-totale">differential</Terme> would be the zero map (because
$\nabla f(0,0) = (0,0)$), and we would have

$$
\frac{f(h,k) - f(0,0) - 0}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2}}
= \frac{h k^2}{(h^2+k^2)^{3/2}}
\xrightarrow[(h,k)\to(0,0)]{} 0 .
$$

But along the path $h = k = t$ with $t > 0$:

$$
\frac{t^3}{(2t^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{t^3}{2\sqrt2\,|t|^3} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt2}
\neq 0 .
$$

The quotient does not tend to $0$: $f$ is **not differentiable** at
$(0,0)$, although it is continuous there with zero partial derivatives.

**What the examiner rewards.** Three distinct strategies, one for each
question: a *bound* to prove continuity (not a collection of
paths!), a *return to the definition* for the partial derivatives at the joining
point, and the *remainder test* $o(\|\mathbf h\|)$ disproved by a well-chosen
path. This is the counterexample to remember: the existence of partial derivatives
does not imply differentiability.

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>

<ExamProblem title="Exercise 3 — Logarithmic differential of a rate constant" points={4}>

A kinetic model gives a constant of the form

$$
K = c^2\,T^{-1}\,e^{-E/(RT)},
$$

where $c$, $T$, and $E$ are measured quantities (with $R$ an exact constant).
Establish the expression for the logarithmic <Terme id="differentielle-totale">differential</Terme> $\dfrac{dK}{K}$ in
terms of $dc$, $dT$, and $dE$.

<Collapsible title="Corrigé and indicative barème">

**Taking logarithms (1 pt).** Products of powers and the exponential
invite us to differentiate $\ln K$:

$$
\ln K = 2\ln c - \ln T - \frac{E}{RT} .
$$

**Term-by-term differentiation (2 pts).** The first two terms give
$2\,dc/c$ and $-\,dT/T$. For the last one, $E$ **and** $T$ vary:

$$
d\!\left(\frac{E}{RT}\right)
= \frac{dE}{RT} - \frac{E}{RT^2}\,dT .
$$

**Result (1 pt).**

$$
\frac{dK}{K} = 2\,\frac{dc}{c} - \frac{dT}{T} - \frac{dE}{RT}
+ \frac{E}{RT^2}\,dT .
$$

**What the examiner rewards.** The “logarithmic differential” reflex
for a product of powers, and especially not forgetting that
the term $E/(RT)$ depends on *two* variables: differentiation with respect to $T$ of
the factor $1/T$ produces the often-missed term $+E\,dT/(RT^2)$. Notice
that the two contributions involving $dT$ ($-dT/T$ and $+E\,dT/(RT^2)$) can
cancel each other: this is the starting point for sensitivity discussions in
kinetics.

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>

<ExamProblem title="Exercise 4 — Nature of the origin depending on a parameter" points={6}>

For a real parameter $a$, let

$$
f(x,y) = x^2 + 2axy + y^2 .
$$

1. Verify that the origin is a <Terme id="point-critique">critical point</Terme> of $f$.
2. Classify the origin (minimum, maximum, <Terme id="point-selle">saddle point</Terme>, degenerate case) according to
   the values of $a$, justifying each case.

<Collapsible title="Corrigé and indicative barème">

**1. Critical point (1 pt).**

$$
\nabla f = (2x + 2ay,\ 2ax + 2y),
\qquad
\nabla f(0,0) = (0,0) .
$$

The origin is indeed a critical point (for every $a$).

**2. Hessian and discriminant (2 pts).**

$$
H_f = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2a \\ 2a & 2 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D = f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2 = 4 - 4a^2 = 4(1 - a^2).
$$

**Discussion according to $a$ (3 pts).**

- If $|a| < 1$: $D > 0$ and $f_{xx} = 2 > 0$, so the origin is a
  **<Terme id="extremum-local">local minimum</Terme>** (in fact global: the quadratic form is positive
  definite).
- If $|a| > 1$: $D < 0$, so the origin is a **saddle point** — $f$ takes
  values of both signs in every neighborhood.
- If $|a| = 1$: $D = 0$, the test is **degenerate**. We conclude directly:
  for $a = 1$, $f = (x+y)^2 \ge 0$, and it vanishes along the entire line $y = -x$;
  for $a = -1$, $f = (x-y)^2$, and it vanishes on $y = x$. The origin is a
  **non-strict** global minimum (an entire line of minima): this is the
  degenerate case.

**What the examiner rewards.** Correct calculation of $D$, an
*exhaustive* discussion of the three cases, and especially honest treatment of the case
$D = 0$: saying “we cannot conclude” is not enough when an
obvious factorization ($f = (x \pm y)^2$) allows you to conclude.

</Collapsible>

</ExamProblem>
