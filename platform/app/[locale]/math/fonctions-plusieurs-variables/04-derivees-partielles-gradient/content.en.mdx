# Predicting how G varies when temperature and pressure change together

<Mission fil="A thermodynamics experiment — predicting the variation of G in the laboratory">

**Situation.** Near $T=298\ \mathrm K$ and $P=1{,}0\ \mathrm{bar}$, raising only $T$ by
$1\ \mathrm K$ makes $G$ drop by $0{,}20\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$; raising only $P$ by
$1\ \mathrm{bar}$ makes $G$ rise by $0{,}020\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. During the experiment,
$T$ rises by $2\ \mathrm K$ while $P$ falls by $0{,}1\ \mathrm{bar}$.
**Precise question.** By how much does $G$ vary, and does it increase or decrease?
**Obstacle.** In L1 (first year), only one input moved at a time; here the two effects arrive together.
**Contract.** In this chapter: 3 tools (isolate, assemble, follow). By the end, we solve this problem, numbers in hand.

</Mission>

## The battle plan

To fulfil the contract, we need three tools:

1. **isolate** the instantaneous effect of one variable while the other stays fixed;
2. **assemble** those effects into a local model that predicts a small simultaneous variation;
3. **follow** an experiment over time and compare the possible directions.

Each section delivers one tool, with the same quantities $G$, $T$ and $P$ as the running thread.

## 1. Isolating the effect of each variable

**Tool 1 — isolate.** Let us begin by translating the laboratory's two separate measurements.

Problem: to measure the effect of $T$, we must keep $P$ still while $T$ moves by a smaller and smaller amount. So we need a name for this local rate.

**Definition 1 (partial derivative).** The <Def id="derivee-partielle">**partial derivative**</Def> (the rate of change when a single variable moves while the others are frozen) of $f(x,y)$ with respect to $x$ at the point $(a,b)$ is

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a,b)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)}{h}.$$

Likewise,

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a,b)=\lim_{k\to0}\frac{f(a,b+k)-f(a,b)}{k}.$$

These numbers are also written $f_x(a,b)$, $f_y(a,b)$, $\partial_xf(a,b)$ and $\partial_yf(a,b)$.

**Example (chemistry) 1.** The mission data directly say

$$\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P}=-0{,}20\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}\,K^{-1}},\qquad \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial P}\right)_{\!T}=0{,}020\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}\,bar^{-1}}.$$

The first number is negative: at fixed pressure, heating makes $G$ drop locally. The second is positive: at fixed temperature, compressing makes $G$ rise locally.

**Remark (chemical notation).** In thermodynamics you will write $\left(\partial G/\partial T\right)_P$: the subscript $P$ recalls what is frozen. It is the same object as $G_T$, in an outfit that makes the experimental condition visible.

**Example 2.** For $f(x,y)=\ln(x^2+y^2)$, keeping $y$ constant and then $x$ constant,

$$f_x(x,y)=\frac{2x}{x^2+y^2},\qquad f_y(x,y)=\frac{2y}{x^2+y^2}.$$

At the origin, the formula itself is not defined: so we do not ask for these two numbers there.

<Rappel title="One-variable differentiation rules">

During a computation with respect to $x$, treat $y$ as a constant. The L1 rules remain valid: $(u^n)'=nu^{n-1}u'$ and $(\ln u)'=u'/u$.

</Rappel>

Problem: two separate numbers describe two effects, but we want to keep them in a single object that also indicates an orientation. So we need to assemble them.

**Definition 2 (gradient).** The <Def id="gradient">**gradient**</Def> (the vector of the separate rates, oriented toward the steepest ascent) is

$$\nabla f(a,b)=\bigl(f_x(a,b),f_y(a,b)\bigr).$$

It is perpendicular to the regular <Terme id="ligne-de-niveau">level curve</Terme> passing through $(a,b)$; Proposition 2 will explain why.

**Example (chemistry) 3.** For $G$, in the announced units,

$$\nabla G(298,1{,}0)=(-0{,}20,\ 0{,}020).$$

This pair simultaneously keeps the sign and the intensity of the two experimental responses.

**Example 4 (bridge with the energy surface).** For $E(q_1,q_2)=(q_1^2-1)^2+q_2^2$,

$$\nabla E=\bigl(4q_1(q_1^2-1),2q_2\bigr),\qquad \nabla E(0{,}5,0{,}5)=(-1{,}5,1).$$

The opposite arrow is $(1{,}5,-1)$, so the fastest descent locally increases $q_1$ and decreases $q_2$. This result from the old surface is now obtained by Definition 2.

## 2. Assembling a small simultaneous variation

**Tool 2 — assemble.** We now look for a local formula that accepts both a small change of $T$ and a small change of $P$ at the same time.

Problem: a curved surface is hard to compute with near a point. So we need its best flat model in a neighbourhood of that point.

**Definition 3 (tangent plane).** For a sufficiently regular function, the <Def id="plan-tangent">**tangent plane**</Def> (the plane hugging the surface at the point, hence its best linear approximation) to the <Terme id="graphe">graph</Terme> of $f$ above $(a,b)$ has equation

$$z=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b).$$

**Example (chemistry) 5.** If $G_0=G(298,1{,}0)$, the laboratory data give the flat model

$$z=G_0-0{,}20(T-298)+0{,}020(P-1{,}0),$$

with the mission's units. It locally replaces the unknown surface of $G$ with a formula that is easy to evaluate.

**Remark.** The plane's coefficients are numbers evaluated at the reference point. Leaving $f_x(x,y)$ or $f_y(x,y)$ in its equation would still produce a curved surface, not a plane.

Problem: writing a candidate plane does not guarantee that its error becomes negligible when we zoom in. So we need a precise property that carries this guarantee.

**Definition 4 (differentiability).** <Def id="differentiabilite">**Differentiability**</Def> (the property of being well approximated by the tangent plane, stronger than the mere existence of the separate rates) means that at $\mathbf a=(a,b)$,

$$f(\mathbf a+\mathbf h)=f(\mathbf a)+f_x(\mathbf a)h_x+f_y(\mathbf a)h_y+o(\|\mathbf h\|).$$

In other words, after dividing by the size $\|\mathbf h\|$ of the displacement, the error of the linear model tends to zero.

**Example 6.** For an affine function $f(x,y)=2x-3y+5$, the model is the function itself: the error is exactly zero for every $\mathbf h$. It therefore immediately satisfies Definition 4.

**Proposition 1 (sufficient criterion).** If $f_x$ and $f_y$ exist and are continuous in a neighbourhood of a point, then $f$ satisfies Definition 4 there.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous layer — why the separate rates are not enough">

$$f(x,y)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{x^2y}{x^2+y^2},&(x,y)\ne(0,0),\\0,&(x,y)=(0,0).\end{cases}$$

The quotients $f(h,0)/h$ and $f(0,k)/k$ are zero: the two separate rates exist and equal zero at the origin. If Definition 4 were satisfied, we would need $f(x,y)/\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\to0$. Yet, on $y=x$ with $x>0$,

$$\frac{f(x,x)}{\sqrt{2x^2}}=\frac{x/2}{\sqrt2x}=\frac1{2\sqrt2}.$$

The limit is not zero: this model is not well approximated by its candidate plane. The existence of the two numbers of Definition 1 is therefore not enough; the continuity required in Proposition 1 is a practical guarantee.

</Collapsible>

Problem: the plane gives a new value, but in the laboratory we want directly the predicted variation when all the inputs move. So we need to name the linear part.

**Definition 5 (total differential).** The <Def id="differentielle-totale">**total differential**</Def> (the first-order variation when all the variables move together) is

$$df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy.$$

At the point $(a,b)$, it turns the small displacement $(dx,dy)$ into the variation given by the plane of Definition 3.

**Example (chemistry) 7.** For the mission,

$$dG=-0{,}20\,dT+0{,}020\,dP.$$

Both contributions keep their sign: heating with $dT>0$ makes $G$ drop, and decompressing with $dP<0$ also makes it drop.

**Method 1 (predicting a small variation).**

1. compute the numbers of Definition 1;
2. evaluate them at the reference point and check Proposition 1;
3. write Definition 5 with the units;
4. replace each displacement by its signed value;
5. add up the contributions and state that this is a first-order approximation.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Effect of $x$ alone: $f_x(a,b)=\lim_{h\to0}[f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)]/h$.
- Local plane: $z=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b)$.
- Simultaneous variation: $df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy$.
- Practical guarantee: $f_x$ and $f_y$ continuous near the point imply a remainder $o(\|\mathbf h\|)$.

</KeyResults>

## 3. Following an experiment and choosing a direction

**Tool 3 — follow.** The experiment imposes $T=T(t)$ and $P=P(t)$: we must convert the two-input model into a rate along this programme.

Problem: $G$ depends on $t$ through two intermediates, $T(t)$ and $P(t)$, whose contributions must add up. So we need a formula for this nesting.

**Definition 6 (chain rule).** The <Def id="regle-de-la-chaine">**chain rule**</Def> (the formula that adds up the contributions of the intermediate variables) gives, for $\mathbf r(t)=(x(t),y(t))$,

$$\frac{d}{dt}f\bigl(x(t),y(t)\bigr)=f_x\,x'(t)+f_y\,y'(t)=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t).$$

**Example (chemistry) 8.** Let us parametrise the experiment's minute by

$$T(t)=298+2t,\qquad P(t)=1{,}0-0{,}1t,\qquad 0\le t\le1.$$

As long as the two sensitivities stay nearly constant,

$$\frac{dG}{dt}=(-0{,}20)(2)+(0{,}020)(-0{,}1)=-0{,}402\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}\,min^{-1}}.$$

Each term pairs an experimental response with the rate of the corresponding variable.

**Remark (several outputs).** For vector-valued maps, the <Terme id="jacobienne">Jacobian matrices</Terme> collect all the separate rates and multiply in the order $D(f\circ g)=Df(g)\,Dg$. This is the matrix version of Definition 6, useful in TD 3.3.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — the chain rule">

Definition 4, applied at $\mathbf a=\mathbf r(t)$, gives

$$f(\mathbf a+\mathbf h)-f(\mathbf a)=\nabla f(\mathbf a)\cdot\mathbf h+\varepsilon(\mathbf h)\|\mathbf h\|,\qquad \varepsilon(\mathbf h)\xrightarrow[\mathbf h\to0]{}0.$$

Take $\mathbf h=\mathbf r(t+\delta)-\mathbf r(t)$ and divide by $\delta$:

$$\frac{f(\mathbf r(t+\delta))-f(\mathbf r(t))}{\delta}=\nabla f\cdot\frac{\mathbf r(t+\delta)-\mathbf r(t)}{\delta}+\varepsilon(\mathbf h)\frac{\|\mathbf h\|}{\delta}.$$

As $\delta\to0$, the first term tends to $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t)$. In the second, $\|\mathbf h\|/\delta$ stays bounded while $\varepsilon(\mathbf h)\to0$; the product therefore tends to zero. We obtain Definition 6. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

Problem: the previous rate depends both on the chosen direction and on the speed of travel. To compare directions only, we must impose an equal length on their vectors.

**Definition 7 (directional derivative).** The <Def id="derivee-directionnelle">**directional derivative**</Def> (the slope when setting off in a chosen unit direction) of $f$ at the point $\mathbf a$ along $\mathbf u$ is

$$D_{\mathbf u}f(\mathbf a)=\lim_{t\to0}\frac{f(\mathbf a+t\mathbf u)-f(\mathbf a)}{t}=\nabla f(\mathbf a)\cdot\mathbf u,\qquad \|\mathbf u\|=1,$$

when Definition 4 applies.

**Example 9.** For $f(x,y)=\ln(x^2+y^2)$, we will see that $\nabla f(1,1)=(1,1)$. In the unit direction $\mathbf u=(1,-1)/\sqrt2$,

$$D_{\mathbf u}f(1,1)=(1,1)\cdot\frac{(1,-1)}{\sqrt2}=0.$$

So we move locally with no first-order variation.

**Proposition 2 (geometry of the gradient).** If $\nabla f(\mathbf a)\ne\mathbf0$, then:

1. $\nabla f(\mathbf a)$ is normal to the regular level curve passing through $\mathbf a$;
2. the maximal slope equals $\|\nabla f(\mathbf a)\|$ in the direction $\nabla f(\mathbf a)/\|\nabla f(\mathbf a)\|$;
3. the minimal slope equals $-\|\nabla f(\mathbf a)\|$ in the opposite direction.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — normality and maximal slope">

Let $t\mapsto\mathbf r(t)$ be a regular parametrisation of a curve on which $f(\mathbf r(t))=c$. Definition 6 gives

$$0=\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t).$$

The gradient is therefore orthogonal to the tangent vector $\mathbf r'(t)$. For a unit vector $\mathbf u$ making an angle $\theta$ with the gradient, Definition 7 gives

$$D_{\mathbf u}f=\|\nabla f\|\cos\theta.$$

The maximum is reached for $\theta=0$, the minimum for $\theta=\pi$ and the value zero for $\theta=\pi/2$. The three statements follow. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — obtaining a plane, an estimate and a slope

**Example 10.** Let us study $f(x,y)=\ln(x^2+y^2)$ near $(1,1)$; this computation directly prepares TD 3.1.

**Step 1 — check where the computation makes sense.** We need $x^2+y^2>0$, so the domain is $\mathbb R^2\setminus\{(0,0)\}$ and $(1,1)$ is interior to this domain.

*Why?* A local model can only be built around a point where the formula exists.

**Step 2 — compute the two separate effects.** By Definition 1,

$$f_x(x,y)=\frac{2x}{x^2+y^2},\qquad f_y(x,y)=\frac{2y}{x^2+y^2}.$$

*Why?* Definition 3 requires these two coefficients to build the flat model.

**Step 3 — evaluate and write the plane.** We obtain

$$f(1,1)=\ln2,\qquad \nabla f(1,1)=(1,1),$$

then, by Definition 3,

$$z=\ln2+(x-1)+(y-1).$$

*Why?* Evaluating at the reference point turns the coefficients into constants and makes the plane numerically usable.

**Step 4 — estimate a nearby value.** Here $\Delta x=0{,}02$ and $\Delta y=-0{,}03$, so

$$f(1{,}02,0{,}97)\approx\ln2+0{,}02-0{,}03=\ln2-0{,}01\approx0{,}6831.$$

*Why?* Definition 5 converts the two small signed displacements into a correction of the known value.

**Step 5 — test a direction of nearly constant value.** For $\mathbf u=(1,-1)/\sqrt2$,

$$D_{\mathbf u}f(1,1)=(1,1)\cdot\frac{(1,-1)}{\sqrt2}=0.$$

*Why?* Proposition 2 predicts that a direction perpendicular to the gradient does not change the value at first order.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

Why must we use $\Delta y=-0{,}03$ and not $0{,}97$ in step 4?

The plane measures a displacement from the reference point: $0{,}97-1=-0{,}03$. Using $0{,}97$ would act as if we started from $y=0$ and would destroy the local character of the estimate.

</Collapsible>

**Example 11 — your turn, with less help.** Set $u=x^2-y^2$, $v=2xy$ and $F(x,y)=e^u\cos v$. You are given

$$\frac{\partial(e^u\cos v)}{\partial u}=e^u\cos v,\qquad \frac{\partial(e^u\cos v)}{\partial v}=-e^u\sin v.$$

**Your turn:** apply Definition 6 to form $F_x$ and $F_y$, then evaluate the last two computations at $(x,y)=(1,0)$.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

Since $u_x=2x$, $u_y=-2y$, $v_x=2y$ and $v_y=2x$,

$$F_x=2xe^u\cos v-2ye^u\sin v,\qquad F_y=-2ye^u\cos v-2xe^u\sin v.$$

At $(1,0)$, $(u,v)=(1,0)$, so $\nabla F(1,0)=(2e,0)$. This is the result of TD 3.3, obtained here with less scaffolding.

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Using a non-unit vector to compare slopes.** Definition 7 assumes $\|\mathbf u\|=1$. For a vector $\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0$, take $\mathbf u=\mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|$; otherwise the travel speed multiplies the result.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believing that two separate rates imply the plane model.** The counterexample of section 2 has both numbers at the origin without satisfying Definition 4. The local continuity given by Proposition 1 suffices.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the evaluation point in the tangent plane.** The coefficients are $f_x(a,b)$ and $f_y(a,b)$, hence constants. Letting them depend on $(x,y)$ does not give a plane.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confusing vector and slope.** The gradient is a vector in the plane of the variables. The slope along a chosen direction is the number $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$.

</Pitfall>

## Lesson summary

You now have the three capabilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Isolate:** compute $f_x$ and $f_y$ by freezing each other variable in turn.
- **Assemble:** use $df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy$ and the plane $z=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b)$.
- **Guarantee:** recognise the remainder $o(\|\mathbf h\|)$; continuous separate rates suffice.
- **Follow:** apply $\frac d{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t)$.
- **Choose:** compute $D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$ with $\|\mathbf u\|=1$.
- **Read the gradient:** normal to the curves of equal value, steepest ascent along its own direction and steepest descent in the opposite one.
- **Predict in chemistry:** write $dG=G_T\,dT+G_P\,dP$ with conditions and units visible.

</KeyResults>
