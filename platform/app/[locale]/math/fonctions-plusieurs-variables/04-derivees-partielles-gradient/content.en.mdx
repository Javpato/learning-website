## Intuition

Place yourself on the surface $z = f(x, y)$ above a point $(a, b)$.

- Walks parallel to the axis $x$ while freezing $y = b$: the slope felt is $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b)$.
- Walks parallel to the axis $y$ while freezing $x = a$: the slope is $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b)$.
- Walk in any direction $\mathbf u$: the slope is a *linear mixture* of the previous two — this is directional derivative.

If you zoom close enough to a point where $f$ is differentiable, the surface becomes indistinguishable from a plane: **tangent plane**. All the slope information for this plane is contained in a single vector: the **gradient** $\nabla f$.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- differential: $df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy$.
- tangent plane: $z=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b)$.
- directional derivative: $D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$; $-\nabla f$ gives the steepest descent.

</KeyResults>

## Formal definition

### Partial derivatives

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a,b)
=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h,\,b)-f(a,b)}{h},
\qquad
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a,b)
=\lim_{k\to0}\frac{f(a,\,b+k)-f(a,b)}{k}.
$$

We also note $f_x$, $f_y$ or $\partial_x f$, $\partial_y f$.

### Differentiability

$f$ is **differentiable** to $\mathbf a$ if there exists a linear map $L$ such that

$$
f(\mathbf a+\mathbf h)
= f(\mathbf a) + L(\mathbf h) + o(\|\mathbf h\|).
$$

For two variables, the **differential** is written

$$
df = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy .
$$

<KeyResults title="Sufficient condition to remember">

If partial derivatives exist and are **continuous** in the neighborhood of a point, then $f$ is differentiable there. (The existence of partial derivatives alone is not enough — see pitfalls below.)

</KeyResults>

### Tangent plane

At the point $(a, b)$, the tangent plane at the graph $z = f(x,y)$ is

$$
z = f(a,b) + f_x(a,b)\,(x-a) + f_y(a,b)\,(y-b).
$$

### Gradient and directional derivative

$$
\nabla f = (f_x,\; f_y),
\qquad
D_{\mathbf u} f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf u
\quad\text{pour } \|\mathbf u\| = 1 .
$$

<KeyResults>

Two fundamental geometric properties of the gradient:

1. $\nabla f(a,b)$ is **normal** to regular level curve passing through $(a,b)$;
2. $\nabla f(a,b)$ points in the direction of **highest growth**, and $\|\nabla f\|$ is the maximum slope. In contrast, $-\nabla f$ is the steepest descent; perpendicularly, the slope is zero.

</KeyResults>

</Collapsible>

<Rappel title="differentiate a compound">

For a function of a variable, $(u^n)'=nu^{n-1}u'$ and $(\ln u)'=u'/u$. In partial derivative, applies the same rules by freezing all other variables.

</Rappel>

## Worked example

Let's take $f(x,y) = \ln(x^2 + y^2)$ at point $(1, 1)$.

**Step 1 — partial derivatives.**

$$
f_x = \frac{2x}{x^2+y^2},
\qquad
f_y = \frac{2y}{x^2+y^2}.
$$

**Step 2 — evaluation.** In $(1,1)$: $f(1,1)=\ln 2$ and $\nabla f(1,1) = (1,\,1)$.

**Step 3 — tangent plane.**

$$
z = \ln 2 + (x-1) + (y-1).
$$

**Step 4 — linear approximation.** To estimate $f(1.02,\,0.97)$:

$$
f \approx \ln 2 + 0.02 - 0.03 = \ln 2 - 0.01 \approx 0.6831 .
$$

**Step 5 — directional derivative.** In the direction of unit vector $\mathbf u = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$:

$$
D_{\mathbf u} f(1,1) = (1,1)\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1) = 0 .
$$

This direction is *tangent to the level curve*: we move there “at constant altitude”.

## Chain rule

If $x = x(t)$ and $y = y(t)$ describe a path, then

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\,f\bigl(x(t),\,y(t)\bigr)
= \nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t)
= f_x\,x'(t) + f_y\,y'(t).
$$

For compound vector applications, Jacobian matrices multiply: $D(f\circ g) = Df(g)\,Dg$.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — the chain rule based on differentiability">

By differentiability from $f$ to $\mathbf a = \mathbf r(t)$:

$$
f(\mathbf a + \mathbf h) - f(\mathbf a)
= \nabla f(\mathbf a)\cdot\mathbf h + \varepsilon(\mathbf h)\,\|\mathbf h\|,
\qquad \varepsilon(\mathbf h)\xrightarrow[\mathbf h\to0]{}0 .
$$

Consider $\mathbf h = \mathbf r(t+\delta) - \mathbf r(t)$. Dividing by $\delta$:

$$
\frac{f(\mathbf r(t+\delta)) - f(\mathbf r(t))}{\delta}
= \nabla f\cdot\frac{\mathbf r(t+\delta)-\mathbf r(t)}{\delta}
+ \varepsilon(\mathbf h)\,\frac{\|\mathbf h\|}{\delta}.
$$

When $\delta\to0$: the first term tends towards $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t)$; in the second, $\|\mathbf h\|/\delta$ tends to $\|\mathbf r'(t)\|$ (bounded) while $\varepsilon(\mathbf h)\to0$, so the product tends to $0$. Hence the result. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — why the gradient is perpendicular to level curves">

Let $t\mapsto\mathbf r(t)$ be a regular parametrization of a level curve: $f(\mathbf r(t)) = c$ for all $t$. By the chain rule:

$$
0 = \frac{d}{dt}\,f(\mathbf r(t)) = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t).
$$

The gradient is therefore orthogonal to the tangent vector $\mathbf r'(t)$ of the contour curve — that is to say normal to the curve. And like $D_{\mathbf u}f = \|\nabla f\|\cos\theta$ ($\theta$ = angle between $\mathbf u$ and $\nabla f$), the maximum is reached for $\theta = 0$: the steepest slope is *in* the direction of gradient, with value $\|\nabla f\|$. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Link with chemistry

For a state function like free enthalpy $G(T, P)$:

$$
dG = \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial T}\right)_{\!P} dT
   + \left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial P}\right)_{\!T} dP .
$$

This is *exactly* the local-linear structure of any differentiable scalar field — the thermodynamic notation $\left(\partial G/\partial T\right)_P$ simply remembers which variable is frozen. The coefficients have a physical meaning ($(\partial G/\partial T)_P = -S$, $(\partial G/\partial P)_T = V$): the partial derivatives *are* the measurable quantities of thermodynamics.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Use a non-unit vector for a directional derivative.** The formula $D_{\mathbf u} f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$ assumes $\|\mathbf u\| = 1$. With any vector $\mathbf v$, first normalize: $\mathbf u = \mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|$ — otherwise the result is multiplied by $\|\mathbf v\|$ and is no longer a slope.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believe that the existence of partial derivatives implies differentiability.** Classic counterexample: $f(x,y) = \dfrac{x^2 y}{x^2+y^2}$ (extended by $0$ at the origin) has zero partial derivatives in $(0,0)$… but is not differentiable there (exercise M2.4 of TD 2 demonstrates this). The *continuity* of the partial derivatives is enough.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forget the evaluation point in the tangent plane.** The coefficients $f_x$ and $f_y$ must be evaluated **at $(a,b)$**, not left as functions of $x$ and $y$. A plane has constant coefficients.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confuse the gradient (vector) with a slope (scalar).** The gradient is a vector in the $(x, y)$ plane of variables — not an altitude, not a number. The slope in a given direction is the *dot product* $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$.

</Pitfall>

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- partial derivative = derivative with one variable, the others frozen.
- Differentiability = approximation by a plane: $f(\mathbf a + \mathbf h) = f(\mathbf a) + \nabla f\cdot\mathbf h + o(\|\mathbf h\|)$; guaranteed if the partial derivatives are continuous.
- tangent plane: $z = f(a,b) + f_x(a,b)(x-a) + f_y(a,b)(y-b)$.
- $D_{\mathbf u}f = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$ (with $\mathbf u$ a **unit vector**).
- $\nabla f$: normal to level curves, direction of steepest slope, norm = maximum slope.
- chain rule: $\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t)) = \nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'(t)$.
- In chemistry: $dG = G_T\,dT + G_P\,dP$ — same structure, different notation.

</KeyResults>
