## Intuition

Imagine the surface $z = f(x,y)$ as a relief. At a point where the tangent plane is horizontal — a **critical point** — three landscapes are possible:

- a **bowl**: the surface rises in all directions (local minimum);
- a **summit**: it goes down in all directions (local maximum);
- a **saddle point**: it rises in certain directions and descends in others, like a mountain pass between two valleys.

To distinguish the three, you have to look at the *curvature* of the surface in each direction: this is exactly the information contained in the second derivatives matrix, the **Hessian**.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- critical point: $\nabla f(a,b)=0$.
- Hessian: $H_f=(f_{ij})$; discriminant $D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2$.
- $D>0$ classifies a minimum or maximum according to $f_{xx}$; $D<0$ classifies a saddle; $D=0$ does not conclude.

</KeyResults>

<Collapsible title="The rigorous version — definitions and conditions">

## Formal definition

### critical point

$(a,b)$ is a **critical point** of $f$ if

$$
\nabla f(a,b) = \mathbf 0
\qquad\text{c'est-à-dire}\qquad
f_x(a,b) = 0 \ \text{ et }\ f_y(a,b) = 0 .
$$

Any local extremum *interior* of a differentiable function is a critical point — but the converse is false (saddles are critical without being extrema).

### Hessian and discriminant

<Rappel title="second derivatives">

$f_{xx}$ measures how the slope along $x$ varies along $x$; $f_{xy}$ derives first along $x$, then along $y$. For a $C^2$ function, the Clairaut's theorem gives $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$.

</Rappel>

$$
H_f(a,b) =
\begin{pmatrix}
f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\
f_{yx} & f_{yy}
\end{pmatrix}(a,b),
\qquad
D = f_{xx}\,f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2 .
$$

($D$ is the determinant of Hessian; for $f$ of class $C^2$, Clairaut's theorem gives $f_{xy} = f_{yx}$.)

<KeyResults title="Discriminant test (second derivative test)">

In a critical point $(a,b)$, with $D = f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2$ evaluated at this point:

1. $D > 0$ and $f_{xx} > 0$: **local minimum**;
2. $D > 0$ and $f_{xx} < 0$: **local maximum**;
3. $D < 0$: **saddle point** (neither max nor min);
4. $D = 0$: **the test does not conclude** — a direct study is required (sign of $f - f(a,b)$ in the neighborhood, well-chosen paths, etc.).

</KeyResults>

In terms of eigenvalues: $D = \lambda_1\lambda_2$. Two strictly positive eigenvalues give a minimum, two negatives a maximum, two of opposite signs give a saddle.

### Global extrema on a compact domain

On a **closed and bounded** domain $K$, a continuous function always reaches its maximum and its minimum (reached bounds theorem). The procedure:

1. **Interior**: search for critical points ($\nabla f = 0$) and calculate $f$ in each;
2. **Edge**: parameterize each edge curve, study the function of a variable obtained (zero derivative + ends);
3. **Corners**: evaluate $f$ in each corner of the domain;
4. **Compare** all candidate values: the largest is global maximum, the smallest is global minimum.

**Mini-example.** $f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2 - 2x$ on disk $x^2+y^2 \le 4$. Interior: $\nabla f = (2x-2,\ 2y) = 0$ gives $(1,0)$, with $f(1,0) = -1$. $x^2+y^2 = 4$ edge: there, $f = 4 - 2x$ with $x \in [-2, 2]$, therefore extreme values $f = 8$ in $(-2,0)$ and $f = 0$ in $(2,0)$. Conclusion: global minimum $-1$ at $(1,0)$, global maximum $8$ at $(-2,0)$.

</Collapsible>

## Worked example

Let's classify all critical points from $f(x,y) = x^3 - 3xy + y^3$.

**Step 1 — critical points.**

$$
f_x = 3x^2 - 3y = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad y = x^2,
\qquad
f_y = -3x + 3y^2 = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = y^2 .
$$

By substituting: $x = (x^2)^2 = x^4$, therefore $x(x^3 - 1) = 0$, or $x = 0$ or $x = 1$. The critical points are $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$.

**Step 2 — Hessian.**

$$
f_{xx} = 6x, \qquad f_{yy} = 6y, \qquad f_{xy} = -3,
\qquad
D = 36xy - 9 .
$$

**Step 3 — classification.**

- In $(0,0)$: $D = -9 < 0$ → **saddle point**.
- In $(1,1)$: $D = 36 - 9 = 27 > 0$ and $f_{xx} = 6 > 0$ → **local minimum**, with value $f(1,1) = 1 - 3 + 1 = -1$.

**Step 4 — verification.** Near the origin, on axis $y = 0$: $f = x^3$ changes sign — the origin is neither a max nor a min, consistent with the saddle. And $f(1,1) = -1$ is much lower than $f$ at nearby points $f(1, 0.9) \approx -0.971$… no, let's calculate: $1 - 2.7 + 0.729 = -0.971 > -1$. Consistent.

Warning: this minimum is *local* only — $f(x, 0) = x^3 \to -\infty$ when $x \to -\infty$, so $f$ has no global minimum on $\mathbb R^2$.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — why the sign of D classifies critical points">

In a critical point, the Taylor formula at order 2 gives, for small $\mathbf h = (h, k)$:

$$
f(a+h,\ b+k) - f(a,b)
= \tfrac12\, Q(h,k) + o(\|\mathbf h\|^2),
\qquad
Q(h,k) = f_{xx}h^2 + 2f_{xy}hk + f_{yy}k^2 .
$$

The local sign of $f - f(a,b)$ is therefore dictated by the quadratic form $Q$. Let's assume $f_{xx} \neq 0$ and complete the square:

$$
Q(h,k) = f_{xx}\left(h + \frac{f_{xy}}{f_{xx}}\,k\right)^{\!2}
+ \frac{f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2}{f_{xx}}\,k^2
= f_{xx}\left(h + \frac{f_{xy}}{f_{xx}}\,k\right)^{\!2}
+ \frac{D}{f_{xx}}\,k^2 .
$$

- If $D > 0$: both coefficients have the sign of $f_{xx}$. The form $Q$ is defined (positive if $f_{xx} > 0$, negative if $f_{xx} < 0$): it only cancels in $(0,0)$, and the term $o(\|\mathbf h\|^2)$ cannot reverse its sign. Hence a minimum or a strict local maximum.
- If $D < 0$: the two coefficients have opposite signs. $Q$ takes strictly positive values in one direction and strictly negative in another: it is a saddle.
- If $D = 0$: $Q$ cancels on an entire direction, and it is the remainder $o(\|\mathbf h\|^2)$ — invisible at order 2 — which decides. The test is silent.

Finally, if $f_{xx} = 0$ with $D < 0$ (therefore $f_{xy} \neq 0$), the crossed term $2f_{xy}hk$ changes sign between the directions $h = k$ and $h = -k$: another saddle. In matrix language: $D = \det H = \lambda_1\lambda_2$ and $f_{xx}$ sets the common sign when $D > 0$. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Extrema subject to a constraint — Lagrange multipliers <ProvenanceBadge provenance="extension" />

Finding the extrema of $f$ *along a curve* $g(x,y) = c$ (and no longer over the entire plane) is a different problem: the gradient of $f$ no longer needs to cancel, it must only no longer have a component *along the constraint*.

<KeyResults title="Lagrange condition">

At an extremum of $f$ on the regular curve $g(x,y) = c$ (with $\nabla g \neq 0$), there exists a real $\lambda$ — the **Lagrange multiplier** — such that

$$
\nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g .
$$

Geometrically: the level curve of $f$ and the stress curve are **tangent** at the optimal point — their normals (the two gradients) are collinear.

</KeyResults>

**Express example.** Extrema of $f = xy$ on the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 2$. The $\nabla f = \lambda \nabla g$ system is written as $y = 2\lambda x$, $x = 2\lambda y$, with $x^2 + y^2 = 2$. By multiplying: $xy = 4\lambda^2 xy$, and combining we find $y = \pm x$, therefore the points $(\pm 1, \pm 1)$. Values: $f = 1$ at $(1,1)$ and $(-1,-1)$ (maximum), $f = -1$ at $(1,-1)$ and $(-1,1)$ (minimum).

## Link with chemistry

Near an equilibrium geometry $\mathbf q_0$, the potential energy of a molecule grows into

$$
V(\mathbf q_0 + \mathbf h)
\approx V(\mathbf q_0) + \tfrac12\,\mathbf h^\top H_V(\mathbf q_0)\,\mathbf h,
$$

the term of gradient being zero by definition of equilibrium. The eigenvalues of the Hessian $H_V$ are the “stiffness constants” of the natural vibration directions:

- **all positive** → stable bowl: it is a *minimum*, a stable molecule or conformer;
- **exactly one negative** → **first-order saddle**: it is a **transition state**, the crossing point of a reaction. The negative-eigenvalue direction is the reaction coordinate;
- several negatives → stationary point of higher order, without direct kinetic role.

This is why quantum chemistry software ends each optimization with a frequency calculation: checking the signs of the eigenvalues of the Hessian, exactly our test of the discriminant in higher dimensions.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Conclude with $f_{xx}$ alone, without calculating $D$.** The sign of $f_{xx}$ only classifies *after* checking $D > 0$. For $f = x^2 + 4xy + y^2$ originally: $f_{xx} = 2 > 0$ but $D = 4 - 16 = -12 < 0$ — it's a saddle, not a minimum.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believe that every critical point is an extremum.** A saddle is critical ($\nabla f = 0$) while being neither a maximum nor a minimum. Conversely, on a domain with an edge, a global extremum can be found *on the edge* without the gradient canceling there.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Force a conclusion when $D = 0$.** The test is then silent: $x^4 + y^4$, $x^4 - y^4$ and $-x^4 - y^4$ all have the same zero Hessian at the origin, but are respectively a minimum, a saddle and a maximum. Only a direct study of the sign of $f$ settles the question (this is exercise 02 of TD 4).

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the edge and the corners in a problem of global extrema.** The interior critical points are never enough: on a compact domain, the maximum or minimum is very often hidden on the edge — in the mini-example of the disk, the maximum $8$ is at the edge, far from the only interior critical point.

</Pitfall>

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- critical point: $\nabla f = 0$ — *necessary* condition for an interior extremum.
- Hessian $H_f$, discriminating $D = f_{xx}f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2$ from critical point.
- $D > 0$, $f_{xx} > 0$: local minimum; $D > 0$, $f_{xx} < 0$: local maximum; $D < 0$: saddle point; $D = 0$: inconclusive test; study the function directly.
- Global extrema on a compact: interior + edge + corners, then compare.
- Lagrange (extension): on $g = c$, extremum ⇒ $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$ (level curves tangents to the constraint).
- Chemistry: $V \approx V_0 + \tfrac12\mathbf h^\top H\mathbf h$; minimum = stable species, first-order saddle = transition state.

</KeyResults>
