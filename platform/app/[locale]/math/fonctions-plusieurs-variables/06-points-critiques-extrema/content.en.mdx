## The battle plan

To honour the contract, we need three tools:

1. **locate** every position where no first-order slope remains;
2. **classify** each one by its curvatures: stable shape, summit or passage;
3. **secure** the conclusion when the domain has a boundary or imposes a constraint.

Each tool will be applied to the same energy $E(q_1,q_2)$ as in L02.

## 1. Locating every candidate position

**Tool 1 — locate.** Let's make "lower than the neighbouring positions" precise, then turn that infinite comparison into equations.

Problem: a stable shape must be lower than the nearby shapes. We need a word for this property.

**Definition 1 (local extremum).** A <Def id="extremum-local">**local extremum**</Def> is a minimum or maximum relative to a neighbourhood. $(a,b)$ is a local minimum if there exists $r>0$ such that

$$
f(a,b)\le f(x,y)\quad\text{dès que}\quad(x-a)^2+(y-b)^2<r^2.
$$

For a maximum, reverse the inequality; the extremum is **strict** if the inequality is strict away from $(a,b)$.

**Example (chemistry) 1.** Since $E$ is a sum of squares, $E\ge0$. It equals $0$ at $(-1,0)$ and $(1,0)$: no position of the landscape is lower.

**Remark.** A function can have a local minimum and take far lower values away from it; Example 9 will show this.

Problem: comparing all the neighbours is impracticable. In L04, the
<Terme id="gradient">gradient</Terme> gathered the
<Terme id="derivee-partielle">partial derivatives</Terme>, the slopes coordinate by coordinate.
We need to name the points where they vanish together.

**Definition 2 (critical point).** A <Def id="point-critique">**critical point**</Def> (locally
flat terrain, hence a candidate) is a point $(a,b)$ such that

$$
\nabla f(a,b)=\mathbf0,\qquad\text{soit}\qquad f_x(a,b)=f_y(a,b)=0.
$$

**Example (chemistry) 2.** For the chapter's landscape,

$$
E_{q_1}=4q_1(q_1^2-1),\qquad E_{q_2}=2q_2.
$$

The second forces $q_2=0$; the first, $q_1=-1$, $0$ or $1$. The three critical points are
$(-1,0)$, $(0,0)$ and $(1,0)$.

**Remark.** The origin is critical for $f=x^2-y^2$, yet $f(x,0)>0$ and $f(0,y)<0$ away from the
origin: it is neither a minimum nor a maximum. "Critical" means candidate, not elected.

**Proposition 1 (necessary condition in the interior).** If $f$ has
<Terme id="differentiabilite">differentiability</Terme> at an interior local extremum, then that
extremum is a critical point.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — why the slopes must vanish">

If $(a,b)$ is an interior local minimum, $\varphi(t)=f(a+t,b)$ has a local minimum at $0$, so
$\varphi'(0)=f_x(a,b)=0$. With $\psi(t)=f(a,b+t)$, we get $f_y(a,b)=0$, then
$\nabla f(a,b)=\mathbf0$.

For a maximum, apply this reasoning to $-f$. "Interior" is essential: on the boundary, a
direction may be forbidden. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## 2. Deciding what happens at each candidate

**Tool 2 — classify.** The equations give the candidates; we must know whether the energy rises
everywhere around them or changes behaviour with the direction.

Problem: the origin of $x^2-y^2$ rises along one axis and falls along the other. We need a name for
this precise type of critical point.

**Definition 3 (saddle point).** A <Def id="point-selle">**saddle point, or mountain pass**</Def> (the passage
between two valleys) is a critical point surrounded by values both larger and smaller.

**Example 3.** For $f=x^2-y^2$, the origin is critical. Yet $f(x,0)=x^2>0$ and
$f(0,y)=-y^2<0$: it is a saddle point.

**Remark (chemistry).** A saddle point descending toward two stable shapes represents a
**transition state**: stable transversally to the path, unstable along the transformation.

Problem: trying a few axes can miss an oblique direction. We need an object that
gathers all the second-order curvatures.

**Definition 4 (Hessian).** The <Def id="hessienne">**Hessian matrix**</Def> (the matrix of
second derivatives, hence of curvatures) of $f$ at $(a,b)$ is

$$
H_f(a,b)=\begin{pmatrix}f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\f_{yx}&f_{yy}\end{pmatrix}(a,b).
$$

**Example (chemistry) 4.** For $E(q_1,q_2)$,

$$
H_E(q_1,q_2)=\begin{pmatrix}12q_1^2-4&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$

At the shapes $(\pm1,0)$, the diagonal curvatures are $8$ and $2$; at the passage $(0,0)$, $-4$ and $2$.
The computation contrasts "rises in two directions" with "rises in one, falls in the other".

**Remark.** If $f$ is $C^2$, Schwarz's theorem gives $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$: the Hessian is
symmetric.

**Method 1 (classifying by the Hessian discriminant).** At each critical point, compute

$$
D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2=\det H_f,
$$

then apply, in this order:

1. $D>0$ and $f_{xx}>0$: strict local minimum;
2. $D>0$ and $f_{xx}<0$: strict local maximum;
3. $D<0$: saddle point;
4. $D=0$: the test is inconclusive; study the sign of $f-f(a,b)$ directly.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Candidates: $\nabla f(a,b)=\mathbf0$.
- Curvatures: $H_f=(f_{ij})$.
- Discriminant: $D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2$.
- Reflex: $D$ first; if $D>0$, then look at the sign of $f_{xx}$.

</KeyResults>

**Proposition 2 (second-derivative test).** For $f$ of class $C^2$ near a critical point,
Method 1 concludes if $D\ne0$; if $D=0$, order two is silent.

With the <Terme id="valeur-propre">eigenvalues</Terme> $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ of the Hessian,
$D=\lambda_1\lambda_2$: two positive ones give a minimum, two negative ones a maximum, and opposite
signs a saddle point.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — why the sign of D classifies">

At the critical point $(a,b)$, second-order Taylor gives, for small $\mathbf h=(h,k)$,

$$
f(a+h,b+k)-f(a,b)=\frac12Q(h,k)+o(\|\mathbf h\|^2),
$$

with

$$
Q(h,k)=f_{xx}h^2+2f_{xy}hk+f_{yy}k^2.
$$

If $f_{xx}\ne0$, let's complete the square:

$$
Q(h,k)=f_{xx}\left(h+\frac{f_{xy}}{f_{xx}}k\right)^2+\frac{D}{f_{xx}}k^2.
$$

If $D>0$, the coefficients have the sign of $f_{xx}$: $Q$ keeps that sign near the point. If $D<0$,
they have opposite signs: $Q$ takes both signs. If $D=0$, $Q$ vanishes in one direction;
the higher orders decide.

Finally, if $f_{xx}=0$ and $D<0$, then $f_{xy}\ne0$; the term $2f_{xy}hk$ changes sign between
$h=k$ and $h=-k$. We again get a saddle point. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Remark (chemical notation and molecular reading).** The $q_i$ denote internal coordinates (angles, torsions, bond lengths). Near a critical geometry $\mathbf q_0$,

$$
E(\mathbf q_0+\mathbf h)\approx E(\mathbf q_0)+\frac12\mathbf h^\top H_E(\mathbf q_0)\mathbf h.
$$

Positive eigenvalues describe a stable species; exactly one negative eigenvalue describes a
first-order transition state and its direction gives the reaction coordinate. In quantum chemistry
computation, an optimisation is therefore followed by a frequency calculation to control the curvatures.

## 3. Securing a global or constrained search

**Tool 3 — secure.** A local classification is not enough to look for the best value over a
whole experimental domain or among the allowed geometries only.

Problem: a local minimum only compares neighbours. We must define "lowest over the whole domain".

**Definition 5 (global extremum).** A **global minimum** of $f$ on $K$ satisfies $f(A)\le f(X)$ for
every $X\in K$; a global maximum reverses the inequality.

**Example (chemistry) 5.** On $K=[-1,1]\times[-1,1]$, $E$ reaches $0$ at $(\pm1,0)$ and $2$ at
$(0,\pm1)$: these are its global minimal and maximal values on this window.

**Remark.** An interior global extremum is local; the converse is false.

Problem: on an open or infinite domain, the best value may never be attained.
We need a condition guaranteeing that both extremes exist.

**Definition 6 (compact domain in the plane).** In $\mathbb R^2$, a **compact** domain is
closed — boundary included — and bounded — contained in a disc of finite radius.

**Example 6.** The disc $x^2+y^2\le4$ and the rectangle $[-1,1]^2$ are compact; the disc
$x^2+y^2<4$ is not, because its boundary is missing.

**Proposition 3 (bounds attained).** Every continuous function on a compact set attains its global extrema.

**Method 2 (interior, boundary, corners).** On a compact set:

1. look for the critical points in the interior and compute their values;
2. parametrise each piece of the boundary and study the resulting one-variable function;
3. evaluate the corners and endpoints;
4. compare all the candidate values.

**Example 7.** For $f=x^2+y^2-2x$ on $x^2+y^2\le4$, the interior gives $(1,0)$ and the value $-1$.
On the boundary, $f=4-2x$ with $x\in[-2,2]$, hence from $0$ at $(2,0)$ to $8$ at $(-2,0)$. The global
minimum is $-1$ and the maximum $8$.

### Extension — imposing a relation between the variables <ProvenanceBadge provenance="extension" />

Problem: under $g(x,y)=c$, the gradient of $f$ no longer has to vanish; only the variation along
the curve disappears. We must respect this constraint.

**Method 3 (Lagrange multipliers).** At an extremum of $f$ on $g(x,y)=c$, with
$\nabla g\ne\mathbf0$, there exists a **Lagrange multiplier** $\lambda$ such that

$$
\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g,\qquad g(x,y)=c.
$$

The <Terme id="ligne-de-niveau">level curves</Terme> of $f$ and the constraint are then
tangent: their normal gradients are collinear.

**Example 8.** To find the extrema of $f(x,y)=xy$ on $x^2+y^2=2$, the system is

$$
y=2\lambda x,\qquad x=2\lambda y,\qquad x^2+y^2=2.
$$

It gives $y=\pm x$, then $(\pm1,\pm1)$. The maximum is $1$ when the signs coincide; the
minimum is $-1$ when they are opposite.

## Worked example — classifying completely without confusing local and global

**Example 9.** Let's classify all the critical points of $f(x,y)=x^3-3xy+y^3$.

**Step 1 — translate "candidate" into two equations.**

$$
f_x=3x^2-3y=0,\qquad f_y=-3x+3y^2=0.
$$

*Why?* Definition 2 provides an exhaustive list of the interior candidates.

**Step 2 — solve without losing any solution.** We have $y=x^2$ and $x=y^2$, so
$x=x^4$ then $x(x^3-1)=0$. The points are $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$.

*Why?* The classification is only valid if every solution of the system has been kept.

**Step 3 — compute the curvatures only once.**

$$
f_{xx}=6x,\qquad f_{yy}=6y,\qquad f_{xy}=-3,\qquad D=36xy-9.
$$

*Why?* A general formula avoids redoing the differentiations at each candidate.

**Step 4 — apply the test in the right order.** At $(0,0)$, $D=-9$: it is a saddle point.
At $(1,1)$, $D=27>0$ then $f_{xx}=6>0$: it is a strict local minimum, of value $-1$.

*Why?* Method 1 requires $D$ before $f_{xx}$; this avoids mistaking a passage for a minimum.

**Step 5 — check the local meaning and the global reach.** On $y=0$, $f=x^3$ changes sign near
the origin. Moreover, $f(1,0{,}9)=-0{,}971>-1$, but $f(x,0)\to-\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$.

*Why?* Definition 5 prevents turning a local minimum into a global minimum.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

Why was the positive sign of $f_{xx}(1,1)$ not enough to conclude?

It only describes one direction; the cross term can create a descending one. Here $D>0$ controls them all.

</Collapsible>

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Concluding with $f_{xx}$ alone.** For $f=x^2+4xy+y^2$ at the origin,
$f_{xx}=2>0$ but $D=4-16=-12<0$: it is a saddle point, not a minimum.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believing every critical point is an extremum.** A saddle point does satisfy $\nabla f=\mathbf0$
without being a minimum or a maximum. Conversely, an extremum sitting on the boundary can have a
non-zero gradient, because some directions are forbidden.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forcing a conclusion when $D=0$.** The functions $x^4+y^4$, $x^4-y^4$ and
$-x^4-y^4$ have the same zero Hessian at the origin, yet give respectively a minimum, a saddle
point and a maximum. You must study the sign directly.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the boundary and the corners.** On a compact set, the interior critical points are not enough:
in Example 7, the maximum $8$ sits on the boundary of the disc.

</Pitfall>

**1. Locate.** Definition 2 gives $\nabla E=(4q_1(q_1^2-1),2q_2)=\mathbf0$, hence exactly

the minimal passage is therefore $(0,0)$ and the barrier is $1$ eV. The contract is fulfilled.

## Lesson summary

You now have the three capabilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Locate:** solve $f_x=0$ and $f_y=0$ simultaneously.
- **Classify:** compute $D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2$, then apply Method 1.
- **Handle the silent case:** if $D=0$, compare $f-f(a,b)$ directly along several paths.
- **Secure on a compact set:** interior, boundary, corners, then comparison of all the values.
- **Respect a constraint:** solve $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$ with $g=c$.
- **Read the chemistry:** positive curvatures = stable shape; one negative sign = transition state.

</KeyResults>
