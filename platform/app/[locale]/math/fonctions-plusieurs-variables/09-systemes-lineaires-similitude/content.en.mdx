## Intuition

The $X' = AX$ system says: “Velocity is a linear function of position”. The $A$ matrix deforms the plane, and the trajectories follow this deformation. The miracle is that there almost always exist **privileged directions** — the eigenvectors — along which the dynamics are radically simple: $x' = \lambda x$, pure exponential growth or decay. Everything else is obtained by superposition.

Changing the base ($A \mapsto P^{-1}AP$, **similar** matrices) does not change the dynamic, only the coordinate frame in which we view it. The real characteristics of a portrait are the similarity invariants: the eigenvalues — or, equivalently in dimension 2, the **trace** and the **determinant**.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- If $Av=\lambda v$, then $e^{\lambda t}v$ solves $X'=AX$.
- Case diagonalizable: $X(t)=c_1e^{\lambda_1t}v_1+c_2e^{\lambda_2t}v_2$.
- In dimension 2, $\lambda^2-\tau\lambda+\Delta=0$ with $\tau=\operatorname{tr}A$ and $\Delta=\det A$.

</KeyResults>

<Collapsible title="The rigorous version — definitions and conditions">

<Rappel title="Find a eigenvalue">

eigenvalues cancels $\det(A-\lambda I)$. For each, a non-zero eigenvector solves $(A-\lambda I)v=0$; two independent eigenvectors form the base which diagonalizes $A$.

</Rappel>

## Formal definition

### Eigenmode solutions and general solution

For $X' = AX$ with $A$ a constant $2\times2$ matrix: if $Av = \lambda v$ with $v \neq 0$, then

$$
X(t) = e^{\lambda t}\,v
$$

is a solution (immediate check: $X' = \lambda e^{\lambda t}v = A X$).

<KeyResults title="general solution (case diagonalizable)">

If $A$ has two eigenpairs $(\lambda_1, v_1)$ and $(\lambda_2, v_2)$ with independent $v_1, v_2$, the general solution is

$$
X(t) = c_1\,e^{\lambda_1 t}\,v_1 + c_2\,e^{\lambda_2 t}\,v_2,
$$

the constants $c_1, c_2$ being fixed by the initial condition (decompose $X(0)$ on the basis $(v_1, v_2)$).

</KeyResults>

### Diagonalization: the change of basis which decouples

If $A = PDP^{-1}$ with diagonal $D$ (columns of $P$ = eigenvectors), let's set $Y = P^{-1}X$. SO

$$
Y' = P^{-1}X' = P^{-1}AX = P^{-1}(PDP^{-1})X = DY :
$$

in the eigenbasis coordinates, the system is **decoupled** into two scalar equations $y_i' = \lambda_i y_i$, solved in one line. Solving a linear system means finding the right coordinate frame.

For **complex** eigenvalues $\lambda = a \pm ib$, real solutions combine $e^{at}\cos(bt)$ And $e^{at}\sin(bt)$ : rotation at frequency $b$, modulated by the exponential $e^{at}$ — spirals.

### Jordan block and matrix exponential

When $\lambda$ is a eigenvalue **double** with a single eigenvector (non-diagonalizable matrix, called *defective*), the model is the Jordan block, whose exponential is calculated exactly:

$$
\exp\!\left(t\begin{pmatrix} \lambda & 1 \\ 0 & \lambda \end{pmatrix}\right)
= e^{\lambda t}
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The **polynomial** factor $t$ next to the exponential is the signature of defective matrices. More generally, the solution to $X' = AX$, $X(0) = X_0$, is always written $X(t) = e^{tA}X_0$ where $e^{tA} = \sum_{n\ge0} (tA)^n/n!$ — exercise 05 of TD 6 calculates this series in full on a fundamental case.

### Trace–determinant classification

Let's put $\tau = \operatorname{tr} A$, $\Delta = \det A$. eigenvalues are the roots of $\lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta = 0$, of discriminant $\mathcal D = \tau^2 - 4\Delta$.

<KeyResults title="The portrait only depends on (τ, Δ)">

- $\Delta < 0$: **saddle** (real eigenvalues of opposite signs) — always unstable;
- $\Delta > 0$, $\mathcal D > 0$, $\tau < 0$: **stable node**;
- $\Delta > 0$, $\mathcal D > 0$, $\tau > 0$: **unstable node**;
- $\Delta > 0$, $\mathcal D < 0$, $\tau < 0$: **stable focus** (converging spiral);
- $\Delta > 0$, $\mathcal D < 0$, $\tau > 0$: **unstable focus**;
- $\tau = 0$, $\Delta > 0$ (therefore $\mathcal D < 0$): **linear center** (closed elliptical orbits);
- $\mathcal D = 0$ (double eigenvalue): examine diagonalizability — star node if $A = \lambda I$, degenerate (defective) node otherwise;
- $\Delta = 0$: a zero eigenvalue — non-isolated equilibria (a whole line).

asymptotic stability ⟺ $\tau < 0$ **and** $\Delta > 0$ (the two strictly negative real parts).

</KeyResults>

### Affine systems

For $X' = AX + b$ with invertible $A$: the equilibrium is $X_* = -A^{-1}b$, and the change $U = X - X_*$ returns to the linear case $U' = AU$. The portrait is that of $A$, simply **translated** to $X_*$.

</Collapsible>

## Worked example

Let's solve

$$
X' = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} X,
\qquad
X(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Step 1 — invariants.** $\tau = 2$, $\Delta = 1 - 4 = -3 < 0$: this is a **saddle**, before any calculation.

**Step 2 — eigenvalues.** $\lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 3 = 0$, i.e. $(\lambda - 3)(\lambda + 1) = 0$: $\lambda_1 = 3$, $\lambda_2 = -1$.

**Step 3 — eigenvectors.** For $\lambda_1 = 3$: $(A - 3I)v = 0$ gives $-2v_x + 2v_y = 0$, or $v_1 = (1, 1)$. For $\lambda_2 = -1$: $2v_x + 2v_y = 0$, or $v_2 = (1, -1)$.

**Step 4 — initial condition.** We are looking for $c_1, c_2$ such as $c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 = (2, 0)$: the system $c_1 + c_2 = 2$, $c_1 - c_2 = 0$ gives $c_1 = c_2 = 1$.

**Step 5 — solution and reading.**

$$
X(t) = e^{3t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}
+ e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Verification at $t = 0$: $(1,1) + (1,-1) = (2,0)$. ✔ When $t \to +\infty$, the term $e^{3t}$ dominates: the trajectory aligns with the unstable direction $(1,1)$. When $t \to -\infty$, it comes from the stable direction $(1,-1)$. This is the geometry of the saddle: two invariant straight lines $y = \pm x$, and all the other trajectories lie on hyperbolas guided by them.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — the exponential of Jordan block">

Let's write the block as $tB = t(\lambda I + N)$ with $N = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which checks for $N^2 = 0$ (nilpotent).

**Key: $I$ and $N$ commute** (the identity commutes with everything), so the exponential factors:

$$
e^{tB} = e^{\lambda t I}\,e^{tN} = e^{\lambda t}\,e^{tN}.
$$

And the $e^{tN}$ series comes to an abrupt end thanks to $N^2 = 0$:

$$
e^{tN} = I + tN + \frac{t^2N^2}{2!} + \cdots = I + tN
= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Hence $e^{tB} = e^{\lambda t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. The solution to $X' = BX$, $X(0) = (a, b)$, is therefore $X(t) = e^{\lambda t}(a + bt,\ b)$: the exponential carries a polynomial factor in $t$ — exactly what we will observe in TD 6, exercise 02. (Note: $e^{M+N} = e^M e^N$ **requires** $MN = NM$; this is false for arbitrary matrices.) $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Proof — why (τ, Δ) is enough: invariance by similarity">

If $B = P^{-1}AP$ (**similar** matrices: same linear map seen in two bases), then

$$
\det(B - \lambda I) = \det\bigl(P^{-1}(A - \lambda I)P\bigr)
= \det(A - \lambda I) :
$$

same characteristic polynomial, therefore same eigenvalues, same trace ($\tau = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$) and same determinant ($\Delta = \lambda_1\lambda_2$).

Furthermore, if $Y = P^{-1}X$ transforms the solutions of $X' = AX$ into those of $Y' = BY$, the two phase portraits are identical up to one (linear) reference change. The **similarity classes** are therefore the real objects of the classification, and $(\tau, \Delta)$ — supplemented by the diagonalizability test when $\mathcal D = 0$ — identifies them in dimension 2. This is the deep meaning of the trace–determinant plane. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Link with chemistry

The sequential kinetics $A \xrightarrow{k_1} B \xrightarrow{k_2} C$ is written matrixly, for the concentrations $(a, b)$:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix}'
= \begin{pmatrix} -k_1 & 0 \\ k_1 & -k_2 \end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} a \\ b \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The matrix is **triangular**: its eigenvalues are read on the diagonal, $-k_1$ and $-k_2$. Each eigenvalue is a **time scale** of the kinetics: $1/k_1$ for the disappearance of $A$, $1/k_2$ for the emptying of $B$. The regime observed depends on their ratio — if $k_2 \gg k_1$, the intermediate $B$ remains in the minority (quasi-stationary state); if $k_1 \gg k_2$, $B$ accumulates before slowly draining. Exercise 04 of TD 6 solves this entire system and calculates the moment of the maximum of $B$ — an absolute classic in Math 250 partials and kinetics courses.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Write the solution without eigenvectors.** $X(t) = c_1e^{\lambda_1t} + c_2e^{\lambda_2t}$ means nothing for a vector: each exponential must multiply *its* eigenvector, $X(t) = c_1e^{\lambda_1t}v_1 + c_2e^{\lambda_2t}v_2$. Directions are part of the answer.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Conclude stability with the sole determinant.** $\Delta > 0$ is not enough: we also need $\tau < 0$. The matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\Delta = 2 > 0$… and explodes (unstable node, $\tau = 3 > 0$).

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Treat a double eigenvalue as two distinct eigenvalues.** If $\mathcal D = 0$, everything depends on the diagonalizability: $\lambda I$ gives a star node, a Jordan block gives the factor $t\,e^{\lambda t}$ and a degenerate node. Reflex: count the independent eigenvectors.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Use $e^{A+B} = e^Ae^B$ without checking commutativity.** This identity requires $AB = BA$. It holds for $\lambda I + N$ (the identity commutes with everything) — this is what makes Jordan's calculation possible — but it is false in general.

</Pitfall>

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- $Av = \lambda v$ ⟹ $e^{\lambda t}v$ is solution of $X' = AX$; case diagonalizable: $X = c_1e^{\lambda_1t}v_1 + c_2e^{\lambda_2t}v_2$.
- diagonalization $A = PDP^{-1}$: change $Y = P^{-1}X$ decouples the system ($Y' = DY$).
- Jordan block: $e^{tB} = e^{\lambda t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ — polynomial factor $t$, signature of the defective case.
- Classification by $\tau = \operatorname{tr}A$, $\Delta = \det A$, $\mathcal D = \tau^2 - 4\Delta$: neck ($\Delta < 0$), nodes ($\mathcal D > 0$), foci ($\mathcal D < 0$), center ($\tau = 0$, $\Delta > 0$); stability ⟺ $\tau < 0$ and $\Delta > 0$.
- Similar matrices = same invariants = same portrait up to change of basis: the classification is a classification by **similarity**.
- For $X' = AX + b$: equilibria $X_* = -A^{-1}b$, then $U = X - X_*$.
- Chemistry: $A \to B \to C$ → triangular matrix, eigenvalues $-k_1, -k_2$ = inverses of time scales.

</KeyResults>
