# Predict the rates and fate of coupled kinetics

<LessonMeta id="math-fmv-c09" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

Two rates hidden in four coefficients are enough to predict whether concentrations decay, rotate, or run away.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="A→B→C kinetics — measure the formation and then the disappearance of B">

**Situation.** One mole per litre of A is converted into B, then B into C, with $k_1=0{,}10$ s⁻¹, $k_2=1{,}0$ s⁻¹, and no B initially.
**Precise question.** What are the two characteristic times, when does B reach its maximum, and what concentration does it reach?
**Obstacle.** In L1, you solved one evolution at a time; here B depends on what A supplies to it and what it loses to C.
**Contract.** In this chapter: 3 tools (isolate the simple rates, recombine them, then predict the overall shape). By the end, we will solve this problem, with numbers to back it up.

</Mission>

*Feeling confident? [Go straight to the TD (tutorial session)](../td-6/) — the theory will still be here when you get stuck.*

## The battle plan

To fulfil the contract, we need three tools:

1. **isolate** the directions in which a coupled evolution becomes a simple decay or growth again;
2. **recombine** these evolutions to satisfy the initial quantities, even when a rate is repeated;
3. **classify** the shape of every path from two numbers, without solving everything.

Each tool returns to the kinetics $A\to B\to C$ and explains the linear motions predicted by the <Terme id="linearisation">linearisation</Terme> from lesson 7.

## 1. Isolate the simple rates hidden in the coupling

**Tool 1 — isolate.** Let us translate the two balance equations into a matrix, then look for the directions it does not deflect.

Problem: two quantities evolve together, but their coefficients remain constant. We need notation that exposes this coupling.

**Definition 1 (linear system).** A <Terme id="systeme-differentiel">system of ODEs</Terme> is **linear with constant coefficients** when $X(t)\in\mathbb R^2$ satisfies

$$
X'=AX,\qquad A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix},
$$

with a matrix $A$ that is independent of time and of $X$.

**Example (chemistry) 1.** For the concentrations $a(t)$ of A and $b(t)$ of B, the balance equations give

$$
a'=-k_1a,\qquad b'=k_1a-k_2b,
$$

that is, $X'=MX$ with $X=(a,b)^\top$ and $M=\begin{pmatrix}-k_1&0\\k_1&-k_2\end{pmatrix}$.

**Remark (chemical notation).** A denotes a species here, whereas $A$ often denotes a matrix. For the running example, the matrix is therefore $M$; $k_1$ and $k_2$ are expressed in s⁻¹.

Problem: in most directions, a matrix rotates and stretches at the same time. We need to name the directions in which it only stretches, and their factor.

**Definition 2 (eigenvector and eigenvalue).** A nonzero vector $v$ is an <Def id="vecteur-propre">**eigenvector**</Def> of $A$ (a direction that is stretched without being rotated) if there is a $\lambda$ such that $Av=\lambda v$.
This number is the associated <Def id="valeur-propre">**eigenvalue**</Def> (the stretching factor); the sign of its real part determines growth or decay.

**Example (chemistry) 2.** For $k_1=0{,}10$ and $k_2=1{,}0$,

$$
M\begin{pmatrix}1\\1/9\end{pmatrix}=-0{,}10\begin{pmatrix}1\\1/9\end{pmatrix},
\qquad M\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}=-1{,}0\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The reciprocals of their absolute values, $10$ s and $1$ s, are the **timescales**: the times after which each factor $e^{-kt}$ equals $e^{-1}$.

**Remark.** $(1,0)$ is not an eigenvector, because $M(1,0)^\top=(-0{,}10,0{,}10)^\top$ is not parallel to it.

Problem: testing directions at random would take forever. We need an equation that gives every possible factor.

**Definition 3 (characteristic polynomial).** The <Def id="polynome-caracteristique">**characteristic polynomial**</Def> of $A$ is

$$
\chi_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I).
$$

Its roots are exactly the eigenvalues of $A$.

**Example (chemistry) 3.** The triangular matrix in the running example gives

$$
\chi_M(\lambda)=(-k_1-\lambda)(-k_2-\lambda),
$$

so the roots are $-k_1=-0{,}10$ s⁻¹ and $-k_2=-1{,}0$ s⁻¹.

**Proposition 1 (eigenvector solution).** If $Av=\lambda v$, then $X(t)=e^{\lambda t}v$ solves $X'=AX$.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — an eigenvector direction remains an eigenvector direction">

We have $X'=\lambda e^{\lambda t}v$ and $AX=e^{\lambda t}Av=\lambda e^{\lambda t}v$.
The <Terme id="trajectoire">trajectory</Terme> remains on the line spanned by $v$; only its length changes. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## 2. Recombine the evolutions and handle the repeated case

**Tool 2 — recombine.** The simple directions are known; let us reconstruct the solution that starts from the concentrations prepared in the laboratory.

Problem: a single eigenvector direction cannot represent an arbitrary initial state. We need a coordinate system formed by independent eigenvector directions.

**Definition 4 (diagonalisation).** A matrix $A$ is **diagonalisable** if it has two independent eigenvectors $v_1,v_2$.
With $P=(v_1\ v_2)$ and $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2)$, we have $A=PDP^{-1}$; $Y=P^{-1}X$ transforms $X'=AX$ into $Y'=DY$.

**Example (chemistry) 4.** The vectors from Example 2 are independent and

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1/9\end{pmatrix}-\frac19\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Proposition 1 gives $a(t)=e^{-0{,}10t}$ and $b(t)=\bigl(e^{-0{,}10t}-e^{-t}\bigr)/9$.

**Proposition 2 (general diagonalisable solution).** If $v_1,v_2$ form an eigenvector basis, then

$$
X(t)=c_1e^{\lambda_1t}v_1+c_2e^{\lambda_2t}v_2,
$$

where $c_1,c_2$ are fixed by $X(0)=c_1v_1+c_2v_2$.

**Method 1 (solve by diagonalisation).**

1. calculate $\chi_A$ and its roots;
2. solve $(A-\lambda_iI)v_i=0$;
3. check that the vectors are independent;
4. decompose $X(0)$ in this basis;
5. attach $e^{\lambda_it}$ to each direction.

Problem: two different matrices can describe the same evolution in two coordinate systems. We need to recognise this change of coordinates.

**Definition 5 (similarity).** Two matrices $A$ and $B$ are **similar** if there is an invertible $P$ such that $B=P^{-1}AP$; the **similarity class** of $A$ contains all its representations in different bases.

**Example 5.** A diagonalisable matrix $A=PDP^{-1}$ is similar to $D$; in the eigenvector coordinate system, the coupled equations become $y_i'=\lambda_i y_i$.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — what similarity preserves">

If $B=P^{-1}AP$, then

$$
\det(B-\lambda I)=\det\!\bigl(P^{-1}(A-\lambda I)P\bigr)=\det(A-\lambda I).
$$

The two matrices have the same polynomial and the same eigenvalues. Moreover, $Y=P^{-1}X$ transforms solutions of $X'=AX$ into solutions of $Y'=BY$: only the coordinate systems differ. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

Problem: a double eigenvalue may provide only one eigenvector direction. We need a formula that remains valid without diagonalisation.

**Definition 6 (exponential and Jordan block).** The **matrix exponential** is

$$
e^{tA}=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(tA)^n}{n!},
$$

and $X(t)=e^{tA}X_0$ always solves $X'=AX$, $X(0)=X_0$. With a double value $\lambda$ and only one eigenvector direction, $J=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}$ is a **Jordan block**.

**Example 6.** For this block,

$$
e^{tJ}=e^{\lambda t}\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad X(t)=e^{\lambda t}\begin{pmatrix}a+bt\\b\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The polynomial factor $t$ signals the absence of a second eigenvector direction.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — the exponential of a Jordan block">

Write $J=\lambda I+N$, where $N=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $N^2=0$. Since $I$ and $N$ commute,

$$
e^{tJ}=e^{\lambda t}e^{tN}=e^{\lambda t}(I+tN).
$$

The series stops because the subsequent powers of $N$ are zero. Be careful:
$e^{A+B}=e^Ae^B$ requires $AB=BA$; this identity is false in general. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Remark (complex values).** For $a\pm ib$, real solutions combine $e^{at}\cos(bt)$ and $e^{at}\sin(bt)$: size and rotation.

<KeyResults title="The formulas to keep in sight">

- $Av=\lambda v$ gives $e^{\lambda t}v$.
- Diagonalisable case: $X=\sum_i c_ie^{\lambda_it}v_i$.
- Any matrix: $X=e^{tA}X_0$.
- Jordan: $e^{tJ}=e^{\lambda t}\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$.

</KeyResults>

## 3. Classify the overall shape without solving

**Tool 3 — classify.** Let us now place each matrix on a map with two coordinates.

Problem: expanding the polynomial every time hides the combinations that control its roots. We need to isolate them.

**Definition 7 (trace, determinant, and discriminant).** For $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$,

$$
\tau=\operatorname{tr}A=a+d,\qquad
\Delta=\det A=ad-bc,\qquad
\mathcal D=\tau^2-4\Delta.
$$

In dimension 2, $\chi_A(\lambda)=\lambda^2-\tau\lambda+\Delta$.

**Example (chemistry) 7.** The running example gives $\tau=-1{,}10$, $\Delta=0{,}10$, and $\mathcal D=0{,}81$: two real values whose sum is negative.

Problem: these numbers must become a map where a position determines a shape. We need a classification coordinate system.

**Definition 8 (trace–determinant plane).** The <Def id="plan-trace-determinant">**trace–determinant plane**</Def>
is the $(\tau,\Delta)$ map that classifies linear <Terme id="portrait-de-phase">phase portraits</Terme>.
The axis $\Delta=0$ and the parabola $\Delta=\tau^2/4$ are its boundaries.

**Example (chemistry) 8.** The point $(-1{,}10,0{,}10)$ from the running example is above the axis, below the parabola, and to the left of $\tau=0$.

Problem: below the parabola, two real factors with the same sign make paths arrive or depart without rotating. We need to name this shape.

**Definition 9 (node).** A <Def id="noeud">**node**</Def> is an <Terme id="point-d-equilibre">equilibrium</Terme> with real eigenvalues of the same sign:
paths converge to it if they are negative or diverge from it if they are positive; the first is <Terme id="stabilite">stable</Terme>, the second unstable.

**Example (chemistry) 9.** The values $-0{,}10$ and $-1{,}0$ make $(a,b)=(0,0)$ a stable node: A and B disappear without oscillating.

**Remark.** $\operatorname{diag}(1,2)$ also gives a node, but an unstable one: real does not mean decaying.

Problem: above the parabola, a complex pair makes paths rotate while changing their size. We need to name this spiral.

**Definition 10 (focus).** A <Def id="foyer">**focus**</Def> is an equilibrium with complex eigenvalues: the trajectories spiral toward it if their real part is negative, and away from it if it is positive.

**Example 10.** $A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&-2\\2&-1\end{pmatrix}$ has the values $-1\pm2i$: its focus is stable, with angular rotation rate $2$ and envelope $e^{-t}$.

**Proposition 3 (trace–determinant classification).**

| Conditions | Shape |
| --- | --- |
| $\Delta<0$ | <Terme id="point-selle">saddle</Terme>: real values of opposite signs, always unstable |
| $\Delta>0$, $\mathcal D>0$, $\tau<0$ | stable node |
| $\Delta>0$, $\mathcal D>0$, $\tau>0$ | unstable node |
| $\Delta>0$, $\mathcal D<0$, $\tau<0$ | stable focus |
| $\Delta>0$, $\mathcal D<0$, $\tau>0$ | unstable focus |
| $\tau=0$, $\Delta>0$ | linear centre: closed elliptical paths |
| $\mathcal D=0$ | repeated value: test diagonalisability |
| $\Delta=0$ | zero eigenvalue: nonisolated equilibria are possible |

Asymptotic stability is equivalent to $\tau<0$ and $\Delta>0$. On $\mathcal D=0$, $A=\lambda I$ gives a star node; a single eigenvector gives a degenerate node with $te^{\lambda t}$.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous extension — shift the equilibrium of an affine system">

For $X'=AX+b$ with $A$ invertible, $X_*=-A^{-1}b$. Setting $U=X-X_*$,
$U'=A(U+X_*)+b=AU$: the portrait of $A$ is merely translated to $X_*$. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Method 2 (classify a linear portrait).**

1. calculate $\tau$, $\Delta$, $\mathcal D$;
2. read the sign of $\Delta$;
3. if $\Delta>0$, read $\mathcal D$, then $\tau$;
4. on a boundary, count the eigenvector directions or look for the equilibria.

## Worked example — measure the accumulation of intermediate B

**Example (chemistry) 11.** Let us solve the mission for $a(0)=1$, $b(0)=0$, $k_1=0{,}10$ s⁻¹, and $k_2=1{,}0$ s⁻¹.

**Step 1 — write the balance equations.**

$$
X'=MX,\qquad M=\begin{pmatrix}-0{,}10&0\\0{,}10&-1{,}0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

*Why?* Definition 1 brings the supply and consumption of B together in the same calculation.

**Step 2 — isolate the rates.** Definition 3 gives
$\chi_M(\lambda)=(-0{,}10-\lambda)(-1{,}0-\lambda)$, so $-0{,}10$ and $-1{,}0$ s⁻¹.

*Why?* Their reciprocals already answer part of the mission: $10$ s for A, $1$ s for B.

**Step 3 — impose the initial state.**

$$
\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1/9\end{pmatrix}-\frac19\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
a(t)=e^{-0{,}10t},\quad b(t)=\frac{e^{-0{,}10t}-e^{-t}}9.
$$

*Why?* Proposition 2 fixes the coefficients for the actual preparation, not for an abstract state.

**Step 4 — locate the maximum.** At the peak, $b'=0$, so $b=0{,}10a$ and

$$
t_{\max}=\frac{\ln(k_2/k_1)}{k_2-k_1}=\frac{\ln10}{0{,}90}\approx2{,}56\ \mathrm s.
$$

*Why?* Equality between supply and drainage answers “when?” without reading a plot.

**Step 5 — calculate the height.** At this time,
$a\approx e^{-0{,}256}\approx0{,}774$, so
$b_{\max}=0{,}10a\approx0{,}0774$ mol·L⁻¹.

*Why?* B reaches only about $7{,}7\,\%$ of the initial A: it drains ten times faster than it is supplied.

**Step 6 — check the shape.** Here $\tau=-1{,}10$, $\Delta=0{,}10$, and $\mathcal D=0{,}81$.

*Why?* Proposition 3 confirms a stable node, consistent with the calculated disappearance of A and B.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

Why is $t_{\max}\approx2{,}56$ s equal to neither $1$ s nor $10$ s?

The maximum occurs when supply and drainage are equal; it therefore depends on both constants,
through $\ln(k_2/k_1)/(k_2-k_1)$, and not on a single timescale.

</Collapsible>

**Example 12 — your turn, with less help.** For $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}$,
$X(0)=(2,0)^\top$, we find $\tau=2$, $\Delta=-3$, and the values $3,-1$. **Your turn:** find the eigenvectors, impose $X(0)$, and describe the two asymptotic directions.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

$v_1=(1,1)$, $v_2=(1,-1)$, and $X(0)=v_1+v_2$, so $X(t)=e^{3t}(1,1)^\top+e^{-t}(1,-1)^\top$.
The dominant directions are $(1,1)$ toward $+\infty$ and $(1,-1)$ toward $-\infty$; the saddle has these invariant lines and hyperbolas guided by them. This example prepares exercises 01 and 03 from TD 6.

</Collapsible>

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Forget the directions.** $c_1e^{\lambda_1t}+c_2e^{\lambda_2t}$ is a scalar; you need
$X=c_1e^{\lambda_1t}v_1+c_2e^{\lambda_2t}v_2$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Conclude from the determinant alone.** $\Delta>0$ rules out a saddle, but you still need $\mathcal D$ to distinguish a node from a focus, then $\tau$ to distinguish stable from unstable.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Treat a repeated value like two simple values.** On $\mathcal D=0$, count the eigenvectors: only one requires $te^{\lambda t}$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Separate exponentials without commutation.** $e^{A+B}=e^Ae^B$ requires $AB=BA$; it works for $\lambda I+N$, not in general.

</Pitfall>

## Lesson summary

You now have the three abilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Isolate:** solve $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$, then $(A-\lambda I)v=0$.
- **Recombine:** write $X=\sum_i c_ie^{\lambda_it}v_i$ and impose $X(0)$.
- **Handle the exception:** use $X=e^{tA}X_0$; Jordan adds $te^{\lambda t}$.
- **Classify:** calculate $\tau$, $\Delta$, $\mathcal D$ and apply Proposition 3.
- **Change coordinates:** similar matrices keep the same rates and the same portrait up to deformation.
- **Interpret in chemistry:** here the times are $10$ s and $1$ s; B peaks at $0{,}0774$ mol·L⁻¹ around $2{,}56$ s.

</KeyResults>
