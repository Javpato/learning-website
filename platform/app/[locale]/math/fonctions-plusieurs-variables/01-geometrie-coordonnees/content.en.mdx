## Intuition

A point in space has no natural “address”: Cartesian, cylindrical and spherical are three ways of naming it. Each system answers a different question:

- **Cartesian** $(x, y, z)$: “how many steps in each fixed direction?” » — perfect for plans and networks.
- **Cylindrical** $(r, \theta, z)$: “at what distance from the axis, at what angle, at what height?” » — perfect for anything with an axis.
- **Spherical** $(r, \theta, \varphi)$: “how far from the center, at what latitude and longitude?” » — perfect for anything with a center.

The good system is the one where the *constant coordinate surfaces* match the geometry of the problem: a cylinder is simply “$r = R$” in cylindrical, a sphere is “$r = R$” in spherical.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- Line: $\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf r_0+t\mathbf u$; plan: $\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)=0$.
- Polar: $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$.
- Spherical: $dV=r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi$.

</KeyResults>

## Formal definition

### Vectors, lines and planes

For two points $A, B \in \mathbb R^3$, the vector that joins them is $\overrightarrow{AB} = B - A$.

A **parametric line** passing through $\mathbf r_0$ with direction vector $\mathbf u$ is written

$$
\ell:\quad \mathbf r(t) = \mathbf r_0 + t\,\mathbf u,
\qquad t \in \mathbb R .
$$

A **plane** of normal vector $\mathbf n = (a, b, c)$ passing through $P_0 = (x_0, y_0, z_0)$ is

$$
\mathbf n \cdot (\mathbf r - \mathbf r_0) = 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
a(x - x_0) + b(y - y_0) + c(z - z_0) = 0 .
$$

The **distance from a point to a plane** $ax + by + cz + d = 0$ is, for $P = (x_0, y_0, z_0)$:

$$
d(P, \Pi) = \frac{|a x_0 + b y_0 + c z_0 + d|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} .
$$

### Polar coordinates (plan)

$$
x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad r \ge 0 .
$$

The vectors of the local basis rotate with the point:

$$
\mathbf e_r = (\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta),
\qquad
\mathbf e_\theta = (-\sin\theta,\ \cos\theta).
$$

### Cylindrical coordinates

$$
x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad z = z .
$$

Constant coordinate surfaces:

- $r = R$: $Oz$ axis circular cylinder;
- $z = z_0$: horizontal plane;
- $\theta = \theta_0$: vertical half-plane leaning against the $Oz$ axis.

### Spherical coordinates — physicists' convention

$$
x = r\sin\theta\cos\varphi, \qquad
y = r\sin\theta\sin\varphi, \qquad
z = r\cos\theta,
$$

with $r \ge 0$, the **colatitude** $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ measured from the axis $Oz$, and the **azimuth** $\varphi \in [0, 2\pi)$. (Mathematicians often interchange the names $\theta$ and $\varphi$ — always check the convention before using a formula.) Constant coordinate surfaces:

- $r = R$: sphere centered at the origin;
- $\theta = \theta_0$: half-angle cone $\theta_0$ around $Oz$;
- $\varphi = \varphi_0$: half-plane containing axis $Oz$.

The azimuth $\varphi$ of the sphericals coincides with the angle $\theta$ of the cylindricals: it is the same “angle of longitude”.

### Differential elements

<Rappel title="An angle is not a length">

On a circle of radius $r$, a small angle $d\theta$ sweeps the arc $r\,d\theta$. On a parallel of radius $r\sin\theta$, $d\varphi$ sweeps $r\sin\theta\,d\varphi$.

</Rappel>

These geometric factors are essential in electromagnetism. The three **local lengths** in spherical form:

$$
d\ell_r = dr,
\qquad
d\ell_\theta = r\,d\theta,
\qquad
d\ell_\varphi = r\sin\theta\,d\varphi .
$$

surface elements and volume:

$$
dS_{\text{polaire}} = r\,dr\,d\theta,
\qquad
dV_{\text{cyl}} = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz,
\qquad
dV_{\text{sph}} = r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi .
$$

<KeyResults title="The factor to never forget">

An angle is not a length: a movement of $d\theta$ sweeps a length $r\,d\theta$, and a movement of $d\varphi$ sweeps $r\sin\theta\,d\varphi$ (the radius of the parallel is $r\sin\theta$, not $r$). This is where the $r$ for the cylindrical ones and the $r^2\sin\theta$ for the spherical ones come from. Writing $dV = dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi$ is *the error* that causes electrostatic integrals to be missed.

</KeyResults>

</Collapsible>

## Worked example

### A. Plane and distance

Let's write the plane $\Pi$ with normal $\mathbf n = (1, 2, -2)$ passing through $P_0 = (1, 0, 3)$, then the distance from $P = (2, 1, -1)$ to this plane.

**Step 1 — plane equation.**

$$
1\,(x - 1) + 2\,(y - 0) - 2\,(z - 3) = 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
x + 2y - 2z + 5 = 0 .
$$

**Step 2 — distance.** With $a = 1$, $b = 2$, $c = -2$, $d = 5$:

$$
d(P, \Pi) = \frac{|2 + 2\cdot1 - 2\cdot(-1) + 5|}{\sqrt{1 + 4 + 4}}
= \frac{|11|}{3} = \frac{11}{3} .
$$

**Check.** $P_0$ satisfies the equation: $1 + 0 - 6 + 5 = 0$. And the normal has norm one up to a factor of $3$ — hence the denominator.

### B. The same point in the three systems

Let's convert $M = (-1,\ 1,\ \sqrt6)$.

**Step 1 — cylindrical.** $r = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt2$. For the angle: $\arctan(y/x) = \arctan(-1) = -\pi/4$… but $x$ is negative and $y$ positive — second quadrant, we adjust by $+\pi$:

$$
\theta = -\frac{\pi}{4} + \pi = \frac{3\pi}{4},
\qquad
M = \left(\sqrt2,\ \tfrac{3\pi}{4},\ \sqrt6\right)_{\text{cyl}} .
$$

**Step 2 — spherical.** $r = \sqrt{1 + 1 + 6} = 2\sqrt2$. The colatitude comes from $z = r\cos\theta$:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\sqrt6}{2\sqrt2} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\theta = \frac{\pi}{6},
$$

and azimuth is the cylindrical angle: $\varphi = 3\pi/4$. So $M = \bigl(2\sqrt2,\ \tfrac{\pi}{6},\ \tfrac{3\pi}{4}\bigr)_{\text{sph}}$.

**Check.** $x = 2\sqrt2\,\sin\frac{\pi}{6}\cos\frac{3\pi}{4} = 2\sqrt2\cdot\frac12\cdot\left(-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) = -1$ — consistent; likewise $y = 1$ and $z = 2\sqrt2\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \sqrt6$.

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — the formula for point-to-plane distance">

Let $\Pi$ be the plane $ax + by + cz + d = 0$, with normal $\mathbf n = (a, b, c)$, and $P$ a point. Let us take any point $Q \in \Pi$. The distance of $P$ to the plane is the length of the projection of $\overrightarrow{QP}$ on the normal direction:

$$
d(P, \Pi)
= \left|\overrightarrow{QP}\cdot\frac{\mathbf n}{\|\mathbf n\|}\right|
= \frac{|\mathbf n\cdot P - \mathbf n\cdot Q|}{\|\mathbf n\|} .
$$

But $Q \in \Pi$ means $\mathbf n\cdot Q = -d$, so

$$
d(P, \Pi) = \frac{|a x_0 + b y_0 + c z_0 + d|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} .
\qquad\blacksquare
$$

The result does not depend on the choice of $Q$ — the proof shows it: $\mathbf n\cdot Q$ equals $-d$ for *any* point on the plane.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — where r²sinθ comes from">

Let's start from a point $(r, \theta, \varphi)$ and vary each coordinate by a small amount, the other two being frozen:

- $r \to r + dr$: radial displacement of length $dr$;
- $\theta \to \theta + d\theta$: arc of meridian, of radius $r$, therefore of length $r\,d\theta$;
- $\varphi \to \varphi + d\varphi$: parallel arc. The radius of the parallel is the distance to the axis $Oz$, that is to say $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = r\sin\theta$ — hence a length $r\sin\theta\,d\varphi$.

These three displacements are two by two orthogonal (this can be verified by deriving $(x, y, z)$ with respect to $r$, $\theta$, $\varphi$ and by taking the dot products). The small swept area is therefore an almost rectangular box, with a volume equal to the product of its three sides:

$$
dV = dr \cdot r\,d\theta \cdot r\sin\theta\,d\varphi
= r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi .
\qquad\blacksquare
$$

Exercise M1.4 of TD 1 takes up this construction in detail and compares it to the calculation of the Jacobian determinant.

</Collapsible>

## Link with chemistry

The choice of coordinates *encodes the symmetry* of the chemical system:

|Chemical system|Symmetry|Adapted contact details|
| --- | --- | --- |
|Isolated atom or ion|spherical|spherical|
|Filled polymer, “infinite” wire|axial|cylindrical|
|Membrane, flat interface|planar|Cartesian|
|Molecular dipole (far field)|quasi-spherical|spherical|
|Reaction surface $E(x, y)$|no a priori|Cartesian or normal modes|

It is this table which will guide the entire electrostatics course: the potential of an ion is calculated in spherical form, that of DNA modeled as a charged wire is calculated in cylindrical form — and the orbital $1s$ of the hydrogen atom is normalized with $dV = r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi$.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Use $\arctan(y/x)$ without correcting the quadrant.** $\arctan$ only returns angles in the interval $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$: for any point with negative $x$, add $\pm\pi$. Safe recipe: place the point on a mini-diagram, calculate the reference angle, then adjust. Example: $(-\sqrt3, 1)$ gives $\theta = 5\pi/6$, not $-\pi/6$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Treat $\mathbf e_r$ and $\mathbf e_\theta$ as constant vectors.** They *rotate with the point*: $\mathbf e_r = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ depends on $\theta$. We cannot take them outside a derivative or an integral as we would with $\mathbf e_x$ — this is the origin of the terms in $\dot\theta$ in polar accelerations.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forget the factor $r$ or $r^2\sin\theta$.** The volume element is never $dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi$: angles must be converted to lengths. Quick check by the units: $dV$ must be have the dimensions of volume, and $dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi$ only has the dimension of a length.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confuse direction vector and normal vector.** A line is given by a *direction* vector (along which we advance); a plane by a *normal* vector (perpendicular to it). In the equation $ax + by + cz + d = 0$, the triple $(a, b, c)$ is a **normal** — not a direction contained in the plane.

</Pitfall>

    <QFeedback of="a">

    </QFeedback>
    <QFeedback of="b">

      Yes: reference angle $\pi/4$, third quadrant (negative $x$ and $y$), therefore $\theta = \pi/4 + \pi = 5\pi/4$, or $-3\pi/4$ modulo $2\pi$.

    </QFeedback>
    <QFeedback of="c">

      $3\pi/4$ corresponds to the second quadrant, i.e. point $(-1, 1)$. Here $y$ is negative too: third quadrant.

    </QFeedback>
    <QExplain>

      Systematic verification: $r = \sqrt2$ and $\sqrt2\,(\cos\frac{5\pi}{4}, \sin\frac{5\pi}{4}) = (-1, -1)$. The mini-point diagram avoids mistakes every time.

    </QExplain>

  </QItem>
  <QItem>

    What is volume element in spherical coordinates?

    <QOption id="a">$dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi$</QOption>
    <QOption id="b">$r\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi$</QOption>
    <QOption id="c" correct>$r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi$</QOption>
    <QFeedback of="a">

      This product does not even have the dimension of a volume: an angle is not a length. Each $d\theta$, $d\varphi$ must be multiplied by its radius.

    </QFeedback>
    <QFeedback of="b">

      $r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$ is the *cylindrical* element. In spherical form, the two angles each bring a factor: $r$ for $\theta$ and $r\sin\theta$ for $\varphi$.

    </QFeedback>
    <QFeedback of="c">

      Exact: product of the three local lengths $dr \times r\,d\theta \times r\sin\theta\,d\varphi$.

    </QFeedback>

      The local lengths $d\ell_r = dr$, $d\ell_\theta = r\,d\theta$, $d\ell_\varphi = r\sin\theta\,d\varphi$ are orthogonal: their product gives $dV$. It is the factor that normalizes all atomic orbitals.

    </QExplain>

  </QItem>

With the bisector on $x$, the hydrogens are $H_\pm=(\ell\cos(\theta/2),\,\pm\ell\sin(\theta/2),\,0)$: the symmetry appears without parasitic calculation. Around an isolated atom, the sphericals follow the central symmetry; around an axis, the cylindrical ones take over. coordinate system therefore becomes a chemical decision before being a integration technique.

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- Line: $\mathbf r(t) = \mathbf r_0 + t\,\mathbf u$ (director vector). Plan: $\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r - \mathbf r_0) = 0$ (**normal** vector).
- point-to-plane distance: $d = |a x_0 + b y_0 + c z_0 + d|\,/\,\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}$.
- Polar/cylindrical: $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ ($+\ z$); spherical (physics): $x = r\sin\theta\cos\varphi$, $y = r\sin\theta\sin\varphi$, $z = r\cos\theta$, with $\theta$ = colatitude.
- polar angle: correct $\arctan(y/x)$ according to the quadrant, systematically.
- differential elements: $dS = r\,dr\,d\theta$, $dV_{\text{cyl}} = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$, $dV_{\text{sph}} = r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi$.
- The coordinate system is chosen according to **symmetry**: atom → spherical, wire → cylindrical, membrane → Cartesian.

</KeyResults>
