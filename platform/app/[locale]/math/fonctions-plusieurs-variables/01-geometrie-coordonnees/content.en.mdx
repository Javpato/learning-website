# Describing a molecule in the frame that simplifies the calculations

<LessonMeta id="math-fmv-c01" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

The shape of a molecule becomes computable as soon as its symmetries are translated into simple addresses for its atoms.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="The water molecule — recovering its shape and preparing the calculation around the atom">

**Situation.** In a water molecule, each O–H bond measures $95{,}8$ pm and the two bonds
form an angle of $104{,}5^\circ$; O sits at the centre, their bisector runs along the horizontal and the
molecule lies in the plane of the table. **Precise question.** What are the three numerical
addresses of the two H atoms, what distance separates them, and how should space be carved up if the
electron's presence depends only on its distance to O? **Obstacle.** The tools from L1 describe a
curve well, but not several directions, nor a shape that turns without changing. **Contract.** In this
chapter: 3 tools (translating a shape into equations, adapting the addresses to its symmetry, measuring
a small piece of space). At the end, we solve this problem, numbers in hand.

</Mission>

## The battle plan

To honour the contract, we need three tools:

1. **translate** alignments, planes and distances into equations;
2. **adapt** a point's address to a planar shape, an axis or a centre;
3. **measure** the small lengths, areas and volumes in each description.

Each tool will be applied to the water molecule, then reused in TD 1.

## 1. Translating a molecular geometry into equations

**Tool 1 — translate.** Let's go from a drawing of a molecule to computable points, directions, planes and distances.

Problem: saying that H is "to the right and up" allows no calculation. So we need to measure the displacement between two points.

**Definition 1 (vector between two points).** In a Cartesian frame, that is, three fixed perpendicular axes, the vector from $A=(x_A,y_A,z_A)$ to $B=(x_B,y_B,z_B)$ and its length are

$$
\overrightarrow{AB}=B-A=(x_B-x_A,y_B-y_A,z_B-z_A),
\qquad
AB=\|\overrightarrow{AB}\|.
$$

**Example (chemistry) 1.** Write $\ell$ for the O–H length and $\alpha$ for the H–O–H angle. With $O=(0,0,0)$, the bisector along $x$ and the molecule in $z=0$,

$$
H_\pm=\left(\ell\cos\frac{\alpha}{2},\ \pm\ell\sin\frac{\alpha}{2},\ 0\right).
$$

The opposite signs encode the reflection with respect to $x$. **Remark.** The norm of each of the two vectors $\overrightarrow{OH_\pm}$ is indeed $\ell$: the formula respects the imposed lengths.

Problem: an ideal bond, or the intersection of two planar surfaces, contains infinitely many aligned points. So we need to advance from a point in a chosen direction.

**Definition 2 (parametric line).** The line through $\mathbf r_0$ with nonzero direction vector $\mathbf u$ is

$$
\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf r_0+t\mathbf u,\qquad t\in\mathbb R.
$$

**Example 2.** The O–$H_+$ axis reads $\mathbf r(t)=t(\cos(\alpha/2),\sin(\alpha/2),0)$. For $t=\ell$, we recover $H_+$. **Remark.** A direction vector follows the line; it does not indicate what is perpendicular to it.

Problem: saying that all the nuclei lie in one same sheet requires an equation shared by that surface. So we need a direction perpendicular to the sheet.

**Definition 3 (plane).** The plane through $P_0=(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ with normal vector $\mathbf n=(a,b,c)\ne\mathbf0$ is given by

$$
\mathbf n\cdot(P-P_0)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
a(x-x_0)+b(y-y_0)+c(z-z_0)=0.
$$

**Example (chemistry) 3.** The plane of the water molecule is $z=0$, with normal $(0,0,1)$. O and the two points $H_\pm$ of Example 1 satisfy its equation. **Remark.** In $ax+by+cz+d=0$, the triple $(a,b,c)$ is a normal, not a direction of the plane.

Problem: a nucleus may lie outside a reference molecular plane. So we need to measure only its perpendicular offset from the plane.

**Proposition 1 (distance from a point to a plane).** For $\Pi:ax+by+cz+d=0$ and $P=(x_P,y_P,z_P)$,

$$
d(P,\Pi)=\frac{|ax_P+by_P+cz_P+d|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}.
$$

**Example 4.** For $\Pi:x+2y-2z+5=0$ and $P=(2,1,-1)$, we find $d(P,\Pi)=11/3$. The denominator removes the effect of the normal's length.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — distance from a point to a plane">

Take $Q\in\Pi$. The distance is the length of the projection of $\overrightarrow{QP}$ onto the normal direction:

$$
d(P,\Pi)
=\left|\overrightarrow{QP}\cdot\frac{\mathbf n}{\|\mathbf n\|}\right|
=\frac{|\mathbf n\cdot P-\mathbf n\cdot Q|}{\|\mathbf n\|}.
$$

Now $\mathbf n\cdot Q=-d$ for every $Q\in\Pi$. We obtain Proposition 1, independently of the chosen point $Q$. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Method 1 (going from the drawing to the equations).** Choose the origin and the axes to bring out the symmetry; write a point and the useful directions; apply Definition 2 to an alignment, Definition 3 to a planar surface, then check a length or a distance.

## 2. Adapting a point's address to the symmetry

**Tool 2 — adapt.** Fixed axes suit a plane; a distance and angles describe an axis or a centre more directly.

Problem: in the plane, a point turning around O keeps its distance to O but changes both of its Cartesian addresses. We need to record that distance and the turned angle directly.

**Definition 4 (polar coordinates).** <Def id="coordonnees-polaires">**Polar coordinates**</Def> locate a point of the plane by its distance $r$ to the origin and its angle $\theta$ from the $x$ axis:

$$
x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad r\ge0.
$$

**Example 5.** For $(-1,1)$, $r=\sqrt2$ and the picture forces the second quadrant: $\theta=3\pi/4$, not $-\pi/4$. **Remark.** At the origin, the angle is undetermined. Elsewhere, $\arctan(y/x)$ alone loses the quadrant; a small sketch restores it.

Problem: around a long object, like a polymer idealised as a wire, the distance to the axis and the height matter separately. So we need to complete the previous description with that height.

**Definition 5 (cylindrical coordinates).** <Def id="coordonnees-cylindriques">**Cylindrical coordinates**</Def> are the plane's polar coordinates completed by the height $z$:

$$
x=r\cos\theta,\qquad y=r\sin\theta,\qquad z=z.
$$

**Example (chemistry) 6.** For a charged polymer modelled by $Oz$, the points at distance $R$ from the wire satisfy $r=R$, instead of $x^2+y^2=R^2$. **Remark.** $r=R$ describes a cylinder, $z=z_0$ a horizontal plane and $\theta=\theta_0$ a vertical half-plane resting on $Oz$.

Problem: around an isolated atom, no direction is privileged and only the distance to the nucleus can govern a property. We need a distance to the centre and two angles.

**Definition 6 (spherical coordinates).** <Def id="coordonnees-spheriques">**Spherical coordinates**</Def> locate a point by its distance to the centre $\rho$ and two angles; they are suited to atoms and to orbitals with central symmetry:

$$
x=\rho\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\qquad
y=\rho\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\qquad
z=\rho\cos\theta,
$$

with $\rho\ge0$, the colatitude $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ measured from $Oz$ and the azimuth $\varphi\in[0,2\pi)$ measured around $Oz$.

**Example (chemistry) 7.** A $1s$ orbital, that is, a model of electronic presence around an atom, depends only on $\rho$. Its surfaces of equal value are therefore spheres centred on the nucleus.

**Remark (physics convention).** Some books swap $\theta$ and $\varphi$. Here $\theta$ starts from $Oz$ and $\varphi$ turns around that axis; this azimuth is the polar angle of the cylindrical coordinates. A value $\rho=R$ describes a sphere, $\theta=\theta_0$ a cone and $\varphi=\varphi_0$ a half-plane containing $Oz$.

**Method 2 (choose, then convert).** Spot what does not change: a plane suggests fixed axes, a rotation around an axis the cylindrical description, a rotation around a centre the spherical description. Compute the distances, place the angles by quadrant, then rebuild $x,y,z$.

| Chemical system | Useful invariance | Suited description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| water molecule or planar membrane | fixed plane | Cartesian |
| polymer or charged wire | rotation around an axis | cylindrical |
| atom, ion or $1s$ orbital | rotation around the nucleus | spherical |
| molecular dipole seen from afar | centre and privileged direction | spherical |

## 3. Measuring a small piece of space without forgetting the angles

**Tool 3 — measure.** Changing a point's address also changes the real size of a small step in each direction.

Problem: an angle is not a length. So we need to convert each small rotation into an arc before computing an area or a volume.

**Definition 7 (local lengths).** The local lengths are the distances travelled when a single coordinate varies while the others stay fixed. In the plane,

$$
\mathbf e_r=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\qquad
\mathbf e_\theta=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta),
$$

and the small lengths are $dr$ and $r\,d\theta$. Both vectors turn with the point.

**Example 8.** On a circle of radius $3$ cm, a rotation of $0{,}01$ rad sweeps $3\times0{,}01=0{,}03$ cm: the angle must be multiplied by the radius.

In spherical coordinates, the three local lengths are

$$
d\ell_\rho=d\rho,\qquad
d\ell_\theta=\rho\,d\theta,\qquad
d\ell_\varphi=\rho\sin\theta\,d\varphi.
$$

**Proposition 2 (surface and volume elements).** The product of the orthogonal local lengths gives

$$
dS_{\text{polaire}}=r\,dr\,d\theta,\qquad
dV_{\text{cyl}}=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz,\qquad
dV_{\text{sph}}=\rho^2\sin\theta\,d\rho\,d\theta\,d\varphi.
$$

**Example (chemistry) 9.** To normalise a $1s$ orbital around O, its presence must be added up over all of space. Even if the formula depends only on $\rho$, each shell receives the factor $\rho^2\sin\theta$: faraway shells contain more volume.

**Remark (chemical notation).** In quantum chemistry, $d\tau$ often denotes the small volume of space. Here $d\tau=dV_{\text{sph}}$: same object, different outfit.

<KeyResults title="The formulas to keep in sight">

- Line: $\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf r_0+t\mathbf u$; plane: $\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)=0$.
- Polar: $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$.
- Spherical: $x=\rho\sin\theta\cos\varphi$, $y=\rho\sin\theta\sin\varphi$, $z=\rho\cos\theta$.
- Spherical volume: $dV=\rho^2\sin\theta\,d\rho\,d\theta\,d\varphi$.

</KeyResults>

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — origin of the factor rho² sin(theta)">

Varying one coordinate at a time gives the three edges $d\rho$, $\rho\,d\theta$ and $\rho\sin\theta\,d\varphi$. The last one is an arc on a parallel of radius $\rho\sin\theta$, not $\rho$. These directions are pairwise perpendicular, as the dot products of the derivatives of $(x,y,z)$ confirm.

$$
dV=d\rho\cdot\rho\,d\theta\cdot\rho\sin\theta\,d\varphi
=\rho^2\sin\theta\,d\rho\,d\theta\,d\varphi.
\qquad\blacksquare
$$

The same factor is the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant, the number that measures the local dilation of volumes. TD 1.4 takes up this construction again.

</Collapsible>

## Worked example — converting a point without losing its quadrant

**Example 10.** Let's convert $M=(-1,1,\sqrt6)$ to cylindrical then spherical coordinates.

**Step 1 — compute the distance to the axis.** $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=\sqrt2$.

*Why?* Definition 5 separates the distance to the axis from the height.

**Step 2 — place the angle.** The projection $(-1,1)$ lies in the second quadrant, so $\theta=3\pi/4$.

*Why?* The geometric check of Method 2 avoids the raw arctangent result $-\pi/4$.

**Step 3 — keep the height.**

$$
M=\left(\sqrt2,\frac{3\pi}{4},\sqrt6\right)_{\text{cyl}}.
$$

*Why?* By Definition 5, the cylindrical conversion does not change $z$.

**Step 4 — compute the distance to the centre.** $\rho=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}=2\sqrt2$.

*Why?* Definition 6 replaces the distance to the axis by the distance to the origin.

**Step 5 — determine the two angles.** The azimuth stays $\varphi=3\pi/4$ and $z=\rho\cos\theta$ gives $\cos\theta=\sqrt3/2$, so $\theta=\pi/6$.

*Why?* Reusing the azimuth avoids a second quadrant computation; the vertical equation fixes the colatitude.

**Step 6 — rebuild.**

$$
x=2\sqrt2\sin\frac{\pi}{6}\cos\frac{3\pi}{4}=-1,\qquad
y=1,\qquad z=2\sqrt2\cos\frac{\pi}{6}=\sqrt6.
$$

*Why?* The last step of Method 2 detects a swap of angles before it propagates.

So $M=(2\sqrt2,\pi/6,3\pi/4)_{\text{sph}}$.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

Why is the cylindrical distance $\sqrt2$ while the spherical distance is $2\sqrt2$?

The first measures the distance to $Oz$ and ignores the height; the second measures the distance to the origin and includes $z^2=6$.

</Collapsible>

**Example 11 — your turn, with less help.** The plane $\Pi:x+2y-2z+5=0$ passes through $P_0=(1,0,3)$. Check it. **Your turn:** compute the distance from $P=(2,1,-1)$ to $\Pi$, then explain the denominator.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

$1+0-6+5=0$. Proposition 1 gives $d(P,\Pi)=11/3$. The denominator $\|(1,2,-2)\|=3$ turns the normal into a unit direction: rescaling the plane's equation does not change the distance.

</Collapsible>

## Guided visualisation

No widget here: a dimensioned sketch is enough and prepares the construction of TD 1.5.

**Cycle 1 — rebuilding water.**

- **Predict.** Will the two H atoms have the same $x$ address? What will change for $y$?
- **Act.** Draw O, the horizontal bisector and two segments of $95{,}8$ pm separated by $104{,}5^\circ$; project each H onto the axes.
- **Observe.** Where do the $52{,}25^\circ$ angles appear? Do the vertical projections have the same length and opposite signs?
- **Connect.** Example 1 translates these projections; Definition 1 then measures H–H.

**Cycle 2 — turning around O.**

- **Predict.** Does an electronic presence that depends only on the distance to O change when you turn without moving away?
- **Act.** Draw three circles centred on O as cross-sections of three spherical shells and place several points on one shell.
- **Observe.** Which addresses change on the shell? Which one stays fixed?
- **Connect.** The distance $\rho$ stays fixed: it is the surface $\rho=R$ of Definition 6 and the choice of Example 7.

Then explore freely the drawing of a vertical wire and look for the surfaces at constant distance from the wire.

## Classic pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the quadrant.** For $(-\sqrt3,1)$, the picture forces $5\pi/6$, not $-\pi/6$. Adjust the arctangent with the signs of $x$ and $y$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confusing direction vector and normal.** The first follows a line; the second is perpendicular to a plane.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Freezing the local basis.** $\mathbf e_r$ and $\mathbf e_\theta$ depend on $\theta$ and turn with the point.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Forgetting the geometric factors.** $d\rho\,d\theta\,d\varphi$ does not have the dimension of a volume; $\rho^2\sin\theta$ is missing.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Swapping the spherical angles.** Here $\theta$ starts from $Oz$ and $\varphi$ turns around $Oz$. The identity $z=\rho\cos\theta$ settles it.

</Pitfall>

<MissionSolved>

**1. Placing the hydrogens.** By Definition 1 and Example 1, $\alpha/2=52{,}25^\circ$ and

$$
H_\pm
=\left(95{,}8\cos52{,}25^\circ,\ \pm95{,}8\sin52{,}25^\circ,\ 0\right)
\approx(58{,}7,\ \pm75{,}7,\ 0)\ \text{pm}.
$$

**2. Measuring H–H.** The $x$ and $z$ addresses cancel in the difference:

$$
H_+H_-=2\times95{,}8\sin52{,}25^\circ\approx151{,}5\ \text{pm}.
$$

Definition 3 also confirms that the three nuclei lie in $z=0$.

**3. Preparing the electronic calculation.** Since the $1s$ orbital depends only on the distance to O, Definition 6 selects spherical coordinates. Proposition 2 imposes $d\tau=\rho^2\sin\theta\,d\rho\,d\theta\,d\varphi$. Contract fulfilled: $H_\pm\approx(58{,}7,\pm75{,}7,0)$ pm, H–H $\approx151{,}5$ pm and a carving of space suited to the atom.

</MissionSolved>

## Lesson summary

You now have the three abilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Translate:** write a line $\mathbf r(t)=\mathbf r_0+t\mathbf u$, a plane $\mathbf n\cdot(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_0)=0$ and a point–plane distance.
- **Adapt:** choose fixed axes for a plane, a distance to the axis for a wire, a distance to the centre and two angles for an atom.
- **Convert:** compute the distances, place the angles by quadrant, then rebuild $x,y,z$.
- **Measure:** turn the angles into $r\,d\theta$, $\rho\,d\theta$ and $\rho\sin\theta\,d\varphi$.
- **Integrate:** remember $dS=r\,dr\,d\theta$, $dV_{\text{cyl}}=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$ and $dV_{\text{sph}}=\rho^2\sin\theta\,d\rho\,d\theta\,d\varphi$.
- **Choose in chemistry:** molecular plane to fixed axes, wire to the cylindrical axis, atom or $1s$ orbital to the spherical centre.

</KeyResults>
