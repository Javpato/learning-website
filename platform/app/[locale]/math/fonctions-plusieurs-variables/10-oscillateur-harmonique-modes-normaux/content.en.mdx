## The battle plan

To fulfil the contract, we need three tools:

1. **transform** the curvature of an energy minimum into a vibration rate;
2. **classify** a return with losses, then predict its response to periodic excitation;
3. **separate** several coupled coordinates into independent collective motions.

Each tool returns to the two CO₂ stretching coordinates $q_1,q_2$. The landscape from L02, classified near its minima in L06, will finally start moving.

## 1. Transform the curvature of a minimum into a rate

**Tool 1 — transform.** Let us start from the potential energy, because its shape near equilibrium dictates the force.

Problem: a <Terme id="extremum-local">local minimum</Terme> tells us where a geometry is stable, but not how it reacts to a small displacement. We need a model that directly relates this displacement to the restoring force.

**Definition 1 (harmonic oscillator).** A <Def id="oscillateur-harmonique">**harmonic oscillator**</Def> is a system pulled back toward equilibrium by a force proportional to the displacement. For one coordinate $x$, $mx''=-kx$, that is, $mx''+kx=0$, where $m>0$ measures inertia and $k>0$ stiffness.

**Example (chemistry) 1.** Around an equilibrium length $r_e$, set $x=r-r_e$. The <Terme id="hessienne">Hessian matrix</Terme> of the energy provides the curvature $k$:

We have $E(r_e+x)=E(r_e)+kx^2/2+o(x^2)$, so the force is $-E'(r_e+x)\simeq-kx$. At small amplitude, the bottom of the energy surface studied in L02 and L06 is a molecular spring.

**Remark.** The approximation is local: far from the bottom, higher-order terms deform the parabola and can lead to the dissociation studied in L08.

Problem: the equation contains a mass and a stiffness, but the spectrum requires a single rate. We need to combine these two numbers.

**Definition 2 (natural angular frequency).** The <Def id="pulsation-propre">**natural angular frequency**</Def> is the system's natural angular rate, fixed here by $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$. In spectroscopy, it fixes the position of the band.

**Example (chemistry) 2.** In the reduced units of the running example, a direction with curvature $1$ and mass $1$ gives $\omega_0=1$. The calibration $\Omega_*/(2\pi c)=1330\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ will convert this reduced rate into a spectral position.

**Remark (chemical notation).** The angular frequency $\omega$ is expressed in rad·s⁻¹. Spectroscopists often display the **wavenumber** $\widetilde\nu=\omega/(2\pi c)$ in cm⁻¹; it is the same rate in different clothing, not new dynamics.

**Proposition 1 (motion without loss).** Every solution of $mx''+kx=0$ can be written $x(t)=A\cos(\omega_0t)+B\sin(\omega_0t)=R\cos(\omega_0t-\varphi)$, with $R=\sqrt{A^2+B^2}$. The energy $E=mx'^2/2+kx^2/2$ remains constant.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — solution, system, and energy">

The <Terme id="polynome-caracteristique">characteristic polynomial</Terme> $m\lambda^2+k$ has the two <Terme id="valeur-propre">eigenvalues</Terme> $\lambda=\pm i\omega_0$. Real combinations of the complex exponentials give the sines and cosines in Proposition 1.

Setting $v=x'$, the equation becomes

$$
\begin{pmatrix}x\\v\end{pmatrix}'
=
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-k/m&0\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\v\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its <Terme id="portrait-de-phase">phase portrait</Terme> is a centre: the paths are the constant-energy ellipses of the <Terme id="systeme-conservatif">conservative system</Terme> from L08. Finally, $E'=mx'x''+kxx'=x'(mx''+kx)=0$. The solution therefore does not spontaneously settle at the bottom: it rotates around it. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## 2. Decide whether the return oscillates and how light excites it

**Tool 2 — classify.** Let us add the losses observed in a real medium, then the periodic action of the infrared field.

Problem: the preceding model conserves its energy forever, whereas a real vibration dies down through collisions and energy transfers. We need to represent this loss.

**Definition 3 (damping).** <Def id="amortissement">**Damping**</Def> is an energy loss that makes oscillations decay. The simplest linear model is $mx''+\gamma x'+kx=0$, with $\gamma\ge0$ and a force $-\gamma x'$ opposite to the motion.

**Example (chemistry) 3.** For a CO₂ vibration coordinate in a dissipative environment, $E'=x'(mx''+kx)=-\gamma x'^2\le0$. The energy curve descends toward the bottom; this identity directly prepares exercise 02 from TD 7.

**Remark.** At times when $x'=0$, we have $E'=0$ without all dissipation stopping: the energy decreases over the whole motion whenever $\gamma>0$ and the solution is not identically at rest.

Problem: knowing the sign of $E'$ does not tell us whether the return crosses equilibrium several times. We need a dimensionless number that compares loss, mass, and stiffness.

**Definition 4 (ratio and regimes).** The **damping ratio** $\zeta=\gamma/(2\sqrt{mk})$ compares the losses with the threshold $\gamma=2\sqrt{mk}$. The return is **underdamped** if $\zeta<1$, **critically damped** if $\zeta=1$, and **overdamped** if $\zeta>1$.

**Example 4.** For $x''+4x'+\omega_0^2x=0$, we have $\zeta=2/\omega_0$. Thus $\omega_0=1,2,3$ respectively give an overdamped, critically damped, and underdamped return: this is the classification requested in exercise 01 from TD 7.

**Proposition 2 (the three returns).** Looking for $x=e^{\lambda t}$ gives $m\lambda^2+\gamma\lambda+k=0$ and $\lambda=(-\gamma\pm\sqrt{\gamma^2-4mk})/(2m)$.

We obtain:

- if $\zeta<1$, $\lambda=-\zeta\omega_0\pm i\omega_d$, with $\omega_d=\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$, and $x=e^{-\zeta\omega_0t}\bigl(A\cos(\omega_dt)+B\sin(\omega_dt)\bigr)$;

- if $\zeta=1$, the repeated root $-\omega_0$ gives $x=(A+Bt)e^{-\omega_0t}$;
- if $\zeta>1$, two negative real roots give a sum of two exponentials, without oscillation.

For $\gamma=0$, we recover Proposition 1.

<Collapsible title="Complete calculation preserved — an underdamped return">

**Example 5.**

Let us solve $x''+2x'+5x=0$ with $x(0)=1$ and $x'(0)=0$. Here $\omega_0=\sqrt5$ and $\zeta=1/\sqrt5<1$: Proposition 2 predicts an oscillating return.

The equation $\lambda^2+2\lambda+5=0$ gives $\lambda=-1\pm2i$, so

$$
x(t)=e^{-t}\bigl(A\cos(2t)+B\sin(2t)\bigr).
$$

The first condition fixes $A=1$. Then $x'(0)=-A+2B=0$ fixes $B=1/2$, hence

$$
x(t)=e^{-t}\left(\cos(2t)+\frac12\sin(2t)\right).
$$

The envelope is divided by $e$ per unit time, and the frequency is $2/(2\pi)\simeq0{,}32$ cycle per unit time. Finally, $\tau=-2$, $\Delta=5$, and $\mathcal D=-16$ confirm the stable focus from lesson 09.

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Rigorous connection with lesson 09 — focus, repeated value, node">

With $X=(x,x')^\top$,

$$
X'=AX,\qquad
A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-k/m&-\gamma/m\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its trace is $\tau=-\gamma/m\le0$, its determinant is $\Delta=k/m>0$, and its discriminant is $\mathcal D=\tau^2-4\Delta=(\gamma^2-4mk)/m^2$.

The underdamped case is therefore a stable <Terme id="foyer">focus</Terme>; the overdamped case, a stable <Terme id="noeud">node</Terme>. At the threshold, the factor $t$ is the one from the Jordan block in L09. This matrix interpretation and the scalar equation give exactly the same three regimes.

</Collapsible>

<KeyResults title="The formulas to keep in sight">

- $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$ and $\zeta=\gamma/(2\sqrt{mk})$.
- $\zeta<1$: oscillations inside the envelope $e^{-\zeta\omega_0t}$.
- $\zeta=1$: critical return $(A+Bt)e^{-\omega_0t}$.
- $\zeta>1$: two decaying exponentials, without oscillation.
- $\gamma=0$: constant energy and sinusoidal motion.

</KeyResults>

### Make the bond respond to a wave <ProvenanceBadge provenance="extension" />

Problem: a spectrometer does not merely wait for a molecule to vibrate; its electric field pushes it periodically. We need to predict when this push produces a large response.

**Definition 5 (resonance).** For $mx''+\gamma x'+kx=F_0\cos(\omega t)$, **resonance** is the amplification of the steady state when the imposed angular frequency $\omega$ approaches the natural rate.

**Example (chemistry) 6.** A CO₂ motion that changes its dipole moment is forced by the infrared field. Absorption becomes strong near its natural frequency; a motion that does not change this dipole remains invisible in IR, even though it exists mechanically.

**Proposition 3 (forced amplitude).** The solution is the sum of a damped transient and a steady state with amplitude $A(\omega)=F_0/\sqrt{(k-m\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2}$. The peak is at $\omega_r=\omega_0\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}$ when $\zeta<1/\sqrt2$. It lies slightly below $\omega_0$, and is higher and narrower when $\gamma$ decreases.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — amplitude and phase lag">

Solve in complex form

$$
mz''+\gamma z'+kz=F_0e^{i\omega t}
$$

with $z=\widehat z\,e^{i\omega t}$. After division by $e^{i\omega t}$,

$$
\bigl(k-m\omega^2+i\gamma\omega\bigr)\widehat z=F_0,
\qquad
\widehat z=\frac{F_0}{(k-m\omega^2)+i\gamma\omega}.
$$

The modulus gives the amplitude in Proposition 3, and the argument of the denominator gives the lag

$$
\tan\varphi=\frac{\gamma\omega}{k-m\omega^2}.
$$

Minimising the square of the denominator gives $\omega_r^2=\omega_0^2(1-2\zeta^2)$. If $\gamma\to0$, then $A(\omega_0)\to\infty$ in this ideal linear model: this is the resonance catastrophe. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## 3. Separate the collective motions of CO₂

**Tool 3 — separate.** Let us return to the two bonds: we must find combinations of $q_1,q_2$ that each vibrate at a single rate.

Problem: if $q_1$ drives $q_2$, neither coordinate alone follows an independent equation. We need to name the collective motions that the coupling does not deform.

**Definition 6 (normal mode).** A <Def id="mode-normal">**normal mode**</Def> is a collective motion in which every coordinate oscillates at the same frequency. For $Mq''+Kq=0$, we look for $q(t)=v\cos(\omega t)$, so $(K-\omega^2M)v=0$.

The $\omega^2$ are the generalised eigenvalues; the <Terme id="vecteur-propre">eigenvectors</Terme> $v$ give the shapes of the motions.

**Example (chemistry) 7.** The running example uses $M=I$ and $K=\Omega_*^2C$. Two directions emerge:

$$
C\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}
=1{,}00\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
C\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}
=3{,}12\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

In the first motion, both lengths increase together: it is **symmetric**. In the second, one increases while the other decreases: it is **antisymmetric**.

**Remark.** The eigenvalue is $\omega^2$, not $\omega$. The two rate factors are therefore $1$ and $\sqrt{3{,}12}\simeq1{,}77$.

**Proposition 4 (decoupling).** If $M$ is symmetric positive definite and $K$ is symmetric, the modes can be chosen independently, and every small vibration is a superposition of these motions.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous justification — reduce the problem to lesson 09">

The matrix $M$ has an invertible square root. With $y=M^{1/2}q$,

$$
y''+M^{-1/2}KM^{-1/2}y=0.
$$

The central matrix is symmetric: it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. In each direction $u_j$, the equation becomes

$$
y_j''+\omega_j^2y_j=0.
$$

We return to $q$ through $v_j=M^{-1/2}u_j$. This is the diagonalisation from L09, applied to the curvature of the well and weighted by the masses. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Remark (spectroscopic selection in CO₂).** With our convention, the symmetric motion $(1,1)$ preserves the inversion centre and does not change the dipole: it is inactive in ideal IR, but visible in Raman. The antisymmetric motion $(1,-1)$ changes the dipole and absorbs strongly in IR. The real IR spectrum of CO₂ also contains a bending band around $667\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, outside this model restricted to the two stretching motions.

Problem: isotopic substitution changes the inertias without remaking the electronic landscape. We need the mass that measures the relative motion of two atoms.

**Definition 7 (reduced mass).** For two atoms with masses $m_1,m_2$, the **reduced mass** $\mu=m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)$ is the inertia of their relative displacement. An isolated bond satisfies $\omega=\sqrt{k/\mu}$.

**Example (chemistry) 8.** Replacing H with D leaves $k$ almost unchanged in the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, but nearly doubles $\mu$ when the other atom is heavy. Thus $\omega'/\omega=\sqrt{\mu/\mu'}\simeq1/\sqrt2$. An O–H band around $3600\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ is therefore expected around $3600/\sqrt2\simeq2550\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$, close to the roughly $2600\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ observed for O–D. This prepares exercise 04 from TD 7.

## Worked example — explain the two CO₂ stretching bands

**Example (chemistry) 9.** Let us solve the mission for

$$
C=\begin{pmatrix}2{,}06&-1{,}06\\-1{,}06&2{,}06\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\frac{\Omega_*}{2\pi c}=1330\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}.
$$

**Step 1 — connect the well to the motion.** Near the bottom,

$$
E(q)=E(0)+\frac12\Omega_*^2q^\top Cq,
\qquad
q''+\Omega_*^2Cq=0.
$$

*Why?* Definition 1 turns the quadratic form at the L02/L06 minimum into a vibration equation: spectroscopy begins in the Hessian matrix.

**Step 2 — try the common motion.**

$$
C(1,1)^\top=(1,1)^\top.
$$

*Why?* The symmetry exchanges the two bonds without changing the molecule; it therefore provides a candidate direction that Definition 6 can isolate.

**Step 3 — try the opposite motion.**

$$
C(1,-1)^\top=3{,}12(1,-1)^\top.
$$

*Why?* This second direction is independent of the first; Proposition 4 then guarantees that the two are sufficient to reconstruct every small stretching motion.

**Step 4 — convert the two curvatures into spectral positions.**

$$
\widetilde\nu_s=1330\sqrt{1}=1330\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1},
\qquad
\widetilde\nu_a=1330\sqrt{3{,}12}\simeq2349\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}.
$$

*Why?* The Remark after Example 7 requires taking the square root of the eigenvalues; it provides the numerical answer to “where?”.

**Step 5 — assign the motions.** The first vector gives $q_1=q_2$, the symmetric stretching motion; the second gives $q_1=-q_2$, the antisymmetric stretching motion.

*Why?* The signs of the components describe what the two bonds actually do, instead of leaving the vectors as mere columns of numbers.

**Step 6 — decide what the infrared sees.** The symmetric motion does not create a dipole, whereas the antisymmetric one does: the strong IR stretching band is therefore the one around $2350\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$; the one around $1330\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ appears mainly in Raman.

*Why?* Proposition 3 explains absorption only if the field can force the motion; the mechanical existence of a frequency is not enough to make it IR-active.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

Why does the curvature matrix produce two rates even though its two diagonal coefficients are equal?

The equal diagonal entries make the bonds equivalent, but the off-diagonal coefficient couples them. Moving together and moving in opposite directions engage this coupling differently, and therefore give the values $1$ and $3{,}12$.

</Collapsible>

**Example 10 — your turn, with less help.** Two equal masses satisfy

$$
mx_1''=-(k+\kappa)x_1+\kappa x_2,\qquad
mx_2''=\kappa x_1-(k+\kappa)x_2.
$$

The candidates are $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$. **Your turn:** apply the stiffness matrix to each one, then deduce the two frequencies and decide which one is faster.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

The matrix maps $(1,1)$ to $k(1,1)$: the central spring is not stretched, so $\omega_s^2=k/m$. It maps $(1,-1)$ to $(k+2\kappa)(1,-1)$: the central spring is engaged, so $\omega_a^2=(k+2\kappa)/m$. For $\kappa>0$, the antisymmetric motion is faster. This is exercise 03 from TD 7.

</Collapsible>

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Confuse $\omega_0$, $\omega_d$, and $\omega_r$.** The lossless rate is $\omega_0$; the underdamped motion oscillates at $\omega_d=\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$; the forced peak is at $\omega_r=\omega_0\sqrt{1-2\zeta^2}$ when it exists. They coincide only when the losses vanish.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Write two independent exponentials at the critical threshold.** The root is repeated: the solution is $(A+Bt)e^{-\omega_0t}$. Forgetting the factor $t$ from the Jordan block loses one integration constant.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Take the eigenvalue for the frequency.** In $(K-\omega^2M)v=0$, the eigenvalue is $\omega^2$. You must take its square root, as between $3{,}12$ and $1{,}77$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Use the mass of a single atom.** For a diatomic bond, the relative inertia is $\mu=m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)$. For two equal masses, $\mu=m/2$, not $m$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Assume that the isotope changes the electronic stiffness.** In the Born–Oppenheimer approximation, $k$ remains almost unchanged; the reduced mass shifts the frequency according to $\omega'/\omega=\sqrt{\mu/\mu'}$.

</Pitfall>

## Lesson summary

You now have the three abilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Transform a minimum:** near a minimum, $E\simeq E_0+kx^2/2$ gives $mx''+kx=0$ and $\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}$.
- **Classify losses:** $\zeta=\gamma/(2\sqrt{mk})$ separates underdamped, critically damped, and overdamped returns; $E'=-\gamma x'^2$.
- **Predict absorption:** periodic forcing gives $A(\omega)=F_0/\sqrt{(k-m\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2}$ and a peak near the natural rate.
- **Separate a coupling:** solving $(K-\omega^2M)v=0$ gives the collective shapes and their frequencies; every small vibration is their superposition.
- **Read CO₂:** the directions $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ predict $1330$ and $2349\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}$; only the second is strongly IR-active in this model.
- **Predict an isotope:** $\omega'/\omega=\sqrt{\mu/\mu'}$ with $\mu=m_1m_2/(m_1+m_2)$.

</KeyResults>
