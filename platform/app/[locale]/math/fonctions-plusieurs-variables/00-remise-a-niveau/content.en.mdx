## How to use this diagnostic bridge

The module will study chemical quantities that depend on several variables and
concentrations that evolve together. The ideas will be new, but the basic calculations come
from L1: differentiating, integrating, solving a small evolution equation and handling a
$2\times2$ matrix. If a move still demands a lot of attention, this page tells you which one to train.
It is a diagnostic, not a judgement.

The path fits in three stages:

1. reread the list of automatic skills and the only formal refresher on the page;
2. solve five questions on paper, without notes or a calculator;
3. open the corrections, then go back only over the shaky points.

## The required automatic skills

You should be able, without hesitating, to:

- solve $ax + b = 0$ and a $2\times2$ linear system;
- differentiate exponentials, logarithms, trigonometric functions and their
  compositions;
- integrate simple powers, exponentials and trigonometric
  functions;
- solve $y' = ay$, $y' + ay = b$ and separable ordinary differential
  equations (ODEs);
- handle vectors, dot products, norms and $2\times2$ determinants;
- factor a quadratic trinomial and interpret its discriminant.

If even one of these points makes you doubt, note it down: the diagnostic below
will tell you which refresher to target.

## The refresher to keep in sight — approximating a signal near a measurement

A chemical model can be exact yet inconvenient to recompute for a small change in
concentration. Problem: we want to replace a complicated formula locally by a polynomial
calculation. So we need an approximation tool around the known measurement.

**Definition 1 (Taylor expansion).** A
<Def id="developpement-limite">**Taylor expansion**</Def> is the approximation of a function
near a point by a polynomial. At order $1$, that polynomial is the tangent line:

$$
f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h+o(h),
$$

where the remainder $o(h)$ becomes negligible compared with $h$ as $h$ becomes small.

**Example (chemistry) 1.** An instrument delivers the signal $S(c)=\ln(1+c^2)$ for a concentration $c$.
Near $c=1$, we have $S(1)=\ln 2$ and $S'(1)=1$, so Definition 1 gives

$$
S(1+h)=\ln 2+h+o(h).
$$

For a small increase $h=0{,}02$, the signal therefore changes by about $0{,}02$.

**Remark.** In one variable, order $1$ gives a tangent line. With two variables, the same
need will lead to the <Terme id="plan-tangent">tangent plane</Terme> of lesson 4.

### Mini-exercise — order 1

The same instrument goes from $c=1$ to $c=0{,}97$. Without recomputing the logarithm,
what change in $S$ does Definition 1 predict?

<Collapsible title="Answer">

Here $h=-0{,}03$, so $S(0{,}97)-S(1)\simeq-0{,}03$. The negative sign
indicates a drop in the signal; the approximation is enough to predict its direction
and its order of magnitude.

</Collapsible>

## The minimal diagnostic

The five models are deliberately stripped down to isolate the mathematical
move. Without notes or a calculator, solve:

1. A concentration follows $y' + 3y = 6$, with $y(0) = 5$. Determine $y(t)$.
2. Two concentration deviations are coupled by the matrix below.
   Find its <Terme id="valeur-propre">eigenvalues</Terme>:
   $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
3. A calibration signal is $f(x)=\ln(1+x^2)$. Determine its tangent line at
   $x=1$.
4. In the plane of a molecule, convert the position
   $(x,y)=(-\sqrt3,1)$ into
   <Terme id="coordonnees-polaires">polar coordinates</Terme>.
5. Near a reference geometry, the energy change is modelled
   by the quadratic form — a homogeneous polynomial of degree $2$ —
   $q(x,y)=x^2-4xy+5y^2$. Classify it.

Take the time to write out your five answers before opening the corrigé (worked solutions).

<Collapsible title="Detailed answers">

**1. First-order linear differential equation.**

**Step 1 — switch off the constant supply.** The resulting equation,
$y'+3y=0$, is the homogeneous equation; its solutions are
$y_h(t)=Ce^{-3t}$.

*Why?* This family describes the transient part that fades with
time; it will provide the constant to be set with the initial measurement.

**Step 2 — look for the value maintained by the supply.** A constant solution
$y_p$ satisfies $3y_p=6$, so $y_p=2$.

*Why?* The constant $2$ is the value compatible with the supply $6$;
in kinetics, it announces the concentration toward which the model
evolves.

**Step 3 — combine the two contributions.** The general solution is
$y(t)=2+Ce^{-3t}$.

*Why?* The sum "maintained value + transient" solves the complete
equation, not only its version without supply.

**Step 4 — use the initial concentration.** The condition $y(0)=5$
gives $5=2+C$, so $C=3$ and

$$
y(t) = 2 + 3e^{-3t}.
$$

*Why?* Without this measurement, all the values of $C$ would remain
possible.

**Step 5 — check inside the model.** We compute
$y'=-9e^{-3t}$ and $3y=6+9e^{-3t}$, so $y'+3y=6$; moreover,
$y(0)=2+3=5$.

*Why?* The substitution checks both the evolution law and the
initial experimental datum.

**2. $2\times2$ matrix.**

**Step 1 — turn the search into an equation.** The
<Terme id="polynome-caracteristique">characteristic polynomial</Terme>
is $p_A(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I)$:

$$
\det\begin{pmatrix} 2-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & 2-\lambda \end{pmatrix}
= (2-\lambda)^2 - 1.
$$

*Why?* If a nonzero vector $v$ satisfies $Av=\lambda v$, then
$(A-\lambda I)v=0$. Such a vector exists exactly when
$p_A(\lambda)=0$.

**Step 2 — solve the resulting equation.** We have

$$
(2-\lambda)^2-1=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
2-\lambda=\pm1,
$$

hence $\lambda=1$ and $\lambda=3$.

*Why?* Factoring a difference of squares avoids expanding a
trinomial and makes both answers visible.

**Step 3 — check with two invariants.** The sum is
$1+3=4$, equal to the trace of $A$, and the product is $1\times3=3$,
equal to $\det A=4-1$.

*Why?* These two checks quickly catch a sign error.
In a model of two coupled concentrations, lesson 9 will give the meaning
of these two numbers.

**3. Tangent line to the calibration signal.**

**Step 1 — compute the value at the known point.** We get
$f(1)=\ln2$.

*Why?* A tangent line must pass through the point of the signal
$(1,\ln2)$.

**Step 2 — measure the local variation.** By the
<Terme id="regle-de-la-chaine">chain rule</Terme>,

$$
f'(x)=\frac{2x}{1+x^2},
\qquad
f'(1)=1.
$$

*Why?* The derivative provides the coefficient of $h$ in the
Taylor expansion of Definition 1.

**Step 3 — write the order-$1$ approximation.** The tangent line is

$$
y = \ln 2 + (x - 1).
$$

*Why?* This line replaces the complicated signal by
$f(1+h)\simeq\ln2+h$ for a small change in concentration.

**4. Polar position — watch the quadrant.**

**Step 1 — compute the distance to the origin.** We find
$r=\sqrt{(-\sqrt3)^2+1^2}=2$.

*Why?* The radius fixes the atom's distance to the chosen centre before
looking for its direction.

**Step 2 — locate the sector of the plane.** The point has $x<0$ and $y>0$: it
lies in the second quadrant.

*Why?* The quotient $y/x$ is not enough to distinguish two opposite
directions; the sign of the coordinates picks the right one.

**Step 3 — correct the angle given by the arctangent.** The computation
$\arctan(y/x)=\arctan(-1/\sqrt3)=-\pi/6$ gives an opposite direction. So we
add a half-turn:

$$
\theta = -\frac{\pi}{6} + \pi = \frac{5\pi}{6} \pmod{2\pi}.
$$

*Why?* On a molecular diagram, a half-turn error puts
the atom on the wrong side of the centre.

**Step 4 — go back to the starting coordinates.** We check
$2\cos(5\pi/6)=-\sqrt3$ and $2\sin(5\pi/6)=1$.

*Why?* The inverse conversion validates the radius and
the angle at the same time. This check will become systematic in lesson 1.

**5. Quadratic energy change.**

**Step 1 — write the associated symmetric matrix.** The cross coefficient
$-4xy$ splits into two coefficients $-2$, so

$$
q(x,y)=
\begin{pmatrix}x&y\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}1&-2\\-2&5\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}.
$$

*Why?* The matrix lets us apply a short, reusable criterion
to classify the energy change.

**Step 2 — test the leading principal minors.** The first equals $1>0$ and the
determinant equals $1\cdot5-(-2)^2=1>0$. The form is therefore positive definite.

*Why?* For a symmetric $2\times2$ matrix, two positive leading principal
minors guarantee that the form stays positive away from
the origin.

**Step 3 — make the sign visible.** We can also write

$$
q(x,y)=(x-2y)^2+y^2.
$$

*Why?* A sum of squares provides a second proof and gives the
chemical meaning: any small nonzero deformation costs energy. The
reference geometry is then a
<Terme id="extremum-local">local minimum</Terme>; a form taking
both signs would instead announce a
<Terme id="point-selle">saddle point</Terme>, studied in lesson 6.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

Why are the two checks of question 5 not two unrelated
methods?

The symmetric matrix encodes exactly the same polynomial as the sum of
squares. The minors certify its sign through a matrix criterion; completing
the square makes that sign visible term by term. Both readings
conclude that $q(x,y)>0$ as soon as $(x,y)\ne(0,0)$.

</Collapsible>

</Collapsible>

<Pitfall>

**The $\arctan(y/x)$ reflex without looking at the quadrant.** This is the most
frequent mistake of the whole start of the module: $\arctan$ only returns
angles between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$, so every point with negative $x$ receives
a wrong angle if you do not correct by $\pm\pi$. Always draw a
mini-sketch of the point before concluding.

</Pitfall>

## Quick check

The same five questions, in instant form — answer on paper first,
then check here.

<Quiz title="Quick check — optional, as always">

  <QItem>

    What is the solution of $y' + 3y = 6$ with $y(0) = 5$?

    <QOption id="a" correct>$y = 2 + 3e^{-3t}$</QOption>
    <QOption id="b">$y = 5e^{-3t}$</QOption>
    <QOption id="c">$y = 6 - e^{-3t}$</QOption>

    <QFeedback of="a">

      Yes: constant particular solution $y_p = 2$, homogeneous part $Ce^{-3t}$, and the
      initial condition sets $C = 3$.

    </QFeedback>

    <QFeedback of="b">

      That is the solution of the *homogeneous* equation $y' + 3y = 0$ — you
      forgot the right-hand side $6$, which imposes the particular solution
      $y_p = 2$.

    </QFeedback>

    <QFeedback of="c">

      Check the initial condition: this candidate gives $y(0) = 5$, but
      substituting it into the equation gives $y'+3y=18$, not $6$. Start again
      with "homogeneous + particular".

    </QFeedback>

    <QExplain>

      Standard method: particular solution (here constant, $y_p = b/a$)
      plus homogeneous solution $Ce^{-at}$, then initial condition. This scheme
      will come back unchanged for first-order chemical kinetics.

    </QExplain>

  </QItem>

  <QItem>

    What is the slope of the tangent line to $f(x) = \ln(1 + x^2)$ at $x = 1$?

    <QOption id="a">$\tfrac12$</QOption>
    <QOption id="b" correct>$1$</QOption>
    <QOption id="c">$2$</QOption>

    <QFeedback of="a">

      You may have differentiated $\ln$ without the chain rule: you must
      multiply $\frac{1}{1+x^2}$ by the *inner* derivative $2x$.

    </QFeedback>

    <QFeedback of="b">

      Exactly: $f'(x) = \dfrac{2x}{1+x^2}$, so $f'(1) = \dfrac22 = 1$.

    </QFeedback>

    <QFeedback of="c">

      $2$ is the inner derivative $2x$ at $x=1$… multiplied by nothing. The
      factor $\frac{1}{1+x^2} = \frac12$ is missing.

    </QFeedback>

    <QExplain>

      Chain rule: $(\ln u)' = u'/u$. The full tangent line is
      $y = \ln 2 + (x-1)$: it is the order-$1$ Taylor expansion of
      Definition 1.

    </QExplain>

  </QItem>

  <QNumeric answer={3} tolerance={0}>

    What is the **largest** eigenvalue of
    $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$?

    <QExplain>

      $(2-\lambda)^2 - 1 = 0$ gives $\lambda = 1$ or $\lambda = 3$. Checking
      trick: trace $= 4 = 1 + 3$, determinant $= 3 = 1\times3$.

    </QExplain>

  </QNumeric>

  <QNumeric answer={2} tolerance={0}>

    What is the radius $r$ of the polar coordinates of the point
    $(-\sqrt3,\ 1)$?

    <QExplain>

      $r = \sqrt{(-\sqrt3)^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt4 = 2$. And the angle is
      $\theta = 5\pi/6$ — not $-\pi/6$: the point lies in the second
      quadrant, you must correct the $\arctan$ by $+\pi$.

    </QExplain>

  </QNumeric>

  <QItem>

    The quadratic form $x^2 - 4xy + 5y^2$ is…

    <QOption id="a" correct>positive definite</QOption>
    <QOption id="b">indefinite (saddle type)</QOption>
    <QOption id="c">negative definite</QOption>

    <QFeedback of="a">

      Yes: leading principal minors $1$ and $5 - 4 = 1$, both positive.
      Another reading: $x^2 - 4xy + 5y^2 = (x - 2y)^2 + y^2$, a sum of two
      squares.

    </QFeedback>

    <QFeedback of="b">

      A saddle would require a *negative* determinant of the associated matrix.
      Here $\det = 1\cdot5 - (-2)^2 = 1$, positive. Careful: the cross
      coefficient $-4$ splits into two off-diagonal entries of $-2$.

    </QFeedback>

    <QFeedback of="c">

      Test $y = 0$: the form equals $x^2$, strictly positive — it cannot
      be negative definite.

    </QFeedback>

    <QExplain>

      Leading-principal-minor criterion for
      $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$: $1$ and $1$,
      both positive, hence positive definite. Lesson 6 will reuse exactly
      this classification to read an energy near a geometry.

    </QExplain>

  </QItem>

## What to do with your score

- **5/5 without hesitating**: you can move straight on to lesson 1.
- **One or two hesitations**: review the precise point (first-order
  ODEs, chain rule, polar quadrant, leading principal minors) then
  redo the question — ten minutes are often enough.
- **Three or more questions in difficulty**: plan an L1 review
  session and keep this page open as a reference for the rest of
  the module.

In every case, all the lessons, all the exercises and all the solutions remain
accessible: this score organises your work, it locks nothing.

## Lesson summary

You now know how to spot the L1 move to review and to tie each of the five calculations to a chemical use.

<KeyResults title="The formulas to keep in sight">

- The module assumes six families of automatic skills: linear equations and
  systems, differentiation, integration, simple ODEs, $2\times2$ vector
  calculus, quadratic trinomials.
- Order $1$ near $a$:
  $f(a+h)=f(a)+f'(a)h+o(h)$ — the tangent line locally replaces the function.
- ODE $y'+ay=b$, with $a\ne0$:
  $y(t)=b/a+Ce^{-at}$, then the initial condition sets $C$.
- $2\times2$ eigenvalues: characteristic polynomial, checked with trace
  and determinant.
- Polar coordinates: $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, and **always** correct
  $\arctan(y/x)$ according to the quadrant.
- Symmetric $2\times2$ quadratic form: positive leading principal minors
  $\Rightarrow$ positive definite form.
- A diagnostic serves to target the review — not to judge.

</KeyResults>
