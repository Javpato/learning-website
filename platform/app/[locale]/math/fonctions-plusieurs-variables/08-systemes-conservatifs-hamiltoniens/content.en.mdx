# Decide whether a bond vibrates or breaks using its energy

<LessonMeta id="math-fmv-c08" />
<Toc />

<Accroche>

In frictionless motion, the curve followed by a bond is exactly an equal-energy curve.

</Accroche>

<Mission fil="A frictionless diatomic bond — vibrate or cross the breaking threshold">

**Situation.** In a reduced model, the elongation $q$ of a bond starts from $q(0)=0$ with $p(0)=1{,}20$; its kinetic energy is $p^2/2$, and the breaking barrier is $1{,}00$ eV.
**Precise question.** Does the bond return and vibrate, or does it break, and what value of $p(0)$ separates these two outcomes? **Obstacle.** In L1, solving the equation of motion would require an explicit formula that is difficult to obtain. **Contract.** In this chapter: 3 tools (identify what does not change, turn energy into a map of motions, compare with an energy loss). By the end, we will solve this problem, with numbers to back it up.

</Mission>

*Feeling confident? [Go straight to the TD (tutorial session)](../td-5/) — the theory will still be here when you get stuck.*

## The battle plan

To fulfil the contract, we need three tools:

1. **identify** a quantity that keeps the same value throughout the motion;
2. **translate** this quantity into paths and locate the boundary between vibration and breaking;
3. **compare** the ideal motion with the same system when a solvent removes energy.

Each tool returns to the bond described by the same variables $q$ and $p$.

## 1. Identify what does not change

**Tool 1 — identify.** We are looking for a calculation that replaces solving the motion explicitly.

Problem: a <Terme id="systeme-differentiel">system of ODEs</Terme> gives the velocities, but not necessarily the functions $q(t)$ and $p(t)$. We therefore need a quantity that can be checked without knowing these functions.

**Definition 1 (first integral).** A **first integral** is a nonconstant function $H$ that keeps the same value along every solution. With $X'=F(X)$, the <Terme id="regle-de-la-chaine">chain rule</Terme> and the <Terme id="gradient">gradient</Terme> of $H$ give the test

$$
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}H(X(t))
=\nabla H(X(t))\cdot F(X(t))=0.
$$

**Example (chemistry) 1.** For a bond whose reduced mass is set to $1$, suppose that $q'=p$ and $p'=-V'(q)$. Then

$$
H(q,p)=\frac{p^2}{2}+V(q)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\mathrm dH}{\mathrm dt}=V'(q)q'+pp'
=V'(q)p-pV'(q)=0.
$$

The total energy $H$ is therefore a first integral, even without knowing $q(t)$.

**Remark.** Checking $\mathrm dH/\mathrm dt=0$ on a single particular solution is not enough: Definition 1 requires the identity for every solution of the model.

Problem: we want to name the evolutions for which an energy remains constant along every possible path. We therefore need a category of systems.

**Definition 2 (conservative system).** A
<Def id="systeme-conservatif">**conservative system**</Def> is a system in which a quantity, in practice the energy, remains constant along every <Terme id="trajectoire">trajectory</Terme> (the path followed by the state over time).

**Example (chemistry) 2.** The frictionless model in Example 1 is conservative: kinetic energy $p^2/2$ and bond energy $V(q)$ are exchanged, but their sum does not change.

**Remark (chemical counterexample).** In a solvent, the model $q'=p$, $p'=-V'(q)-\gamma p$ with $\gamma>0$ satisfies

$$
\frac{\mathrm dH}{\mathrm dt}=-\gamma p^2\le0.
$$

It is not conservative: the bath removes energy whenever the bond moves.

**Proposition 1 (confinement to one value).** If $H$ is a first integral, every path starting from $X_0$ remains on the <Terme id="ligne-de-niveau">level curve</Terme>
$H=H(X_0)$, that is, the curve of states with the same energy.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — from zero derivative to constant value">

Set $h(t)=H(X(t))$. Definition 1 gives $h'(t)=0$ on every interval where the solution exists. The mean value theorem then implies $h(t)=h(0)=H(X_0)$. Thus $X(t)$ belongs to the set $H=H(X_0)$ for every $t$. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## 2. Turn energy into a map of motions

**Tool 2 — translate.** We will construct the laws of motion from the energy, then read the breaking threshold directly from its map.

Problem: Proposition 1 confines paths to $H=C$, but does not say which velocity field travels along these curves. We therefore need a structure that turns energy into motion.

**Definition 3 (planar Hamiltonian).** A
<Def id="hamiltonien">**Hamiltonian**</Def> is an energy function $H(x,y)$ whose regular components $H=C$ are the trajectories of the conservative system it generates through

$$
x'=H_y,\qquad y'=-H_x.
$$

A system written in this form is called a **planar Hamiltonian system**.

**Example (chemistry) 3.** For the bond,

$$
H(q,p)=\frac{p^2}{2\mu}+V(q)
$$

gives

$$
q'=H_p=\frac p\mu,\qquad p'=-H_q=-V'(q),
$$

so $\mu q''=-V'(q)$: this is Newton's law written with two variables.

**Remark (chemical notation).** $q$ is the bond elongation, $p=\mu q'$ its momentum, and $\mu$ the reduced mass of the two atoms. The subscripts $H_q$ and $H_p$ denote <Terme id="derivee-partielle">partial derivatives</Terme>: one variable changes while the other is held fixed.

**Proposition 2 (automatic conservation).** Every planar Hamiltonian system conserves $H$, because

$$
\frac{\mathrm dH}{\mathrm dt}=H_xx'+H_yy'
=H_xH_y-H_yH_x=0.
$$

The field $(H_y,-H_x)$ is $\nabla H$ rotated by a quarter-turn: it is tangent to the curves $H=C$. This closes the loop with lesson 2: **the regular trajectories ARE the level curves of $H$**, now oriented by time.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — when an entire level is a trajectory">

Proposition 2 first shows that every trajectory remains in $H=C$. Conversely, on a connected component where $\nabla H\ne0$, the field $(H_y,-H_x)$ is nonzero and tangent to the level. Uniqueness of solutions therefore makes it possible to travel along this component without leaving it.

A critical level requires more care: it may contain a stationary state and several distinct branches that approach it. Saying “the level is a trajectory” without this qualification would be false. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

Problem: two energy paths can correspond to different outcomes; we need to name the one that forms their boundary. Near an <Terme id="point-d-equilibre">equilibrium point</Terme>, the motion can in particular stop.

**Definition 4 (separatrix).** A
<Def id="separatrice">**separatrix**</Def> is a boundary trajectory passing through a <Terme id="point-selle">saddle point</Terme> (a stationary state that attracts in one direction and repels in another); it separates different behaviours, here vibrating or escaping.

**Example (chemistry) 4.** Take, in reduced units,

$$
V(q)=D(3q^2-2q^3),\qquad D=1{,}00\ \mathrm{eV}.
$$

$q=0$ is the bottom of the bound well and $q=1$ the top of the barrier, with $V(0)=0$ and $V(1)=D$. In the $(q,p)$ plane, the level $H=D$ passes through the stationary state $(1,0)$: it is the separatrix. Below $D$, a loop surrounds $(0,0)$; above $D$, the path heads toward large $q$.

**Proposition 3 (energy reading of a bond).** For $H(q,p)=p^2/(2\mu)+V(q)$:

- a closed component of $H=E$ around a <Terme id="extremum-local">minimum</Terme> describes a periodic vibration;
- at the barrier level, the separatrix approaches the unstable stationary state in infinite time;
- above the barrier, an open component describes escape if the potential opens up.

<Collapsible title="Example 5 — the double well, complete calculation preserved">

For $V(q)=q^4/4-q^2/2$ and $\mu=1$,

$$
q'=p,\qquad p'=q-q^3,\qquad H(q,p)=\frac{p^2}{2}+\frac{q^4}{4}-\frac{q^2}{2}.
$$

The stationary states are $(0,0)$ and $(\pm1,0)$, with $H(\pm1,0)=-1/4$ and $H(0,0)=0$. The <Terme id="portrait-de-phase">phase portrait</Terme> map contains two loops for $-1/4<C<0$, a figure-eight separatrix for $C=0$, then one large loop for $C>0$.

The <Terme id="jacobienne">Jacobian matrix</Terme> of the field is

$$
J(q,p)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1-3q^2&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$

At $(\pm1,0)$, its trace is $0$ and its determinant is $2$: the <Terme id="linearisation">linearisation</Terme> suggests a **centre**, that is, a stationary state surrounded by loops. Conservation proves that this centre persists in the nonlinear system. At $(0,0)$, the determinant is $-1$: the saddle point is consistent with the figure-eight separatrix.

</Collapsible>

**Method 1 (decide using energy).**

1. write $H$ and check $\mathrm dH/\mathrm dt=0$;
2. calculate the energy $E_0$ of the initial state;
3. calculate the energy of stationary states and barriers;
4. compare $E_0$ with these values, then read the component of $H=E_0$.

## 3. Predict what a solvent changes

**Tool 3 — compare.** Ideal energy follows a curve; a dissipative medium can instead make that energy decrease.

Problem: the frictionless model does not represent a molecule coupled to a bath. We therefore need a motion that directly follows the direction of steepest descent on a landscape.

**Definition 5 (gradient flow).** The **gradient flow** of a potential $V$ is

$$
X'=-\nabla V(X).
$$

It crosses the curves $V=C$ perpendicularly, toward lower values.

**Example (chemistry) 6.** In the highly damped limit of the bond, the momentum readjusts quickly and the elongation follows $q'=-V'(q)$. Then

$$
\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}=V'(q)q'=-\bigl(V'(q)\bigr)^2\le0.
$$

The bond descends toward the accessible bottom instead of endlessly travelling around a loop.

**Proposition 4 (decrease and absence of loops).** Along a gradient flow,

$$
\frac{\mathrm dV}{\mathrm dt}
=\nabla V\cdot(-\nabla V)=-\|\nabla V\|^2\le0.
$$

A nonstationary solution therefore cannot be periodic.

<Collapsible title="Rigorous proof — a descent cannot form a loop">

If $X(t)$ were nonconstant and periodic with period $T$, then $V(X(T))=V(X(0))$. Yet

$$
V(X(T))-V(X(0))
=-\int_0^T\|\nabla V(X(t))\|^2\,\mathrm dt<0,
$$

because the integrand is not identically zero. This is a contradiction. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

**Example (chemistry) 7.** The landscape in TD 5.3,

$$
V(x,y)=\frac14(x^2-1)^2+\frac12y^2,
$$

has stationary states $(\pm1,0)$ and $(0,0)$. Its <Terme id="hessienne">Hessian matrix</Terme> is $\operatorname{diag}(3x^2-1,1)$: the first two are minima, the last a saddle point. Under $X'=-\nabla V$, Proposition 4 makes $V$ decrease toward one of the minima; these are <Terme id="stabilite">asymptotically stable</Terme> in this model.

**Remark (chemistry).** Over short times, a nearly isolated vibration follows $H=p^2/(2\mu)+V(q)$. With friction, $\mathrm dH/\mathrm dt=-\gamma p^2$. In the highly damped limit, one obtains the gradient flow. A geometry optimisation does the same on an energy surface; the steepest-descent path from a transition state is called the **intrinsic reaction coordinate**
(IRC).

**Method 2 (do not confuse the two maps).**

1. calculate the derivative of the energy along the field;
2. if it is zero, look for the levels that are followed;
3. if it equals $-\|\nabla V\|^2$, look for the minima toward which the system descends;
4. if no identity appears, do not force either the conservative label or the gradient label.

<KeyResults title="The formulas to keep in sight">

- First integral: $\mathrm dH/\mathrm dt=\nabla H\cdot F=0$.
- Hamiltonian: $x'=H_y$, $y'=-H_x$; the regular trajectories are $H=C$.
- Mechanics: $H(q,p)=p^2/(2\mu)+V(q)$, so $q'=p/\mu$ and $p'=-V'(q)$.
- Gradient flow: $X'=-\nabla V$, so $\mathrm dV/\mathrm dt=-\|\nabla V\|^2\le0$.

</KeyResults>

## Worked example — decide between vibration and breaking

**Example 8.** Let us now solve the mission with $V(q)=3q^2-2q^3$, $D=1{,}00$ eV, and $\mu=1$.

**Step 1 — construct the controlled energy.**

$$
H(q,p)=\frac{p^2}{2}+3q^2-2q^3,
\qquad q'=p,\qquad p'=-6q(1-q).
$$

*Why?* Definition 3 turns the bond model into a Hamiltonian system; Proposition 2 then guarantees that this energy will remain available for making the decision.

**Step 2 — evaluate the initial state.**

$$
E_0=H(0,1{,}20)=\frac{1{,}20^2}{2}=0{,}72\ \mathrm{eV}.
$$

*Why?* Method 1 requires the number of the energy curve that is actually followed.

**Step 3 — measure the barrier.** $V(1)=3-2=1{,}00$ eV and $V'(1)=0$. The state $(1,0)$ is therefore the unstable stationary state at the top.

*Why?* By Definition 4, the energy of this stationary state is that of the separatrix: it provides the threshold with which to compare $E_0$.

**Step 4 — decide for $p(0)=1{,}20$.** Since $0{,}72<1{,}00$, the initial level remains on the bound side. Its two turning points, obtained with $p=0$, satisfy $V(q)=0{,}72$ and are approximately $q=-0{,}432$ and $q=0{,}651$.

*Why?* These numbers bound the elongation: the bond oscillates between them and never reaches the barrier $q=1$.

**Step 5 — calculate the momentum threshold.** At $q(0)=0$, the equality $H=D$ gives

$$
\frac{p_{\mathrm{seuil}}^2}{2}=1,
\qquad p_{\mathrm{seuil}}=\sqrt2\simeq1{,}414.
$$

*Why?* Proposition 3 then separates the three cases: vibration below this number, separatrix at equality, and breaking above it.

**Step 6 — recover the map from lessons 2 and 7.** The initial motion travels around the loop $H=0{,}72$ in the $(q,p)$ plane; it does not converge to $(0,0)$.

*Why?* Proposition 2 identifies this loop both as a level curve from lesson 2 and as a trajectory in the phase portrait from lesson 7.

<Collapsible title="Comprehension question — answer">

Why does the stationary state $(0,0)$ not attract the bond, even though it corresponds to the minimum of $H$?

Because a trajectory with energy $0{,}72$ cannot descend to the stationary state's energy $0$. It remains on its own loop. A minimum therefore sits inside a centre in the conservative model, but becomes attractive only when a mechanism dissipates energy.

</Collapsible>

2. **Your turn:** complete the cancellation and deduce the curve followed from $(1,0)$.
3. **Your turn:** explain why this curve is closed and why the motion is periodic.

<Collapsible title="Answer">

$H_x=x+x^3$ and $H_y=y$, so $\mathrm dH/\mathrm dt=(x+x^3)y+y(-x-x^3)=0$. From $(1,0)$, $H=3/4$. Since $H$ tends to infinity when $\|(x,y)\|$ tends to infinity, this positive level is closed and bounded; it surrounds the only stationary state $(0,0)$. The field never vanishes on it, so the trajectory travels around this loop periodically.

</Collapsible>

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Reverse only one sign.** The form is $x'=H_y$, $y'=-H_x$. Reversing both signs only changes the direction of time; reversing just one generally destroys conservation. Always recalculate $\mathrm dH/\mathrm dt$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Treat the entire critical level as a single trajectory.** The double-well separatrix contains the stationary state and several branches that approach it in infinite time. Uniqueness prevents a branch from crossing that state in finite time.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Assume that a first integral always exists.** A planar Hamiltonian field satisfies $\operatorname{div}F=\partial_x(H_y)+\partial_y(-H_x)=0$. A nonzero divergence therefore rules out this form, even if the portrait looks almost circular.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Look for an oscillation in a gradient flow.** Proposition 4 imposes a strict decrease away from stationary states. If a supposedly purely dissipative model loops, it is not a gradient flow or one sign is wrong.

</Pitfall>

<MissionSolved>

**1. What does not change — Definitions 1 and 2.** For $H=p^2/2+3q^2-2q^3$, the chain rule gives $\mathrm dH/\mathrm dt=0$: the frictionless bond keeps its energy.

**2. The path followed — Definition 3 and Proposition 2.** The initial state has $H(0,1{,}20)=0{,}72$ eV. Its trajectory IS therefore the level loop $H=0{,}72$, between $q\simeq-0{,}432$ and $q\simeq0{,}651$: the bond vibrates and returns.

**3. The threshold — Definition 4 and Proposition 3.** The barrier is $H(1,0)=1{,}00$ eV. At $q(0)=0$, the separatrix requires $p(0)=\sqrt2\simeq1{,}414$: below it the bond vibrates, at equality it follows the boundary, and above it the bond breaks.

**4. The effect of the solvent — Proposition 4.** With friction, $\mathrm dH/\mathrm dt=-\gamma p^2\le0$: the motion no longer remains on a loop and relaxes toward an accessible well. The three tools have settled the question, with numbers to back it up.

</MissionSolved>

## Lesson summary

You now have the three abilities announced in the battle plan.

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- **Identify:** propose $H$, then check $\mathrm dH/\mathrm dt=0$ along every solution.
- **Translate:** write $x'=H_y$, $y'=-H_x$, and read the trajectories as the curves $H=C$.
- **Decide:** compare the initial energy with the barrier values; the separatrix marks the threshold.
- **Compare:** Hamiltonian motion follows the levels, while a gradient flow descends them.
- **Connect the chapters:** the level curves from lesson 2 are the trajectories from lesson 7
  as soon as the energy generates the motion.
- **Running example:** $0{,}72$ eV is below the $1{,}00$ eV barrier; the threshold is
  $p=\sqrt2\simeq1{,}414$.

</KeyResults>
