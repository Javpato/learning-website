# Conservative and Hamiltonian systems

<Accroche>

When a quantity is conserved, the trajectories no longer have a choice: they are prisoners of the level curves — and when a quantity decreases, they descend perpendicularly.

## Intuition

Take the wind map from lesson 7 and superimpose the level map of a function $H(x,y)$ on it.

- **Conservative case**: if the wind is everywhere *tangent* to the level curves of $H$, a particle carried by the wind remains on its starting line — $H$ never changes. Around a minimum of $H$, the level curves are small closed loops: the trajectories go around in circles forever. The true center exists.
- **Dissipative case**: if the wind is everywhere *perpendicular* to the level curves and points towards the descent ($X' = -\nabla V$), the particle crosses the levels from the highest to the lowest and ends up trapped in a basin — a local minimum of $V$.

Same map, two opposing dynamics: *follow* the levels or *go down* them.

<KeyResults title="Formulas to keep visible">

- first integral: $\dfrac{dH}{dt}=\nabla H\cdot F=0$.
- Hamiltonian: $x'=H_y$, $y'=-H_x$; the trajectories follow $H=C$.
- gradient flow: $X'=-\nabla V$ and $\dfrac{dV}{dt}=-\|\nabla V\|^2\le0$.

</KeyResults>

## Formal definition

### first integral

A non-constant function $H(x,y)$ is a **first integral** of the system $X' = F(X)$ if it is constant along any solution:

<Rappel title="chain rule along a trajectory">

If $x=x(t)$ and $y=y(t)$, then $\dfrac{dH}{dt}=H_xx'+H_yy'=\nabla H\cdot X'$. It is this identity which makes it possible to decide whether an energy remains constant or decreases.

</Rappel>

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\,H\bigl(x(t),\,y(t)\bigr)
= \nabla H \cdot F = H_x\,f + H_y\,g = 0 .
$$

(This is the chain rule from lesson 4 in action.) Geometric consequence: **each trajectory is contained in a level curve** $H = C$, the constant being fixed by the initial condition.

### Planar Hamiltonian systems

A system is **Hamiltonian** if it derives from a function $H$ by

$$
x' = H_y(x,y),
\qquad
y' = -H_x(x,y) .
$$

Then $H$ is automatically a first integral:

$$
\frac{dH}{dt} = H_x\,x' + H_y\,y'
= H_x H_y - H_y H_x = 0 .
$$

<KeyResults title="The fundamental case: the mechanics of a particle">

For a position $q$ and momentum $p$, the total energy

$$
H(q,p) = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V(q)
$$

generates Hamiltonian system

$$
q' = H_p = \frac{p}{m},
\qquad
p' = -H_q = -V'(q),
$$

that is to say exactly Newton's law $mq'' = -V'(q)$. Mechanical energy is conserved, and phase portrait is the level map of $H$: troughs of $V$ → closed loops (oscillations), necks of $V$ → separatrices.

</KeyResults>

### gradient flows

The **gradient flow** of a potential $V$ is the system

$$
X' = -\nabla V(X)
\qquad\text{soit}\qquad
x' = -V_x,\quad y' = -V_y .
$$

Along a solution,

$$
\frac{dV}{dt} = \nabla V \cdot X' = -\|\nabla V\|^2 \le 0 :
$$

$V$ **strictly decreases** as long as $\nabla V \neq 0$. The trajectories cross the level curves *perpendicularly* (the gradient is perpendicular to the level curves, lesson 4) and converge towards the local minima of $V$.

<KeyResults title="Hamiltonian versus gradient flow — the contrast to remember">

For the same landscape function:

- **Hamiltonian**: the field is tangent to level curves of $H$; $H$ is retained; the minima are surrounded by **centers** (periodic orbits, no convergence);
- **gradient flow**: the field is perpendicular to the level curves of $V$; $V$ decreases; the minima are **attractive sinks** (stable nodes), the saddles of $V$ are saddles of the flow.

A Hamiltonian system never has an asymptotically stable equilibrium; a gradient flow never has a periodic orbit.

</KeyResults>

</Collapsible>

## Worked example

Let's study the particle in the **double well** $V(q) = \tfrac14 q^4 - \tfrac12 q^2$, that is, the Hamiltonian system

$$
q' = p,
\qquad
p' = -V'(q) = q - q^3,
\qquad
H(q,p) = \frac{p^2}{2} + \frac{q^4}{4} - \frac{q^2}{2}
$$

(we take $m = 1$).

**Step 1 — equilibria.** $p = 0$ and $q - q^3 = 0$: three equilibria, $(0,0)$ and $(\pm 1, 0)$ — the origin at the top of the bump of $V$, the other two at the bottom of the wells.

**Step 2 — values of $H$.** $H(\pm1, 0) = \tfrac14 - \tfrac12 = -\tfrac14$ and $H(0,0) = 0$.

**Step 3 — reading the phase portrait from the levels of $H$.**

- For $-\tfrac14 < C < 0$: the $H = C$ line consists of **two closed loops**, one around each well → oscillations trapped in a well.
- For $C = 0$: the level curve passes through the origin, forming a **figure eight** — it is the *separatrix*, border between the two regimes.
- For $C > 0$: **a single large loop** surrounding the three equilibria → large amplitude oscillations over the bump.

**Step 4 — consistency with linearization.** The Jacobian of the $(p,\ q - q^3)$ field is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 - 3q^2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. In $(\pm1, 0)$: zero trace, determinant $2$ — linear center, which the conservation of $H$ **confirms** to be a real nonlinear center (the neighboring levels are closed loops). In $(0,0)$: determinant $-1$, a saddle — consistent with the figure-eight separatrix.

This is the general scheme: the first integral transforms the fragile verdict “linear center” into certainty.

<PhasePortraitWidget />

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — dH/dt = 0 for all Hamiltonian system">

Let $t \mapsto (x(t), y(t))$ be a solution of $x' = H_y$, $y' = -H_x$. chain rule (lesson 4) gives:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}H(x(t), y(t))
= H_x\,x'(t) + H_y\,y'(t)
= H_x\,(H_y) + H_y\,(-H_x)
= 0 .
$$

So $H$ is constant on each solution: $H(x(t), y(t)) = H(x_0, y_0)$ for all $t$. The trajectory is included in the level curve of its initial condition. Note the *antisymmetric* structure of the field $(H_y, -H_x)$: it is the rotation of $+90°$ of gradient $(H_x, H_y)$, hence the tangency at the levels. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

<Collapsible title="Demonstration — the gradient flow decreases V, and cannot oscillate">

**Decrease.** Along a solution of $X' = -\nabla V$:

$$
\frac{dV}{dt} = \nabla V \cdot X' = \nabla V \cdot (-\nabla V)
= -\|\nabla V\|^2 \le 0,
$$

with tie only to critical points of $V$. Therefore $V$ decreases strictly along any non-stationary trajectory.

**No periodic orbit.** Assume a non-constant periodic solution with period $T$. So $V(X(T)) = V(X(0))$, but

$$
V(X(T)) - V(X(0)) = \int_0^T \frac{dV}{dt}\,dt
= -\int_0^T \|\nabla V(X(t))\|^2\,dt < 0
$$

(the integrand is not identically zero for a non-stationary solution) — contradiction. A gradient flow can therefore never loop: it only knows how to go down. $\blacksquare$

</Collapsible>

## Link with chemistry

The two archetypes of this lesson are the two regimes of molecular movement:

- **Conservative vibration**: over short periods of time, a vibrating connection exchanges kinetic energy and potential without loss — $H = p^2/2\mu + V(q)$ is conserved. The phase portrait loops *are* the vibration states; this is the starting point for vibrational spectroscopy (lesson 10).
- **Dissipative relaxation**: a system coupled to a bath (solvent, thermostat) loses its energy; at the overdamped limit, the geometry follows gradient flow $X' = -\nabla V$. This is exactly what a **geometry optimization** algorithm does: descend the surface of potential energy to the minimum. And the path of greatest slope which connects a transition state (neck of $V$, lesson 6) to the two neighboring minima is the **intrinsic reaction coordinate** (IRC) of the chemists: a trajectory of gradient flow.

Remember: *the short time scale is Hamiltonian, the long time scale with friction is gradient flow* — and both are read on the same surface $V$.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall>

**Reverse the signs in a Hamiltonian system.** It's $x' = +H_y$ and $y' = -H_x$ (the minus on the *second* equation). With the two signs reversed, we obtain the same portrait traversed in reverse — but with a single wrong sign, $H$ is no longer preserved at all. Always check by recalculating $dH/dt$.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Confuse "the trajectory is in a level" with "the level is a trajectory".** A level curve can contain *several* trajectories: the separatrix $H = 0$ of the double well contains the saddle **and** four trajectories which approach it in infinite time, without ever reaching it (uniqueness required).

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Believe that a first integral always exists.** Most systems are neither Hamiltonian nor gradient — quick criterion: a Hamiltonian field satisfies $\operatorname{div} F = \partial_x(H_y) + \partial_y(-H_x) = 0$. A non-zero divergence immediately excludes any Hamiltonian structure.

</Pitfall>

<Pitfall>

**Look for oscillations in a gradient flow.** Impossible: $V$ decreases strictly along the trajectories (demonstration above). If a “purely dissipative” model oscillates, the field is not gradient — or there is a calculation error.

</Pitfall>

## Lesson summary

<KeyResults title="The essentials">

- first integral: $\dfrac{dH}{dt} = \nabla H\cdot F = 0$ — each trajectory is enclosed in a level curve $H = C$.
- Hamiltonian system plan: $x' = H_y$, $y' = -H_x$; then $H$ is preserved (one-line proof by chain rule).
- Mechanics: $H(q,p) = p^2/2m + V(q)$ gives $q' = p/m$, $p' = -V'(q)$, i.e. Newton; $V$ bowls → centers, necks → separatrices.
- gradient flow: $X' = -\nabla V$, with $dV/dt = -\|\nabla V\|^2 \le 0$ — descent perpendicular to levels, convergence towards minima, never periodic orbit.
- Contrast: Hamiltonian *follows* the levels (conservation), gradient *goes down* them (dissipation).
- Chemistry: molecular vibration = Hamiltonian; geometry optimization and path IRC = gradient flow on the same surface $V$.

</KeyResults>
